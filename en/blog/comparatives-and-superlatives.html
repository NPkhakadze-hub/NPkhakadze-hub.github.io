Comparatives and superlatives in English — bigger, the best 📏

6 November 2026 · Nodar

“My brother is taller than me”, “This is the best film”, “It's colder today than yesterday” — comparing things is part of everyday conversation. English has two forms for this: the comparative (bigger, more interesting) and the superlative (the biggest, the most interesting).

The rule is simple, but learners still say more better or the most big. In this article I put everything in one place: when to use -er, when to use more, what happens with the exceptions, and which mistakes come up most often.

Short adjectives: -er and -est

A one-syllable adjective takes -er in the comparative and the … -est in the superlative:

✅ My brother is taller than me.
✅ This is the cheapest hotel in the city.
✅ It's colder today than yesterday.

Three small spelling details:

  • Ending in -e → just add -r/-st: nice → nicer → the nicest.
  • One syllable with one vowel + one consonant → the last consonant doubles: big → bigger, hot → the hottest (w, x and y don't double: new → newer).
  • Consonant + -y → -ier/-iest: dry → drier, easy → easier, happy → the happiest (this applies to two-syllable adjectives too).

Long adjectives: more and the most

Adjectives with three or more syllables, and most two-syllable ones (except those ending in -y), don't take an ending — instead, more or the most goes in front of them:

✅ This book is more interesting than the film.
✅ It was the most difficult exam of my life.
✅ Online lessons are more convenient for me.

Some two-syllable adjectives have both forms: quieter / more quiet, simpler / more simple. Both are correct, although you'll hear the -er form more often.

Exceptions you need to know by heart

A few of the most common adjectives change in their own way:

good → better → the best
bad → worse → the worst
far → farther / further → the farthest / the furthest
much / many → more → the most
little → less → the least

This is exactly where the most common mistake appears. Better is already a comparative, so adding more is one word too many:

❌ My English is more better now.
✅ My English is better now.
✅ My English is much better now. — with emphasis.

If you want to add emphasis, put much, a lot or far in front of the comparative (never a second more): much cheaper, a lot easier, far more expensive — in the last one, more expensive is the comparative itself.

than — the word that links the comparison

Languages mark comparison in different ways: some use the same word as for that, some say “from”, some use a case ending. In English it is always one word — than. A common mistake is using that or from instead:

❌ She is older that me.
❌ She is older from me.
✅ She is older than me.

In conversational English, than is usually followed by me, him, her. Than I am, than he is are more formal:

✅ He's taller than me. — conversational, the most natural.
✅ He's taller than I am. — slightly formal.

The superlative: the and in/of

The superlative is almost always preceded by the, because we're talking about one specific thing — the only one of its kind. To show the place or the group, we use in or of:

❌ It's most beautiful city in Georgia.
✅ It's the most beautiful city in Georgia.
✅ She's the youngest of the three sisters.

With a place or a group seen as one unit (a city, a country, a family, a class, a team) — in (in the world, in my family, in the class); with a number or a plural noun — of (of all, of the three, of my friends).

✅ This is the best day ever! — (ever often goes with the superlative.)

as … as — when two things are equal

To show that two things are equal, or to soften a difference, we use as + adjective + as. The adjective stays in its basic form, without -er:

✅ Batumi is as beautiful as Tbilisi. — equally beautiful.
✅ This phone isn't as expensive as I thought.
❌ He is as taller as his father.
✅ He is as tall as his father.

Not as … as often sounds more polite than a direct comparison: The second hotel wasn't as clean is softer than The second hotel was dirtier.

Test yourself

1. My new flat is ___ (big) than the old one.
2. This is ___ (good) pizza I've ever eaten.
3. Chinese has thousands of characters, so it is ___ (difficult) to learn than Italian.
4. My sister is two years older ___ me.
5. Today isn't as ___ (hot) as yesterday.
6. Everest is ___ (high) mountain in the world.

Answers: 1. bigger. 2. the best. 3. more difficult. 4. than. 5. hot. 6. the highest.

A practical tip: every day, make three comparisons about your own life — “My coffee today is stronger than yesterday”, “This week is busier than last week”. That way these forms will start coming to you on their own when you speak. Frequently asked questions

When do we use -er and when more?

One-syllable adjectives take -er (tall → taller); adjectives with three or more syllables and most two-syllable ones use more (more interesting). Two-syllable adjectives ending in -y take -ier: easier, happier.

Is more better correct?

No. Better is already a comparative, so more is one word too many. For emphasis, say much better or a lot better.

After than — me or I?

In conversational English, than me is natural (He's taller than me). Than I am is more formal.

What does as … as mean?

It shows that two things are equal: Batumi is as beautiful as Tbilisi — both are equally beautiful. The adjective stays in its basic form here, without -er.
