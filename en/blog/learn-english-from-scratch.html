English for beginners — where to start: an A1 plan 🌱

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“I studied English at school, but I don't remember a thing” — or “I've never studied it at all”. Either way, you have the same question: where do I start so I don't waste time? Learning English from scratch as an adult is completely normal, and it isn't as hard as many people think — as long as you go in the right order.

In this article I'll explain what to learn first, what to leave for later, and what a beginner's first month looks like — not by calendar, but step by step.

First, what not to do

Don't memorise tense tables. All the tenses at once, in three columns — that's the school method, and it's exactly why almost nothing stuck. A beginner doesn't need a tense table; a beginner needs about twenty phrases they can use to say something tomorrow.

Don't translate every word. Looking up each word in a dictionary stops your reading and makes listening impossible. Learn whole phrases: How are you? is one unit, not three words.

Don't wait until you're “ready” to speak. That day never comes by itself — speaking starts in your very first lesson, with just a few words.

First phrases: I am, I have, there is

A1 English stands on three structures: be (I am), have (I have) and there is (something exists, something is there). With these three you can already talk about yourself, your family and your surroundings.

I am Nino.
I am from Georgia.
I am tired.
I have a brother.
She has a car.
There is a café near my house.
There are two children in the room.

Watch out with age: in English it takes be, not have — I am 25. Many learners whose language uses “have” for age say “I have 25 years”. I have a separate article on this: “I have 25 years” — how to say your age.

I can, I like, I want — and question words

The next three verbs are about wishes and abilities: can, like, want. Add six question words — what, where, when, who, how, why — and you can not only answer questions but ask them too.

I can swim.
Can you help me?
I like coffee.
I don't like it.
I want to learn English.
Where are you from?
What is this?
How much is it?

Add to this the numbers from 1 to 100, the days of the week and telling the time — What time is it? It's half past two. Numbers are the most boring part, but in a shop, a taxi or an airport they're the first thing you need.

Typical beginner mistakes

Almost every beginner makes mistakes like these, and that's normal. It just helps to know from the start where people tend to trip up.

❌ I am student.
✅ I am a student. — (we need an article before a job)
❌ She have a car.
✅ She has a car.
❌ He like coffee.
✅ He likes coffee.
❌ I no understand.
✅ I don't understand.
❌ Is cold today.
✅ It is cold today. — (some languages can drop the subject, but an English statement almost always needs one)
❌ I can to swim.
✅ I can swim. — (no to after can)

The first month — steps, not a calendar

Everyone has a different pace, so I won't give dates. The order, though, is usually this:

1. Introductions and yourself — I am…, I'm from…, I live in…, I work as… — four or five sentences about yourself on day one.

2. Family and things — have / has, there is / are, a / an, plurals.

3. Daily routine — I get up, I go to work, I like, I don't like; days of the week, the time.

4. Questions and short dialogues — shop, café, asking the way: Can I…? How much…? Where is…?

Each step builds on the one before: until I am comes out automatically, it's too early to move on to I was. What level you're at and what comes after A1 — I've written about that separately: English levels.

Speaking from day one

The most common beginner plan is: “First I'll learn, then I'll speak.” In reality it's the other way round — speaking isn't the result of learning, it is learning itself. I like tea, said out loud in three words, gives you more than ten rules you've read, because your brain links the phrase to your own voice.

And if it's fear — that's a separate topic, not a question of talent; I've written about fear of speaking in detail. For a beginner the main rule is simple: make your mistakes in the lesson so you don't make them out on the street.

What to expect from a teacher

A good teacher doesn't lecture a beginner. Here's what to expect: for most of the lesson you're the one speaking, not the teacher; explanations you can understand, examples in English; a few new phrases every lesson that you use that same day; and corrections made in a way that doesn't make you feel stupid. If at the end of a lesson you can't say what new thing you can do today that you couldn't do yesterday, something isn't right.

Test yourself

Fill in am / is / are, have / has or a / an:

1. I ___ from Georgia.
2. My sister ___ two children.
3. This is ___ orange.
4. It ___ cold today.
5. We ___ a small dog.

Answers: 1. am. 2. has. 3. an. 4. is. 5. have.

One last tip: pick five phrases from this article and say them out loud about yourself today — I am…, I have…, I like…, I want…, I can… That's exactly how one-to-one online English lessons with me start, too: first a free 20-minute intro call on Google Meet, where we see where you're starting from, and then, from the very first lesson, you're the one speaking.

Frequently asked questions

Where do I start learning English from scratch?

With three structures — be (I am), have (I have) and there is — and about twenty everyday phrases about yourself, your family and your surroundings. You don't need tense tables or a translation of every word at the start.

I studied English at school but remember nothing — do I have to start from zero again?

Usually not from zero: the basics (I am, I have, I like) are still in there somewhere, just “asleep”, and they wake up faster than new material is learned. The order is the same — yourself, family, routine, short dialogues — you'll just get through part of it quickly.

Should a beginner speak in the very first lesson?

Yes — with a few simple sentences (I am…, I'm from…, I like…). Speaking isn't the result of learning, it's part of learning itself, and putting it off until you're “ready” only slows the process down.

How do I use “I am” and “I have”, and how do I give my age?

“I am” is be: I am, you are, he/she/it is, we/they are. “I have” is have: I have, she has. Age also takes be: I am 25, not “I have 25 years”.
