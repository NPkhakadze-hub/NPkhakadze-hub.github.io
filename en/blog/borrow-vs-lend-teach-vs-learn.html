Borrow or lend, teach or learn — one idea, two English words 🔄

26 October 2026 · Nodar

"Can you borrow me your pen?" — I have heard this sentence from learners so many times that I no longer even notice it as a mistake until I stop and think. And the reason is simple: in many languages “borrowing” and “lending” are one and the same word — I borrow from you, you lend to me, and both are just “borrowing”. In English, though, these are two different verbs, and the direction decides which one you need.

English has several pairs like this, and many learners mix them up for the same reason: their own language says with one word, or one root, what English says with two. Below we’ll look at seven such pairs — why they get mixed up and how to tell them apart.

Borrow or lend — who takes and who gives

Borrow = to take something for a while (you receive it). Lend = to give something for a while (you hand it over). If your language uses one verb for both and only a small ending or prefix changes, the two ideas easily stay as one word in your head.

A quick check: borrow is followed by from (who you take it from), lend by to or directly by the person (who you give it to). And one more thing: lend is irregular (lent), borrow is regular (borrowed).

❌ Can you borrow me your pen?
✅ Can you lend me your pen? / Can I borrow your pen? — either “you give it to me” (lend) or “I take it from you” (borrow).
❌ I lent some money from my brother.
✅ I borrowed some money from my brother.

Teach or learn — who teaches and who studies

Teach = to give knowledge (the teacher does it). Learn = to get knowledge (the student does it). In many languages both come from the same root, or one verb covers both.

The mistake usually goes in one direction: learn appears where teach should be. Learn is not followed directly by a person (whom?) — learn me does not exist. Teach is: teach me, teach the students.

❌ My father learned me to drive.
✅ My father taught me to drive.
❌ He learned us English at school.
✅ He taught us English at school.
✅ I want to learn English better. — (teach English better = be a better English teacher for others.)

Bring or take — towards here, away to there

Many languages already separate these two ideas, so this pair is easier in principle. Bring = carry something towards the speaker or the listener, or towards the place we’re talking about. Take = carry something somewhere else. The mistake still happens, because both are about carrying something, and the quiet assumption “it’s probably one word in English” keeps working in the background.

A rule of thumb: bring — “here”, take — “there”. If the thing is moving towards you or towards the person you’re talking to, bring; if it’s going somewhere else, take.

❌ Could you take me a glass of water?
✅ Could you bring me a glass of water? — (here, to me.)
✅ Take an umbrella when you go out — it's going to rain. — (you are going somewhere else.)

Come or go — arriving and leaving

This pair follows exactly the same logic as bring/take, only applied to yourself: come = move towards the speaker or the listener, go = move somewhere else.

The trap: when a friend invites you to their place — or to wherever they will be — and you answer, in English you say come: you’re moving towards them, so from their point of view you are coming. Many languages are more flexible here, and go slips out on its own.

❌ "Are you coming to my party?" — "Yes, I'll go!"
✅ "Are you coming to my party?" — "Yes, I'll come!" — towards the speaker’s place = come.
✅ I'm going to Batumi next week. — (neither I nor the listener is there.)
✅ "Are you going to Nino's party?" — "Yes, I'll go." — neither speaker is there — go.

Say or tell — in brief

In many languages one verb covers both, but in English tell is followed by a person (tell me, tell her), and say is not (say something, say to me). I have a separate article on this pair — together with speak and talk — so here is just one example.

❌ He said me the truth.
✅ He told me the truth.
✅ He said nothing to me. — say + to + person.

Lose or miss — losing and missing

Even if your language has separate words for these, the broad meaning of lose (“to lose” anything at all) is tempting: not catching the bus is miss in English, while lost the bus would mean the bus physically went missing. Lose = no longer have something (a thing, a game, weight, your way). Miss = fail to catch or attend, let something pass, long for someone (a bus, a lesson, a chance, a person).

❌ I lost the bus this morning.
✅ I missed the bus this morning.
✅ I lost my keys again.
✅ I miss my family. — (= I feel sad that they are not with me.)

Watch, see or look — one idea, three verbs

In many languages one or two verbs cover all three, but English has three:

See — notice with your eyes, without trying: I saw him in the street. Look (at) — direct your eyes on purpose, usually at something still: look at the photo. Watch — look on purpose for a while, at something that moves: watch a film, watch TV, watch the kids.

With films you hear both — watch a film (the process) and see a film (the fact: you have seen it); look a film — never.

❌ Look this photo — it's from our trip!
✅ Look at this photo — it's from our trip! — look needs at.
❌ I looked TV all evening.
✅ I watched TV all evening.
⚠️ Have you seen that film? — correct: it asks about the fact, not the process.

Why these pairs get mixed up — and one trick for all of them

These pairs fall into three groups. borrow/lend, teach/learn, say/tell — many languages cover each pair with one word or one root, perhaps with a small change in form, while English uses a separate word; when we think in our own language, the root is one, so we look for one English word too. bring/take, come/go — many languages already have two words, but the “here/there” logic has to be rethought in English, from the speaker’s point of view. lose/miss, watch/see/look — the meanings of the words in your language don’t line up exactly with the English ones.

The trick is simple: ask each pair one question. borrow/lend, teach/learn — “Who receives?” (I receive = borrow, learn; I give = lend, teach). bring/take, come/go — “Which direction?” (towards the speaker or the listener = bring, come; somewhere else = take, go). lose/miss — “Did I lose it, or did I not make it in time?” watch/see/look — “Is it moving, and am I doing it on purpose?”

There is one more group of words where your first language can lead you astray — “false friends”: words like magazine, fabric, actual and others that look like words in another language but mean something different.

Check yourself

Choose the correct verb. Each item has one answer.

1. Could you ______ me your charger? Mine is at home. (a) borrow (b) lend
2. My grandmother ______ me how to make khachapuri. (a) learned (b) taught
3. I'm on my way to your place — should I ______ you anything from the shop? (a) bring (b) take
4. I overslept and ______ the first lesson. (a) lost (b) missed
5. We ______ a great film last night. (a) looked (b) looked at (c) watched

Answers: 1. b — you are giving it to me, so lend; with borrow it would be Could I borrow your charger? 2. b — learn me does not exist. 3. a — I’m coming to your home and carrying something to you, so bring you. 4. b — I didn’t make it to the lesson; I didn’t lose it. 5. c — a film moves and you look at it for a long time, so watch; look at is for photos and still objects, and looked a film doesn’t exist at all.

One practical tip: this week, focus on just one pair — borrow/lend, for example — and every time the idea of “borrowing” crosses your mind, ask yourself: “Who receives?” One week, one pair, and the mistake gradually fades. Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between borrow and lend?

borrow = take something for a while (you receive it, from someone), lend = give something for a while (you hand it over, to someone). Can I borrow your pen? / Can you lend me your pen? — both are correct; Can you borrow me your pen? — no.

Can I say “My father learned me to drive”?

No. learn is not followed directly by a person — giving knowledge is teach: My father taught me to drive. learn is what the student does: I learned to drive.

Bring or take — how do I tell them apart?

By direction: bring = carry something towards the speaker or the listener, or to the place we are talking about (“here”), take = carry it somewhere else (“there”). If it comes towards you or the person you’re talking to — bring; if it goes somewhere else — take. come/go follow the same logic.

Why is “I lost the bus” a mistake?

lose means no longer having a thing, losing a game or your way; not catching a bus is miss: I missed the bus. miss also means not attending a lesson and longing for a person.
