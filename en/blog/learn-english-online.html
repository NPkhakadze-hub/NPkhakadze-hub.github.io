Learning English online — app, video or live lesson? 💻

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“I want to learn English, but where do I start — download Duolingo, watch a course on YouTube or find a teacher?” — I hear this question almost every week.

Today there are so many ways to learn English online that the choice itself has become the problem: everything is available, but it's not clear what each thing is for. In this article I'll compare all the options honestly — apps, video courses, free resources, a language partner and live lessons. None of them is “bad”. Each one simply does a different job, and one can't replace another.

Apps: habit and vocabulary, but not speaking

Apps like Duolingo do one thing really well — they build a daily habit. Five minutes on the bus, five minutes before bed, a reminder on your phone. Anyone who has spent years starting “tomorrow” is already ahead with this one habit.

The second thing an app gives you is basic vocabulary: words that come up again and again and gradually stick. At A1–A2 level, that's genuinely useful.

Where does it stop? An app, at least the free version, doesn't correct you. It checks whether you tapped the right button, but it can't tell you that a sentence you built yourself sounds odd. Speaking practice is limited too: repeating one sentence into a microphone isn't a conversation, and a dialogue with an AI imitates a partner rather than giving you live correction.

That's why I often meet people with a two-year streak in an app who can't put two sentences together in a real conversation.

👍 An app is good for: 10–15 minutes a day, basic words, keeping up a learning rhythm.
👎 An app can't give you: speaking practice, correction of your mistakes, a plan for where you're going.

Video courses and YouTube: great explanations, no feedback

YouTube and video platforms are full of excellent grammar explanations. If you didn't get the Present Perfect the first time, you listen to a second teacher, then a third — and one of them is bound to “click”. That's the big advantage of video: the explanation is free, you can pause it, and you can watch it as many times as you like.

The problem lies elsewhere. A video doesn't know who you are. It tells everyone the same thing and can't see where you went wrong. Understanding a grammar rule and using that rule in conversation are two different skills — and a video can't teach you the second one, because it has never heard your sentence.

There's a second, more hidden trap too: watching videos gives you a feeling of learning. I watched for an hour — “I learned something”. In reality, if you haven't written or said a single sentence after that hour, your knowledge is still passive.

Free resources that are really worth it

Free doesn't mean weak. Two sources I recommend to my students myself:

  • BBC Learning English. Short video lessons on pronunciation, vocabulary and grammar; vocabulary programmes built on the news (Learning English from the News, News Review); 6 Minute English — an ideal format for listening. It's British English, and the quality is consistent.
  • British Council LearnEnglish. Exercises by level (A1 to C1), listening, reading, a grammar section with answers. You can also check your level there yourself.

Both are free, and both are made by experienced course designers. What can't they give you? The same as video: nobody will see your mistake, and you won't have a speaking partner. But as daily “food” for listening and reading, they're the best option there is.

A language partner: free conversation, with conditions

On language exchange platforms (Tandem, HelloTalk and the like), you're matched with English speakers who are learning your language: you help them with theirs, and they talk to you in English in return. It's one of the best cures for the fear of speaking: the other person is learning too, you both make mistakes, and nobody judges you.

Two conditions. First — a partner isn't a teacher: they either won't notice your mistake or won't be able to explain why it's a mistake. Second — this works from B1 upwards. At A1–A2 level, the conversation often falls apart after three sentences, and both sides switch to translating.

Learning English online with a teacher: what a live lesson gives you

Learning English online with a teacher adds one thing that none of the options above can give you — feedback. You say a sentence, the teacher corrects you on the spot and explains why. It's in this loop — say it, fix it, say it again — that speaking grows.

The second thing is speaking time. In a one-to-one lesson, most of the 75 minutes is you speaking, not listening. The third is a plan: someone sees where you are, where you need to get to and what you've missed along the way. Apps and videos give you material; a teacher gives you direction.

What can't a live lesson give you? The everyday part. Two sessions a week are great, but the other five days are still up to you — and that's exactly where the app, the BBC and listening come back in.

A realistic mix for each level

  • A1–A2 (beginner). 10–15 minutes a day in an app for vocabulary + two live lessons a week where you build sentences with those words. Video explanations — on a specific grammar topic, when something from the lesson is still unclear. It's too early for a language partner.
  • B1 (intermediate). The app now gives you less — listening takes its place: the BBC's 6 Minute English, a series with subtitles. Two lessons a week on speaking and mistakes + one conversation a week with a language partner.
  • B2 (upper intermediate). Your main “food” — English-language content from your own area of interest: podcasts, articles, videos without subtitles. Here the teacher acts more like an editor: nuances, natural phrases, corrections of the “that's correct, but people don't say it like that” kind. A language partner — the more often, the better.

Notice the pattern: the higher you go, the less an app gives you and the more live conversation does. A beginner needs structure; a B2 learner needs practice and precise correction.

The most common trap

It isn't a bad app or a bad course. The trap is collecting resources instead of practising. Five apps on your phone, twenty saved videos, three PDF textbooks — and not one of them finished. Every new resource gives you the feeling of a fresh start, but things almost never get as far as the middle.

The other face of the same trap is jumping around. Two weeks of Duolingo, then “this doesn't work”, two weeks of a YouTube course, then a new app. Every tool delivers results after several months of regular use — and none of them does in two weeks.

A simple rule I suggest to my students: one resource for listening, one for vocabulary, one place for speaking and correction. Three main channels are enough; everything else is just an extra, not yet another “system”.

Before you choose a format, it's worth finding out your level — the English level test will help with that.

How to build a weekly routine

Here's what one week could look like for a B1 learner with 30–40 minutes a day:

📅 Mon / Thu — live lesson: speaking, correction, a new topic.
Tue / Fri — 20 minutes: one BBC episode, new phrases in your notebook; 10 minutes — vocabulary review (app or flashcards).
Wed — homework from the lesson + three sentences using the new grammar (send them to your teacher).
Sat — a 30-minute conversation with a language partner, or a voice message to yourself in English.
Sun — rest, or one episode of a series with subtitles.

The key here isn't the schedule itself but the fact that every free resource connects to the live lesson: use a phrase you heard on the BBC in your lesson, build a sentence with a word you learned in the app, tell your teacher what you struggled with when talking to your partner. That's how all the tools turn into one system.

✍️ Check yourself: 1. How many apps or courses have you started at the moment, and how many have you finished? 2. In the last week, how many minutes did you speak English out loud (listening doesn't count)? 3. Who corrects your mistakes? 4. Do you know what level you're at and what the next step is? If your answer to the second question is “almost zero” and to the third “nobody”, it isn't resources you're missing — it's feedback.

If you're starting from zero, read the article for beginners first — the first steps are set out there. For daily speaking practice, the 30-day plan will come in handy.

Frequently asked questions

Can you learn English with an app alone?

An app is good for a daily habit and basic vocabulary, but, at least in the free version, it doesn't correct your mistakes or give you real speaking practice. Getting to the point of speaking with an app alone usually doesn't work: it's an extra, not the main path.

Which free resources are really worth it?

BBC Learning English (video lessons, 6 Minute English, Learning English from the News and News Review for vocabulary from the news) and British Council LearnEnglish (exercises from A1 to C1, listening, reading). Both are free and made by experienced course designers.

What does a live lesson give you that an app or video can't?

Feedback: the teacher corrects your sentence on the spot and explains why. On top of that come speaking time and a plan, because someone sees where you are and what you've missed. Apps and videos give you material; a teacher gives you direction.

How do I combine free resources and lessons?

One resource for listening, one for vocabulary and one place for speaking and correction: three main channels are enough, everything else is just an extra. The key is to use what you learn from free resources in your lessons, so the tools turn into one system.
