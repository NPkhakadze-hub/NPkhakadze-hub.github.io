English for moving abroad — Germany and the USA: what level you need ✈️

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“I need English to move abroad — but what level, and where do I start?” I hear this question almost every week. And usually it's about one of two destinations: Germany or the United States.

These two paths are not alike. Before you open a textbook, it's worth working out where you'll need English and what for — that's exactly what decides what you learn first.

Germany and the USA — English plays a different role

English for working in Germany. The official language is German: government offices, neighbours, kindergarten. English is what you need in international companies, IT, at university and in everyday life in big cities — in Berlin, English will get you much further than in a small town. Some programmes or employers also require a certain level of English — always check this in the conditions of the specific job or programme.

English for moving to the USA. In the US, English is everything: your job, your landlord, school, the doctor, getting a driver's licence at the DMV. “I'll learn it later” won't work here — you need it from day one.

So: for Germany, English is more of a professional tool; for the US, it's the language of everyday life. Both have one thing in common: “average” school English isn't enough.

What level of English do I need — realistically

I explain the levels in detail in the article English levels A1–C2; here, only from the angle of moving abroad:

B1 — survival level: renting a flat, shopping, booking a doctor's appointment, simple work instructions. At this level you can usually already work in jobs where language isn't the main tool.
B2 — office work, university, interviews, emails and calls with clients. In most cases, this is the “comfortable” level.
C1 — professional language: presentations, negotiations, legal or medical vocabulary, management positions.

An important nuance: your level on paper and your level in a real conversation often don't match. With a B2 certificate you can still get lost on the phone — and the other way round.

Which skill comes first

For moving abroad, the order of skills is different from school:

1. Understanding real speech — fast, accented, hard to follow. Films with subtitles, podcasts, ordinary people on YouTube — not just textbook audio.
2. Phone calls — no facial expressions, no gestures. The hardest skill, and the one we practise least.
3. Email — short, polite, well structured.
4. Small talk — at work and with neighbours. Less in Germany, every day in the US.
5. Forms and documents — surname, date of birth, marital status, signature.

Three situations, three phrase sets

Housing. American English says apartment, British English (and often people in Germany too) says flat — both are correct.

✅ Is the apartment still available?
✅ Are utilities included in the rent?
✅ How much is the deposit?
✅ When can I move in?

Work — calls and emails.

✅ Hi, this is Nino from accounting. I'm calling about the invoice.
✅ Sorry, I didn't catch your name.
✅ Could you put that in an email, please?
✅ I'll get back to you by Friday.

Official offices. In British English you fill in a form, in American English more often fill out — both are understood everywhere.

✅ I have an appointment at ten with Ms Weber.
✅ Which form do I need to fill out?
✅ Do I need the original or a copy?
✅ Could I get that in writing?

Phrases for asking for clarification at the doctor's and at official offices are in a separate article — English for emigrants: at the doctor's, at offices, at work.

Common mistakes I see among people moving abroad

❌ I'm interesting in this job.
✅ I'm interested in this job. — interesting = something that interests you, interested = feeling interest

❌ Can you explain me the form?
✅ Can you explain the form to me? — explain is not followed directly by me

❌ I live in Berlin since two years.
✅ I've lived in Berlin for two years. — a typical mistake for speakers of languages that use one word for both “since” and “for”, like German seit

❌ My actual job is in Munich.
✅ My current job is in Munich. — actual = real, not “present”; in several European languages a similar-looking word means “current”

Five steps to prepare — an order, not a calendar

1. Find out your real level — not with a certificate, but through a live conversation.
2. Make a list of your situations — housing, work, bank, school, doctor. This is your “textbook”.
3. Give each situation its own short phrase list, which you say out loud, not just read.
4. Listen every day — real voices, real speed.
5. Role-play — a call with your landlord, a visit to an office, an interview in English. With a teacher or a friend, but out loud.

The fear I hear most often: “I have an accent and I'll be embarrassed.” When you live abroad, everyone has an accent — what matters is speaking clearly, not “perfectly”. The second fear: “I won't understand on the phone.” That's exactly why we practise calls in lessons, before you need to make a real one.

Check yourself

Choose the correct option or correct the sentence.

1. I've lived in Hamburg ___ 2024. (since / for)
2. I'm very ___ in this position. (interesting / interested)
3. Correct the sentence: Can you explain me how this works?
4. On the phone, you didn't catch the other person's name. What do you say? (a) Repeat your name. (b) Sorry, I didn't catch your name.
5. Correct the sentence: My actual address is in Munich — I moved last month.

Answers: 1. since — a specific year, a starting point. 2. interested. 3. Can you explain to me how this works? (or …explain how this works to me? / simply Can you explain how this works?). 4. (b) — (a) is grammatically correct, but it sounds like an order. 5. My current address is in Munich. (also correct: my new address / my present address).

The most practical advice: English for moving abroad doesn't start on page one of a textbook — it starts with the situations of your first week. Frequently asked questions

What level of English do I need to move abroad?

B1 for getting by and simple jobs, B2 for office work, university and interviews, C1 for professional language. Your level on paper and in real conversation often don't match — test it in a live conversation.

Is English enough in Germany, or do I need German too?

The official language is German — you need it for government offices and everyday life. English is useful in international companies, in IT, at university and in everyday life in big cities. You can work on both languages in parallel.

Which skill should I learn first for moving abroad?

Understanding real speech, phone calls, short polite emails, small talk and filling in forms — an order quite different from school.

How should I start preparing?

In five steps: find out your real level, list your situations, learn the phrases for each situation out loud, listen every day and role-play the situations — a call, an office visit, an interview.
