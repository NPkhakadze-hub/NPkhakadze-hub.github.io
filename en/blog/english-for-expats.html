English for life abroad — at the doctor's, at an office, at work ✈️

26 October 2026 · Nodar

English abroad is a different thing from English at school. Here nobody corrects your grammar — but at the doctor's, at the bank or at work, one sentence you didn't understand can become a real problem.

This text is for people who already live in Germany, another European country or the US — or are planning to move there.

Why “intermediate” English isn't enough

Tourist English and the English of someone who has moved abroad are different skills. A tourist gets by with a few hundred words and a friendly smile. Someone living abroad needs the language of bureaucracy: appointment, deadline, application, insurance, landlord, contract, tax return.

This is exactly the vocabulary textbooks hardly cover. And yet it's exactly what you meet in everyday life.

Three situations where we get lost most often

1. At the doctor's. Describing symptoms in English is an art of its own.

✅ I've had a headache for three days.
✅ It hurts when I move my arm.
✅ I'm allergic to penicillin.
✅ Could you write it down, please?

That last phrase is worth its weight in gold: nobody will think less of you for asking them to write something down.

2. At a public office. What matters most here is checking politely.

✅ Sorry, could you say that again, please?
✅ Could you speak a bit more slowly?
✅ So, if I understand correctly, I need to…
✅ What documents do I need to bring?

If I understand correctly is the most useful phrase for life abroad: it protects you from misunderstandings and sounds professional at the same time.

3. At work. Here the problem is often not grammar but tone. A word-for-word translation from your own language can sound rude in English.

😐 Send me the file. — sounds like an order.
😊 Could you send me the file when you have a moment? — the same request in a normal tone.

😐 I don't agree.
😊 I see your point, but I think… — disagreeing politely.

If you live in Germany: two languages at once

I often hear: “I have to learn German first, English can wait.” In practice this rarely works out — because a large part of the work (especially in IT, research and international companies) is done in English.

You can work on both languages in parallel, as long as they don't get mixed up. What helps here is that in lessons the explanations are in a language you understand — German or English itself, depending on your level — and English is the practice material.

The time difference isn't an obstacle

This is a technical question that holds many people back. In practice: the difference between Tbilisi and Germany is 2–3 hours, and between Tbilisi and the US East Coast 8–9 hours (depending on the season). The schedule is arranged individually — at the intro call we work out together which time suits us both.

I have students in different countries. This stopped being a problem long ago.

Test yourself

How would you say this in English? Answers below.

1. Tell the doctor your head has been hurting for three days (and still hurts).
2. Politely ask someone to repeat what they just said.
3. Start a sentence that checks you've understood correctly…
4. Ask what papers you have to bring with you.

Possible answers: 1. I've had a headache for three days. — three days, and it still hurts → Present Perfect. 2. Sorry, could you say that again, please? 3. So, if I understand correctly… 4. What documents do I need to bring?

Abroad, your English doesn't have to be perfect — it has to be enough. One clarifying question at the doctor's or in an office means more than flawless grammar. That's exactly why we start with the situations that are part of your everyday life — not with page one of a textbook.

Frequently asked questions

Which phrase helps if I didn't understand?

“Sorry, could you say that again, please?” or “Could you speak a bit more slowly?” To check: “So, if I understand correctly…” — that last phrase is the most useful one for life abroad.

Should I learn German first or English?

In practice you can work on both in parallel. A large part of the work — especially in IT, research and international companies — is done in English.

Won't the time difference get in the way of online lessons?

Usually not. Tbilisi is 2–3 hours ahead of Germany and 8–9 hours ahead of the US East Coast (9 in winter). The schedule is arranged individually, and at the intro call we work out together which time suits us.
