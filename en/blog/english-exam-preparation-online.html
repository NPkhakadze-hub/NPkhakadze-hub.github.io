Online English preparation — exams, interviews, university 📝

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“I need to get my English ready” — there’s a different story behind this phrase every time. One person has a school-leaving exam ahead, another needs IELTS for a university abroad, a third has a job interview coming up, and a fourth simply wants to finally “get moving”. If you’re one of them, you’re probably also wondering how preparation differs from ordinary lessons.

In short: lessons teach you the language, preparation gets you ready for a specific day. Below I explain how online English preparation is planned for different goals — and what to ask a teacher before you start.

One phrase — four different goals

School-leaving exams and the school curriculum. What sets preparation for school-leavers and school students apart is that the format is known in advance: for example reading, writing, vocabulary and grammar, listening. That’s why English exam preparation always covers two things: knowing the language and recognising the task types, including how to manage your time.

IELTS / TOEFL. Some universities and immigration programmes ask for an international certificate — which one and with what score, you should always check with the specific institution. Preparing for IELTS online is entirely possible, because all four parts (Listening, Reading, Writing, Speaking) can be practised well at a screen, and a Speaking rehearsal on Google Meet is almost the same as face to face.

Job interviews. Here, besides your English, the structure of your answers and staying calm also decide things — there’s a separate article on this: job interviews in English.

Raising your general level. “I just want to get better” — this is the vaguest goal, and that’s exactly why it needs the most structure: first we find out which level you’re at, then we choose what your next step should be.

How preparation differs from “just lessons”

In five ways — and if any of them is missing, it isn’t preparation, it’s just lessons:

1. A goal — a specific day or result we are preparing for.
2. A level diagnosis — where you are now in all four skills, each one separately.
3. A plan built around skills — not “we’ll go through the grammar”, but what, in what order and why.
4. Practice in the real format — on the same kinds of tasks and with the same time limits as in the exam or interview.
5. Feedback on homework — mistakes are explained, not just corrected.

Diagnosis is the step that gets skipped most often. A student may be at B1 in reading but at A2 in speaking — and if we don’t see this at the start, the plan gets built around the wrong skill. That’s exactly what the free 20-minute intro call on Google Meet is for: not to sell you anything, but to make a first assessment of your level — the detailed diagnosis across all four skills continues in the first lessons.

What a realistic plan looks like

I deliberately don’t give timeframes here — they depend on where you start and how much you work between lessons (how long it takes to learn English). The only thing I can say for certain is the order:

① Diagnosis → which skill is weak and which is ready.
② Foundations → the grammar and vocabulary without which the tasks can’t be done.
③ Format → we get to know the task types, strategies and time management.
④ Practice + feedback → every task is followed by analysis: what worked, what didn’t and why.
⑤ Full-format rehearsal → the whole test or the whole interview in one go, timed.

The stages overlap — while you work on the format, your foundations get stronger too. But the order matters: starting with ⑤, that is, doing practice tests non-stop, reinforces your mistakes, not your knowledge.

Typical mistakes during preparation

Memorising word lists. A word learned from a list often won’t come back to you in a sentence. Words are learned in context — from a text, from your own sentence, from an example said out loud.

Ignoring listening and speaking. Tests are “quiet” — you write in silence, at your own pace. But listening and speaking are exactly the parts where your nerves let you down first in the exam. That’s why they need a place in your preparation from the very first week.

Not a single full rehearsal. Going through a whole exam against the clock is a completely different experience from doing individual tasks. Without it, your first “real” attempt will be on exam day itself.

And in writing tasks — three mistakes that turn up most often in students’ work:

❌ I am agree with this opinion.
✅ I agree with this opinion.
❌ Nowadays many people is working from home.
✅ Nowadays many people are working from home.
❌ In the past, people have used letters to communicate.
✅ In the past, people used letters to communicate.

What to ask a teacher before you start

Any tutor who offers exam preparation should be able to answer these questions calmly:

❓ How will you determine my level before we start?
❓ Will the plan be built around skills or around the chapters of a book?
❓ How often will I practise in the real format?
❓ How will I get feedback on my homework?
❓ How will I know that I’m making progress?

The last question is the most important. If the answer is “you’ll feel it yourself over time”, that isn’t preparation. Progress should be measurable: which tasks you can already do, and which you can’t yet.

Check yourself

Choose or correct. Each item has one correct answer.

1. A university abroad asks for an international certificate. Which is the right path — (a) general conversation lessons · (b) preparation in the format of the specific exam?
2. Correct the mistake: I am agree with the author.
3. A Speaking task: “Describe a time when you helped someone.” Which tense carries the main story — Past Simple or Present Perfect?
4. Correct the mistake: Last year many students has passed the exam.
5. You’re preparing for a job interview and only doing grammar tests. What’s missing — (a) more tests · (b) rehearsing out loud in interview format?

Answers: 1. (b) — the format and time management have to be learned separately. 2. I agree with the author. 3. Past Simple — a specific, finished event (Last summer I helped…). 4. Last year many students passed the exam. 5. (b) — speaking can only be practised by speaking.

One practical tip: before you start, put your goal into one sentence — “I want to [do what] by [which day]”. Without that, any plan stays vague. Frequently asked questions

How does English preparation differ from ordinary lessons?

Preparation has a specific goal (an exam, an interview, university admission), starts with a level diagnosis in all four skills, follows a plan built around skills, practises in the real format, and every piece of homework gets feedback and an explanation.

Can I prepare for IELTS online?

Yes. All four parts — Listening, Reading, Writing, Speaking — can be practised well at a screen, and a Speaking rehearsal on Google Meet is almost the same as face to face. Check with the specific university or programme which certificate and which score you need.

In what order is preparation planned?

Diagnosis → foundations (essential grammar and vocabulary) → getting to know the format → practice with feedback → full timed rehearsal. How long it takes depends on your starting level and how much you work between lessons.

What are the most common mistakes during preparation?

Three: memorising word lists without context, putting off listening and speaking “until the end”, and not doing a single full rehearsal before exam day. All three are easy to fix if they are built into the plan from the start.
