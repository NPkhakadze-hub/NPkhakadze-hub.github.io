English for IT professionals — what a developer really needs 🧑‍💻

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“I read documentation with no trouble, I find the answer on Stack Overflow in seconds, but when it’s my turn at the morning standup, everything in my head gets jumbled.” I have heard this sentence, in one form or another, from almost every developer who has come to me to learn English. From testers and designers too.

English for IT professionals is not the English you find in textbooks. Technical text is usually not the problem — the problem is the live person on the other side: a colleague on Slack, a team lead at the retro, an interviewer from a company abroad. That is what this article is about: where a developer actually needs English, which phrases work, and how someone who spends eight hours a day writing code can learn all this.

Why you read documentation easily but go quiet at standup

The paradox is easy to explain. You have had documentation and code in front of you every day for years — reading is a passive skill, and it gets trained on its own. Speaking is an active skill: you have to build the sentence yourself, right now, while five people wait. Reading can’t teach you that, however much you read.

That is why I often see the same picture: reading at a confident B2, speaking at a shy A2. The learner thinks they “don’t know English”. In reality they do; one skill is simply far behind the other. Fixing this is nothing like starting from zero — it’s more about activating the knowledge you already have.

English for IT professionals: five situations where you really need it

When someone tells me, “I want English for developers,” I first ask which of these five bothers them most. The answer changes the lesson plan.

  • Documentation and Stack Overflow. Reading. Most people have no problem here; if they do, it’s a vocabulary issue and it gets fixed relatively quickly.
  • Written communication. Slack, Jira tickets, pull request comments, emails. The key here is tone and brevity: how to say “this code isn’t good” without offending a colleague, and how to write a bug report people understand without asking follow-up questions.
  • Meetings. Standup, planning, retro. A fairly small set of phrases keeps coming back here, and you can practise it until it becomes automatic.
  • A technical interview with a company abroad. Talking about your project, justifying a decision (“Why did you choose this database?”), asking your own questions at the end.
  • Presentations and demos. Sharing your screen, “here’s what we did this sprint”, answering questions live.

The first point is usually already sorted. The other four all demand the same thing — speaking and writing in real time.

Phrases that work at standup, on Slack and in interviews

Below are the phrases my IT students use most often. They are all natural — this is how people talk in teams, not in textbooks.

At standup:

"Yesterday I worked on the login bug. Today I'm moving on to the tests."
"I'm blocked on the API integration."
"No blockers on my side."

On Slack and in tickets:

"Could you walk me through this ticket?"
"What's the expected behavior here?" — (when describing a bug)
"I'll pick this up after the review."
"Can you give me some context on this?"

In meetings:

"Let's take this offline." — i.e. let’s talk about it separately, after the meeting
"I'd suggest we split this into two tickets."
"Does that make sense so far?" — (during a demo)

In interviews:

"I chose PostgreSQL because we needed complex queries and strict consistency."
"Good question — let me think about that for a second."
"What does a typical sprint look like on your team?"

Pay attention to the last phrase: at the end of an interview, answering “no” to “Do you have any questions?” is almost always a bad sign. Two questions prepared in advance make a candidate come across completely differently.

Typical mistakes in technical English

Even experienced developers make these mistakes — some are word-for-word translations from their own language, some are just habit.

❌ I have doubt about this function.
✅ I'm not sure about this function.

In many languages you literally “have a doubt”, but that doesn’t carry straight over into English. I have doubts about… (in the plural) is correct — it just sounds a bit more sceptical than a simple I'm not sure.

❌ Can you explain me the logic?
✅ Can you explain the logic to me?
❌ Let's discuss about the architecture.
✅ Let's discuss the architecture.

Discuss is not followed by about — it takes a direct object: discuss the architecture, discuss it with the team. If you want to say “about”, use talk about: Let's talk about the architecture.

❌ I found bug in login page.
✅ I found a bug on the login page.

Leaving out articles is one of the most common mistakes for learners whose language has no articles, and in a ticket it really stands out. A simple rule to start with: a new bug nobody knows about yet — a bug; the specific page you both know — the page.

❌ I'll send the pull request until Friday.
✅ I'll send the pull request by Friday.

Many languages use one word for both, but in English by is a deadline (“no later than”), while until is a duration (“up to that point, the whole time”). For a ticket deadline you almost always need by.

B1 is enough to start — and what B2 adds

People often ask me: “What level do I need to work for a company abroad?” My answer is this: B1 is enough to get started. At B1 you can say what you did yesterday, ask about what you didn’t understand, write a short, clear message and follow a standup to the end.

Many people start working remotely at exactly this level and keep growing on the job.

B2 adds something else: reasoning and tone. At B2 you can explain in an interview why you chose one approach over another; in a pull request comment you can tell a colleague, gently, that you don’t like their solution.

At B2 you can also handle an unexpected question during a demo and follow a fast conversation full of idioms to the end. These are often exactly the skills a senior position or a team-lead role requires.

I explain the levels in detail in a separate article. The main point here is this: don’t wait for B2 before sending out your CV. Start with B1 — you can work on B2 at the same time.

How a lesson for an IT professional is built

English for developers, in my experience, fails in one case: when it’s taught through the chapters of a general textbook — “Unit 7: At the Office” and so on. An IT professional needs material from their own work. So the lesson is built like this:

  • The material is your project and your tickets. In the lesson we open your open-source or personal project — or a similar practice repo — and write pull request comments together; we read a ticket and rewrite the bug report so that a colleague abroad understands it without asking. Work code and tickets — only if your company explicitly allows it.
  • Every lesson starts with a standup. Three minutes: what you did, what you’re doing, what’s blocking you. In English, unprepared. It feels awkward at first — that is exactly why we do it.
  • Interview rehearsal. We take a real job posting: I’m the interviewer, you’re the candidate. We listen to the recording together, fix things and run it again.
  • Demo. You share your screen and show me your feature, and I ask you the kind of questions a product manager would.
  • Grammar — only where it’s needed. Articles, by/until, correct question word order — all of it comes out of your own mistakes, not out of the order of textbook chapters.

The format is online — for a developer that’s a natural environment; screen sharing is an everyday thing anyway. If you want to see how this works in practice, a free 20-minute intro call on Google Meet is the quickest way.

Check yourself

Choose the correct option. Each item has one correct answer.

1. Can you explain ___ how the cache works? (me / to me)
2. Let's ___ the new architecture at the planning meeting. (discuss / discuss about)
3. I found ___ bug on ___ checkout page — nobody has seen it before. (a / the)
4. I'll have the pull request ready ___ Friday, not later. (until / by)
5. Which one sounds natural when you’re simply not sure? (a) I have doubt about this approach. (b) I'm not sure about this approach.

Answers: 1. to me. 2. discuss. 3. a bug on the checkout page. 4. by. 5. (b).

One practical tip to finish: tomorrow, before standup, put together two sentences in English in advance — what you did and what you’re doing. If your team doesn’t work in English, say them quietly to yourself. Those two minutes a day are the practice that reading can’t replace.

Frequently asked questions

What level does a developer need to work for a company abroad?

B1 is enough to start: talking at standup, asking questions, writing a short message. B2 adds reasoning and tone — explaining a decision in an interview, gentle criticism in a pull request comment, answering unexpected questions during a demo.

Why do I read documentation easily but go quiet at standup?

Reading is a passive skill and gets trained on its own through everyday work with code. Speaking is active — you have to build the sentence in real time. It is a different skill and needs separate practice, not learning from zero.

What mistakes do developers make in technical English?

Most often: “I have doubt” instead of “I'm not sure”, “explain me” instead of “explain to me”, “discuss about” instead of “discuss”, leaving out articles, and “until” instead of “by” for deadlines.

How is an English lesson for an IT professional built?

The material is your open-source or personal project — pull requests and tickets (work code only with your company's permission), a three-minute standup in English at the start of each lesson, interview rehearsal for a real job posting, and a demo with screen sharing.
