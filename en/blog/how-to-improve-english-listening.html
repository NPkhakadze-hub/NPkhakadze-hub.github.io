Can't understand spoken English? How to improve your listening 🎧

6 November 2026 · Nodar

“I read a text and understand everything, but when an American says the same words, I don't understand a thing.” I often hear this from students, and it's completely normal. Reading and listening are two different skills: in a text there are spaces between the words, in live speech there aren't.

The good news is that listening, like a muscle, gets stronger with practice. In this article I explain why spoken English is so hard to follow and how to practise so that you can see your own progress.

Why you don't understand: the words stick together

English speakers don't say words one by one — they join them into one stream. This is called connected speech. The most common things that happen:

  • Linking: a consonant “sticks” to the vowel of the next word: turn it off sounds like “tur-ni-toff”.
  • Lost sounds: next day sounds like “nex-day” — the t almost disappears.
  • Weak forms: short words (to, for, and, can, of) lose their stress: a cup of tea ≈ “uh CUP uh TEA” /ə ˈkʌp ə ˈtiː/.

A few examples of how a phrase is written and how it sounds:

What do you want? — in fast speech ≈ “whaddya WANT?”
I'll pick it up. — ≈ “I'll pi-ki-DUP”: three words merge into one.
Did you eat? — ≈ “didja EAT?”: d and y together sound like “j”.

That's why your ear keeps searching for the word you saw in the dictionary, while in live speech it sounds different.

gonna, wanna, gotta — spoken short forms

In spoken English some phrases get so compressed that they almost become new words. You'll rarely see them in writing, but in films and everyday conversation you'll hear them all the time:

gonna = going to — I'm gonna call you.
wanna = want to — Do you wanna come?
gotta = have got to — I gotta go. — I have to go.
lemme = let me — Lemme see. — wait, let me check.
dunno = don't know — I dunno.

You need to recognise these forms when you hear them. You don't have to use them when you speak, and in a formal email or an exam essay it's better not to use them at all.

⚠️ I'm gonna send the report tomorrow. — normal in conversation, but in a work email write: I'm going to send…

can or can't — how to tell them apart by ear

This is one of the hardest pairs, because the t at the end is often barely heard. In American English the main signals are stress and the vowel:

I can swim. — can is weak and short: “I kn SWIM” /kən/, the stress is on swim.
I can't swim. — can't is stressed and longer, with the vowel of “cat”: “I KANT swim” /kænt/.

In the pronunciation of southern England it's easier to tell them apart: can't has a long “a” — “kaant” /kɑːnt/.

Exercise 1: short dictation

One of the most useful exercises I suggest to students is dictation. It shows you which words “got lost” on the way to your ear:

  1. Choose a 30–60-second recording that has a transcript or subtitles (a podcast, a YouTube video).
  2. Listen to one sentence, pause, and write down what you heard.
  3. Where you didn't catch something, replay it 2–3 times. If you still can't, skip it.
  4. At the end, compare with the text and see which words you missed.

In most cases you'll find that the words you missed weren't new ones, but familiar short ones: of, to, have, the. Those are exactly the words to train your ear on.

Exercise 2: shadowing — repeating out loud

Shadowing means repeating what a speaker says with a slight delay, almost at the same time — at the same speed, with the same intonation and pauses. When you say wanna or turn it off in the linked way yourself, your ear recognises them more easily too.

Start with a slow, clear recording and a short piece. I describe how to practise with films and series in detail in the article learning English with films and TV series.

What to listen to at your level

  • A2–B1: podcasts for learners, where people speak slowly and clearly, often with a transcript. Also simple videos and children's series.
  • B1–B2: interviews, YouTube videos on a topic that interests you, series with English subtitles.
  • B2 and above: podcasts at natural speed, the news, films without subtitles.

The main rule: you should be able to understand most of the material, but not all of it. If you understand almost nothing, it won't help — choose something easier.

Test yourself

Write the full form:
1. I'm gonna be late. → I'm ___ be late.
2. Do you wanna dance? → Do you ___ dance?
3. I gotta work tomorrow. → I've ___ work tomorrow.
4. Lemme help you. → ___ help you.
5. I dunno where he is. → I ___ where he is.
6. I wanna learn Spanish. → I ___ learn Spanish.

Answers: 1. going to. 2. want to. 3. got to. 4. Let me. 5. don't know. 6. want to.

A tip: for several days in a row, 5–10 minutes a day, listen to the same short recording — first without the text, then with the text, and finally without it again. Understanding one recording deeply teaches you more than listening to ten on the surface. Frequently asked questions

Why do I understand English when I read it, but not when I hear it?

In live speech words join together, short words become weak and some sounds disappear. You can't see this in a text, so your ear needs its own practice.

Are gonna and wanna correct English?

They're very common in spoken English, and you need to recognise them. In a formal email or an exam essay, though, write going to and want to.

Which exercises improve listening?

Short dictation works well (listen, write it down, compare with the text), and so does shadowing — repeating out loud right after the speaker. Choose material you can mostly understand.
