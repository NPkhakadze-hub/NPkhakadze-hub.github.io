The 10 most common mistakes in English — and how to fix them 🔟

26 October 2026 · Nodar

If you learn English through your own language — that is, you think in your language first and then translate — a few mistakes are almost guaranteed to follow you around. It isn't carelessness: languages are simply built differently, and your brain looks for the structure it knows from your mother tongue.

Below are the ten mistakes I hear most often in my lessons. Each one comes with an example and a short explanation; where a topic has its own article, you'll find a link too.

1. “I'm agree” — agree is a verb

❌ I'm agree with you.
✅ I agree with you.

In many languages you say something like “I am in agreement”, so it feels natural to put am/is/are in the English sentence too. But agree is a verb — just like like or want — and in negatives and questions it needs do/does: I don't agree. Do you agree? The full explanation is in the article on “I'm agree”.

2. too where you need very

⚠️ Tbilisi is too beautiful. — grammatically correct, but it sounds like a complaint: “so beautiful that it's actually a problem”.
✅ Tbilisi is very beautiful.

In many languages both words often come out as the same “very” (even if there's a word that matches too exactly). In English, though, too before an adjective almost always means “more than is good or wanted”. For a compliment, use very, so or really. The difference is explained in detail in the article on too and very.

3. “I have 25 years” — age goes with be

❌ I have 25 years.
✅ I am 25.

This is a very common mistake, and it comes from languages where you “have” an age — French “j'ai 25 ans”, Spanish “tengo 25 años”. In English, age is expressed with be. The question uses the same verb: How old are you? All the details are in the article on age.

4. say and tell — mixed up

❌ He said me the truth.
✅ He told me the truth.

In many languages one verb covers both. In English it's tell + a person (tell me, tell her) and say + words (say hello, say that…). The difference between all four — say, tell, speak and talk — has its own article.

5. make and do

❌ I did a mistake.
✅ I made a mistake.

Many languages use a single verb for both. A simple guide: make — something new comes into existence (make a cake, make a decision, make a mistake); do — you carry out a task or activity (do homework, do the dishes). The full list of combinations is in the article on make and do.

6. The missing article

❌ I am teacher. I work in school.
✅ I am a teacher. I work in a school.

Many languages — Russian, Polish, Chinese and Japanese, for example — have no articles at all, so a and the are the first thing to get lost. A simple rule to start with: a singular countable noun (teacher, school, car) almost never stands alone — it needs a/an, the or a word like my/this in front of it. There's a separate article on articles.

7. The lost -s in the third person

❌ She work in a bank.
✅ She works in a bank.

In the Present Simple, the verb takes an -s with he / she / it — everyone knows that, and yet it still disappears in conversation. The reason is simple: this one sound doesn't change the meaning, so the brain treats it as “extra”. In negatives and questions, the -s moves to does: She doesn't work. Does she work?

8. “I live here since 2019”

❌ I live here since 2019.
✅ I have lived here since 2019.

In many languages you use the present tense here. In English, an action that started in the past and is still going on is expressed with the Present Perfect (or the Present Perfect Continuous: I've been living here since 2019). since gives the starting point, for the length of time: for five years. I write about this pair in the article on since and for.

9. he and she mixed up

❌ My sister is a doctor. He works at a hospital.
✅ My sister is a doctor. She works at a hospital.

Some languages — Finnish, Hungarian and Turkish, for example — have a single word for “he” and “she”. In English you have to choose every time: he or she, his or her. Even when your own language makes the same distinction, the mistake usually happens because in live conversation there's no time to think about it. A good exercise is to talk about your family members out loud and consciously pause at every pronoun.

10. Questions without do

❌ Where you live?
✅ Where do you live?

In many languages you can turn a sentence into a question with intonation alone, or simply by swapping the verb and the subject. In English, questions in the Present Simple and Past Simple almost always need an auxiliary verb — do / does / did — and it goes between the question word and the subject: What does she do? When did you arrive? (The exception is questions where the question word itself is the subject: Who lives here? What happened?) Be and modal verbs are different: there you just swap the places: Where are you? Can you help me?

A separate group of mistakes is “false friends” — words that look like words in your own language but mean something else: magazine, fabric, sympathetic.

Test yourself

Each sentence has one mistake — correct it:

1. I'm agree with Nino.
2. She live in Batumi now.
3. Where you work these days?
4. I work for this company since 2020.
5. My mother is a teacher. He works at a school.
6. I did a big mistake yesterday.

Answers: 1. I agree with Nino. 2. She lives in Batumi now. (Also possible: She's living in Batumi now.) 3. Where do you work these days? (Also correct: Where are you working these days?) 4. I have worked for this company since 2020. (Also correct: I've been working for this company since 2020.) 5. My mother is a teacher. She works at a school. 6. I made a big mistake yesterday.

All ten mistakes have one thing in common: you know the rule, but in conversation the old form still slips out. That's why my advice is to pick one of the ten — the one you make most often — and track only that one for a week: every time you notice the mistake, say the sentence again out loud, correctly. Then move on to the next one. Frequently asked questions

What is one of the most common mistakes learners make in English?

“I'm agree” is one of the most common. Many languages express this idea with “to be”, but in English agree is a verb and doesn't need be. Correct: I agree with you. Do you agree?

Why do learners make exactly these mistakes?

Most of them come from the structure of the learner's own language: some languages have no articles, some use one pronoun for both he and she, some form questions with intonation alone rather than with do. The brain carries the native-language structure over into English.

Where does “I have 25 years” come from?

From languages where you “have” an age, such as French (j'ai 25 ans) or Spanish (tengo 25 años). In English, age is expressed with be, never with have: How old are you? I am 25.

How do I get rid of these mistakes when I speak?

Focus on one mistake at a time: pick the one that slips out most often and track only that one for a week, repeating the correct form out loud every time. That's how a habit changes, not by reading the rule once.
