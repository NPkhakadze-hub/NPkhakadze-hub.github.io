Present Simple or Present Continuous — the difference, with examples 🕒

26 October 2026 · Nodar

In many languages, one present-tense verb does the whole job. In English, though, you have two options: I work and I'm working. Which one do I choose? Almost every beginner asks this, and it's no accident: in those languages, the one present tense covers both meanings — the context decides what you mean.

English, however, makes you choose up front. The good news is that the logic is simple. In this article, we'll boil everything down to one question.

One form in many languages, two in English

📆 every day → I work at a bank. — Present Simple
⏱️ right now → I'm working now — I'll call you later. — Present Continuous

In many languages, the verb stays the same — the difference comes from words like “every day” and “now”. In English, that difference is built into the verb form itself. So the key question is: is this generally true, or is it happening now?

🔑 Present Simple — the big picture: habits, facts, timetables.
🔑 Present Continuous — a snapshot of the moment: happening now, temporary, or already arranged.

Present Simple — when it's generally true

1. Habits and routines — things you do regularly.

✅ I get up at seven. — every day.
✅ She drinks two coffees a day.

2. Facts that don't change.

✅ Water boils at 100 degrees.
✅ My brother lives in Kutaisi.

3. Timetables — trains, lessons, films: the time doesn't depend on you.

✅ The train leaves at 6:40.
✅ The lesson starts at nine.

Remember one thing: in the third person (he, she, it), the verb takes an -s. This little letter is the one that gets lost most often — we'll look at it separately below.

Present Continuous — when it's happening now

1. Right at this moment — or during this period, even if you're not doing it this very second.

✅ I'm writing an email — give me a minute.
✅ She's studying for her exams this month.

2. Temporary situations — it's not usually like this, but it is right now.

✅ I usually drive to work, but this week I'm taking the bus.

3. Arranged future — when the time and place are already agreed.

✅ We're meeting Nino on Friday. — it's arranged.
✅ I'm flying to Berlin next week.

This last one often confuses people: we're talking about the future, but the tense is a present one. When will or going to works better — we have a separate article on that.

Words that tell you which tense to use

Present Simple: always · usually · often · sometimes · never · every day/week · on Mondays · once a week
Present Continuous: now · right now · at the moment · today · this week · these days · Look! · Listen!

State verbs — verbs that don't like -ing

Some verbs describe a state rather than an action: knowing, feeling, wanting, opinions. These verbs usually stay in the Present Simple, even when we're talking about “now”.

know · understand · believe · like · love · hate · want · need · mean · remember · belong
❌ I'm knowing the answer.
✅ I know the answer.
❌ She's wanting a new phone.
✅ She wants a new phone.

⚠️ As for McDonald's famous I'm lovin' it — that's informal, emotional language — you'll hear it in everyday conversation too (I'm loving this weather!). The slogan borrowed exactly that lively feel. In exams and in neutral or formal English, though, stick with I love it.

Some verbs have two meanings: I think it's a good idea (my opinion — a state) and I'm thinking about it (I'm considering it — a process). I have a car (I own one) and I'm having lunch (I'm eating). In cases like these, the meaning decides.

The three mistakes I see most often

1. The missing -s. In the Present Simple, the verb has almost no endings: I work, you work, we work — and then he works. Only the third person changes, and precisely because it's the only one, it's easy to forget.

❌ He work in a hospital.
✅ He works in a hospital.
❌ Does she likes coffee?
✅ Does she like coffee? — does has already taken the -s.

2. -ing with state verbs — I'm knowing, I'm needing. We saw it above: these verbs need the Present Simple.

3. Present Simple where “now” is meant.

❌ Sorry, I can't talk — I cook dinner.
✅ Sorry, I can't talk — I'm cooking dinner.

And one more that looks similar but is a different story: I am agree. That's not a tense mistake but a mix-up between a verb and an adjective — we have a separate article on it.

If the habit is in the past and no longer exists, you need used to instead of the Present Simple: I used to smoke. More on that in used to and be used to — the difference.

Test yourself

Put the verb in the correct form. Answers below.

1. Listen! Someone ______ (knock) at the door.
2. He ______ (go) to the gym three times a week.
3. I ______ (not / understand) this word — can you help me?
4. I usually walk to work, but this week I ______ (take) the bus.
5. The last bus ______ (leave) at 11:30 every night, so don't be late.
6. We ______ (have) dinner with Nino on Saturday — we booked the table yesterday.

Answers: 1. is knocking — Listen! It's happening now. 2. goes — a habit, third person, -s. 3. don't understand — a state verb. 4. am taking — a temporary situation (this week). 5. leaves — a timetable (every night). 6. are having (are going to have is also possible) — an arranged meeting; have here means “eat”.

One practical tip to finish: before you say the sentence, ask yourself for a second — “Is this generally true, or is it happening now?” Most of the time, that one question will give you the right form. And if the action started in the past and is still going on (“I've lived here for five years”), you need a third tense — we talk about that in the article “Present Perfect or Past Simple”. Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between the Present Simple and the Present Continuous?

The Present Simple gives the big picture: habits, facts, timetables (I work at a bank). The Present Continuous gives a snapshot of the moment: what's happening now, what's temporary or already arranged (I'm working now). The key question: is this generally true, or is it happening now?

Why do so many learners mix up I work and I'm working?

In many languages, one present tense covers both meanings, and words like "every day" or "now" show the difference. In English, the verb form itself carries that difference: I work (in general) versus I'm working (now).

When is the Present Continuous used for the future?

When a meeting or plan is already arranged and the time and place are agreed: We're meeting Nino on Friday. I'm flying to Berlin next week. For timetables (trains, lessons), use the Present Simple: The train leaves at 6:40.

Can you say "I'm knowing" or "I'm loving it"?

know, understand, like, want, need and similar verbs describe a state and usually don't take -ing: I know the answer, not I'm knowing. "I'm lovin' it" is informal, emotional language. People say it in everyday conversation too (I'm loving this weather!), and the slogan borrowed exactly that lively feel. In neutral speech and in exams, I love it is the better choice.
