“Tbilisi is too beautiful” — why this is a complaint, not a compliment 😅

26 October 2026 · Nodar

Picture this: you tell a visitor “Your city is too beautiful!” — and they give you a strange look. Why? Because in English too almost never just means “very”. too = “more than you want, so much that it's a problem”. So what you actually told them was: “Your city is so beautiful that it's a bad thing.” 🙃

✅ Tbilisi is very beautiful. — a compliment
✅ This coffee is too hot. I can't drink it. — a problem
✅ The bag is too expensive. — I can't afford it.

The rule in one sentence: if you could add “…and that's bad / so I can't…” to the sentence, use too. If you just want to add emphasis, use very.

And there is one more too, which goes at the end of a sentence and means something completely different — “as well, also”: I like this song. — Me too!

✍️ Mini quiz: too or very? — “The test was ___ long to finish in an hour.” (Answer: too. The pattern too + adjective + to: so long that you can't finish it in an hour.)

Why this matters

This is not a small detail. Before an adjective, “too” in English almost always signals a problem (the exception is a few fixed compliments, like You're too kind) — something is more than it should be. So when you say “Tbilisi is too beautiful”, the other person is waiting for the rest of the sentence: “…and that's why I can't stand it.”

✅ The coffee is very hot. — just a fact, not a problem. 👍
✅ The coffee is too hot. — I can't drink it, I'd burn my mouth. 👎

✅ This bag is very expensive. — it's pricey (but maybe I'll still buy it).
✅ This bag is too expensive. — I can't buy it.

Two patterns you need to know

too + adjective + to + verb — “so … that you can't”:

✅ It's too cold to swim.
✅ He's too young to drive.

adjective + enough + to — “… enough to be able to”. This is the opposite of “too”:

✅ She's old enough to drive. — she's allowed to now.
✅ It's warm enough to swim.

Watch the word order: too goes before the adjective, enough goes after it.

❌ She's enough old. → ✅ She's old enough.
⚠️ It's cold too. — correct, but it means something else: “it's also cold”.
✅ It's too cold. — “colder than it should be”.

The second meaning of too

At the end of a sentence, “too” means something completely different — “also”:

✅ I'm hungry too.
✅ She speaks German, too.

So the position of the word decides the meaning: too cold = colder than it should be. cold too = also cold.

And if you really want to pay a compliment?

✅ Tbilisi is so beautiful! — the most natural, emotional option.
✅ Tbilisi is really beautiful.
✅ Tbilisi is absolutely stunning. — with strong adjectives we don't use “very”; “absolutely” works better.

Not many learners know this rule: very goes with ordinary adjectives (very good, very tired), while strong ones take absolutely or really: absolutely amazing, not ❌ very amazing.

Test yourself

1. This soup is ___ salty to eat.
2. Your English is ___ good!
3. He isn't tall ___ to play basketball.
4. I'm tired ___. (meaning: “I'm also tired”)

Answers: 1. too. 2. very (also really, so, pretty, extremely, incredibly — any word that just adds emphasis; not too). 3. enough. 4. too (at the end = “also”).

This is one of those cases where a single word changes the whole mood. To keep a compliment a compliment, remember it simply: very — you praise, too — you complain.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between too and very?

Very simply makes a word stronger, and that's fine. Too means more than needed or wanted, and that's a problem. “The coffee is very hot” = it's just very hot. “The coffee is too hot” = I can't drink it.

Too or enough?

Too goes before the adjective and signals a problem: too cold to swim. Enough goes after the adjective and signals that something is possible: warm enough to swim.

What does “I'm tired too” mean?

At the end of a sentence, too means “also”: I'm tired as well. “I'm too tired”, on the other hand, means I'm more tired than I should be, for example too tired to go out.
