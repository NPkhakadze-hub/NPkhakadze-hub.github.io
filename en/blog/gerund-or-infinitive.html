Gerund or infinitive — to do or doing: which verbs take which form 🧩

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“I finished writing the letter” and “I decided to write a letter” — in many languages, the second verb in both sentences would take the same form, for example an infinitive or a verbal noun. In English, though, these two sentences are built differently: I finished writing the letter, but I decided to write a letter. In the first case the verb is followed by the -ing form (Gerund), in the second by to + V1 (Infinitive).

So relying on your own language won't help here: the form is chosen by the main verb, not by how you would say it in your language. Below I've sorted the most common verbs into groups, and at the end a short test is waiting for you.

Verbs followed by -ing

There is no solid logical rule for this group — you simply have to memorise the verbs. One hint will still help: many of them express an attitude towards an action or refer to an action that is already going on.

enjoy, avoid, mind (be bothered by, object to), finish, suggest, consider (think about, weigh up), keep (do again and again), miss, imagine, practise (American spelling: practice), give up (stop doing), can't stand (hate).

✅ She enjoys reading before bed.
✅ He keeps forgetting my name.
✅ We're considering moving abroad.
✅ I can't stand waiting in queues.
❌ Do you mind to close the door?
✅ Do you mind closing the door?

In American English can't stand is sometimes followed by to, but the -ing form is correct in both British and American English. I write about Would you mind…? as a polite request in the article on modal verbs.

Verbs followed by to + V1

Here, too, you need to memorise, but this group also has a hint: these verbs are often directed towards the future. A wish, a plan, a decision, a promise — all of them are about an action that hasn't happened yet.

want, would like, need (with things, in a passive sense, it also takes -ing: The car needs washing), decide, hope, plan, agree, refuse, promise, expect, learn, manage (succeed in doing), offer, afford (usually can afford — “have enough money for”).

✅ We've decided to sell the car.
✅ She refused to apologise.
✅ I'd like to book a table for two.
✅ He promised to call me back.

If there is a person between the verb and the action, some verbs (want, ask, tell, expect, would like) are built like this: verb + person + to + V1. Many languages use a “that” clause here, but in English no that appears in this pattern (and want and would like are never followed by a that clause at all).

✅ I want you to come with me.
✅ My boss asked me to stay late.

Two common verbs don't follow this pattern: make and let. After them, the person is followed by V1, without to.

✅ My boss made me stay late. — forced me to stay.
✅ My parents didn't let me go to the party. — didn't allow me to go.
❌ She made me to wait for an hour.
✅ She made me wait for an hour.

One clarification: in the passive, to comes back after make — I was made to wait. After help, both options are correct: Can you help me (to) carry this?

Both forms — with almost the same meaning

start, begin, continue, like, love, hate and prefer — these verbs can take either form, and the meaning mostly doesn't change.

✅ It started raining. / It started to rain.
✅ I love cooking. / I love to cook.

Two nuances. First: if start or begin is itself in the -ing form, people avoid two -ing forms in a row: It's starting to rain, not It's starting raining.

Second: in British English like doing usually means enjoyment, while like to do means a habit or a choice you think is sensible: I like to get to the airport early (not because it's fun, but because it keeps me calm). In American English like to is common in the sense of enjoyment as well. would like, would love and would prefer, on the other hand, express a specific wish and are followed by to + V1: I'd love to come.

Both forms — with different meanings

This is the most interesting group: here, changing the form changes the meaning too. A simple guide: -ing often refers to something that has already happened or was happening, while to refers to something that is still to come.

stop. stop doing — give something up; stop to do — stop (what you're doing) in order to do something.

✅ I stopped eating sugar last year. — I gave it up.
✅ We stopped to buy some water. — we stopped in order to buy water.

remember and forget. -ing — remembering something that has already happened; to — a task that still needs to be done. forget + -ing is mainly found in sentences like I'll never forget….

✅ I remember meeting him in Batumi.
✅ Remember to buy some bread on your way home.
✅ Don't forget to lock the door.
✅ I'll never forget seeing the sea for the first time.

try. try to do — you make an effort to do something (you don't know yet whether you'll succeed); try doing — an experiment: “do it and see whether it helps”.

✅ I tried to open the jar, but the lid was stuck.
✅ If your laptop is slow, try restarting it. — restart it and see if that helps.

regret. regret doing — you are sorry about something you did (or didn't do). regret to — a formal phrase for announcing bad news, mainly with the verbs inform, tell and say.

✅ I regret not learning English earlier.
✅ We regret to inform you that your flight has been cancelled.

After a preposition — -ing

If a verb comes after a preposition (in, at, of, for, about, before, after, without…), the verb takes the -ing form. This is one of the most reliable rules and will save you from a great many mistakes.

✅ I'm interested in learning Japanese.
✅ She's very good at drawing.
✅ Thank you for helping me.
✅ After finishing school, he moved to Tbilisi.
✅ He left without saying goodbye.

The main trap: in some expressions to is not the infinitive marker but a preposition. These fixed expressions include look forward to, be used to, get used to, object to. Check them like this: if a noun fits after to (I'm looking forward to the weekend), to is a preposition and the verb takes the -ing form. But this test only works for these fixed expressions: it doesn't apply to be going to or agree to (I'm going to see, I agreed to help).

❌ I'm looking forward to hear from you.
✅ I'm looking forward to hearing from you.

I go through be used to and get used to in detail in the article on used to.

Typical mistakes

1. to where -ing should be. This mistake is especially common with enjoy.

❌ I enjoy to swim.
✅ I enjoy swimming.

2. V1 after look forward to. to is a preposition here, so you need -ing.

❌ I'm looking forward to see you.
✅ I'm looking forward to seeing you.

3. suggest + person + to. suggest isn't built with this pattern. The correct options are suggest + -ing or suggest (that) + a clause in which the verb is in V1.

❌ I suggest you to take a taxi.
✅ I suggest taking a taxi.
✅ I suggest (that) you take a taxi.

4. want that. This mistake appears when “I want that…” is translated word for word from another language. want is not followed by a that clause.

❌ I want that you come with me.
✅ I want you to come with me.

5. would like + -ing. like takes both forms, but would like takes only to + V1.

❌ I would like visiting London.
✅ I'd like to visit London.

Test yourself

Put the verb in brackets into the correct form: -ing, to + V1 or V1. Each item has one answer.

1. Have you finished ___ (pack)? The taxi is already here.
2. We've decided ___ (move) to Batumi next year.
3. I'm really looking forward to ___ (meet) your parents on Saturday.
4. Please remember ___ (turn off) the lights when you leave tonight.
5. My parents never let me ___ (stay) out late when I was a teenager.
6. He stopped ___ (smoke) ten years ago, and now he feels much better.
7. She's very good at ___ (remember) people's names.
8. The manager refused ___ (give) us our money back.

Answers: 1. packing — finish is followed by -ing. 2. to move — decide + to. 3. meeting — in look forward to, to is a preposition. 4. to turn off — a task that still needs to be done (tonight). 5. stay — let + person + V1, without to. 6. smoking — he gave up smoking (now he feels much better confirms this). 7. remembering — -ing after a preposition (at). 8. to give — refuse + to.

A practical tip: don't write a new verb in your vocabulary notebook on its own — add the form that follows it: enjoy doing, decide to do, let someone do. That way you'll remember the form together with the word. I explain how to set up a notebook like this in the article on memorising words. Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a gerund and an infinitive?

A gerund is the -ing form of a verb (swimming), an infinitive is to + V1 (to swim). Which one comes after another verb is decided by the main verb: enjoy is followed by -ing (I enjoy swimming), decide by to (I decided to go).

Why is “I enjoy to swim” wrong?

enjoy belongs to the verbs that are followed only by the -ing form. The correct sentence is I enjoy swimming. The same applies to avoid, finish, mind, consider and keep.

Why do we say looking forward to seeing and not to see?

In look forward to, to is not the infinitive marker but a preposition, and after a preposition the verb takes the -ing form. In fixed expressions like this (look forward to, be used to, get used to, object to), check it like this: if a noun fits after to (looking forward to the weekend), the verb takes the -ing form. This test doesn't apply to be going to or agree to: I'm going to see, I agreed to help.

Do stop smoking and stop to smoke mean the same thing?

No. stop smoking means giving up smoking, while stop to smoke means stopping in order to smoke. The form also changes the meaning with remember, forget, try and regret.
