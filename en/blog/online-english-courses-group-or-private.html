Online English courses — group or private lessons, how to choose 🎯

26 October 2026 · Nodar

Type “online English courses” into a search engine and within a minute you'll have dozens of offers: a group course with a live teacher, a video course you watch on your own, private lessons. The prices vary, the descriptions all sound alike, and in the end one question remains: which one do I need?

In this article I'll go through all three options — honestly, without putting any of them down. A group course is a perfectly legitimate and cheaper path; private lessons give you more speaking time and a personal programme. Which one suits you depends on your goal, your time and your budget.

What “online English courses” means today

Online, the word “course” refers to three completely different things. Before you compare prices, first work out which one you're looking at.

  • Group online course. A live teacher, several students, a fixed schedule and a shared programme — usually based on one textbook. It runs on Zoom or Google Meet.
  • Video course or platform. Recorded lessons and automatic exercises. There's either no teacher at all, or one who only answers questions. It's the cheapest option and depends entirely on your discipline.
  • Private lessons. One teacher — one student. The programme is written for your level and your goal, and the timing fits your schedule.

All three are called an “online course”, but inside they differ as much as a lecture, a book and a conversation. So the question “How much does a course cost?” only has an answer once you know which course you mean.

How a group course is built, and how a private one is

A group course is built in modules: for example, A2 level — 12 weeks, two sessions a week, 60–90 minutes each. The programme is written in advance and moves at the group's average pace. That's an advantage — the structure is ready — and a drawback: if you didn't understand a topic, the group moves on anyway.

In a group, speaking time inevitably gets shared out. Divide 90 minutes among ten people and each gets nine minutes — and that's before you subtract the teacher's explanations. Good group courses solve this with pair work: students talk to each other in separate breakout rooms.

A private lesson is built the other way round: first your level is measured, then your goal is clarified, and only then is the programme written. If you still haven't got the Present Perfect by the second lesson, the third one goes on it too — nobody's rushing. The speaking time is all yours, and a mistake gets corrected the moment you make it.

📊 Group: 90 minutes ÷ 10 students = 9 minutes each, before subtracting the teacher's explanations
Private: 75 minutes ÷ 1 student = all the time is yours — this is arithmetic, not advertising.

Who a group suits — and who it doesn't

A group really is a good choice if:

  • Your budget is very tight. A group course is usually much cheaper than private lessons, and starting to learn is almost always better than not starting.
  • You're more motivated alongside others. For some people, the group's rhythm and the feeling of “I need to keep up too” really help.
  • You need the basics first. At A1–A2, the material is almost the same for everyone and works well in a group.
  • Your schedule is stable. The group won't move for you, so you need to be free at the same time every week.

A group is less useful if:

  • Your main goal is speaking. You only learn to speak by speaking, and in a group there's little time for it.
  • You have a specific deadline and goal — IELTS in four months, a job interview, moving abroad. Here the programme has to be tailored to your gaps, not to the group's average level.
  • Your level doesn't match the group's. A much weaker student falls behind, a much stronger one gets bored — either way, the money is wasted.
  • Your schedule keeps changing — shift work, business trips, a small child. A missed group lesson is simply lost.

And one more thing: there's a middle option between a group and private lessons — a mini-group of two or three people, with more speaking time and a lower price than private lessons.

If you're learning with a friend or colleague and you're at the same level, this is often the best compromise.

How to read a course description

A course description is an advert, and that's how you should read it: pay attention to what it says, and even more to what it doesn't. Five things you should definitely find in the description:

  • Placement. How do you get into a group — a test, an interview or “pick your own level”? The last option means the group will have people at different levels.
  • Hours. “Course — 3 months” tells you nothing. Work it out: how many sessions in total, and how long is each? 24 sessions × 60 minutes and 24 × 90 minutes are two different courses with the same name.
  • Speaking share. If the description says “communicative method”, ask specifically: how many minutes does each student speak in one session?
  • Homework correction. An automatic test marks a mistake but doesn't explain it. Who reads the texts you write, and who corrects them?
  • Materials. Is the textbook included in the price, or do you have to buy it separately? Do you keep the materials after the course, or does access close?

If you can't find answers to two of these five, ask the course administrator. If they can't answer either, that's an answer too.

What the price is made of

The price of a group course is split among all the students: the teacher's time, the platform, the materials — everything is shared across the group. That's why it's cheap, and that's why it can't be personal.

A video course is even cheaper, because the teacher's time isn't included at all: a lesson recorded once is sold many times. On the other hand, there's usually nobody to correct your mistakes.

The price of a private lesson includes something quite different: 75 minutes of the teacher's time just for you, materials prepared for your level before the lesson, and corrected homework after it. All three are the teacher's time, and that's exactly what you're paying for.

One tip for comparing: work out the price per hour, not per month. “So much a month” can mean eight hours or sixteen; two identical monthly prices can turn out to differ by a factor of two once you convert them to an hourly rate.

Six questions to ask before you pay

  • 1. “Which level will I be placed in, and how did you determine it?” The answer should be concrete: a test, a conversation, a result.
  • 2. “What's the maximum number of people in the group?” Six and fifteen are two different courses.
  • 3. “How many minutes will I speak in one session?” Exactly “I”, not “the group”.
  • 4. “Who corrects the homework, and how quickly?” “Automatically” and “the teacher, with comments” are different answers.
  • 5. “If I miss a session, how do I catch up?” A recording, the materials as a link, or is it simply lost?
  • 6. “How will I see my progress after two months?” If the answer is “you'll feel it”, that's not enough.

And a seventh — ask for an intro session. With me, that's a free 20-minute intro call on Google Meet — enough time to find out whether you understand the teacher and whether the teacher understands you.

Check yourself — group or private

✍️ Check yourself: answer “yes” or “no”:
1. Is your main goal speaking, not reviewing grammar?
2. Do you have a specific deadline — an exam, an interview, a move?
3. Does your schedule change from week to week?
4. After your last course, did you say “I couldn't keep up with the group” or “I got bored”?
Three or more “yes” answers — private lessons will suit you better. Two — a mini-group or private lessons, depending on your budget. One or none — a good group course is perfectly enough.

In short

A group online course is a good start if budget is the deciding factor and your level matches the group's. Private lessons pay off when your main goal is speaking or you have a specific deadline and want the programme built around your gaps.

A video course is a good addition to either, but on its own it's rarely enough.

Frequently asked questions

Group online course or private lessons — which is better?

Both are legitimate paths. A group is cheaper and good for the basics, if your level matches the group's and your schedule is stable. Private lessons give you all the speaking time and a personal programme — that pays off when your main goal is speaking or you have a specific deadline.

How many minutes will I speak in a group?

It's arithmetic: divide 90 minutes among ten students and each gets nine minutes, before you subtract the teacher's explanations. Good courses solve this with pair work — before you pay, ask how many minutes each student speaks in one session.

How should I read an online course description?

Find five things: how your level is assessed, how many hours there are in total (number of sessions × length), how much of the time is speaking, who corrects homework and whether materials are included in the price. If you can't find the answer to two of them, ask — and if they can't answer, that's an answer too.

How much does an online English course cost?

The price of a group course is shared across the group, so it's cheap; with private lessons you pay for the teacher's time — the lesson, the preparation and the homework correction. When comparing, work out the price per hour, not per month. My rates are on the pricing page.
