some or any — the rule, the exceptions and a common mistake 🍵

26 October 2026 · Nodar

In many languages you'd simply say “I have money” — no extra word needed. In English, though, three situations call for three different options: I have some money. Do you have any money? I don't have any money. That's exactly why they get mixed up: you seem to know the rule, but when you speak, you say whatever comes to mind first.

The good news is that the rule is short and there are only two exceptions. Below we'll go through the rule and the exceptions, look at why they trip people up, and at the end you can check yourself.

The basic rule: some / any

✅ some → in positive sentences: I have some money. (an unspecified amount)
✅ any → in negative sentences: I don't have any money.
✅ any → in questions: Do you have any money?

Both are used with uncountable nouns (money, water, time) and with plural countable nouns (friends, questions, apples): some friends, any questions. In many languages there's often no equivalent word at all — just “I have money”, “Do you have questions?” — and that's exactly why we tend to forget them.

❌ I don't have some money.
✅ I don't have any money.
✅ I have no money. — the same idea, just without don't.

So a negative can be expressed in two ways: not … any or no. Not both together (we'll come back to this below). any follows not only not but also other “negative” words — never, hardly, without — and if as well:

✅ She left without any explanation.
✅ There's hardly any milk left.
✅ If you have any questions, just ask.

Exception no. 1: some in questions — offers and requests

some appears in a question when you're not really asking anything — you're offering or requesting something and expecting a “yes”.

✅ Would you like some tea? (an offer)
✅ Can I have some water, please? (a request)
✅ Could you lend me some money?

Now compare two almost identical questions:

✅ Do you have any questions? (neutral: I don't know whether you have any or not)
⚠️ Do you have some questions? — not a mistake! You'd say this when you can see that the person really does have questions: “You've got some questions, right?”

So Do you have some…? is grammatically correct — it just has a different shade of meaning. If in doubt, any is the safe choice in questions; keep some for offers and requests. (In British English you'll often hear Have you got any…? — it means the same as Do you have any…?)

Exception no. 2: any in positive sentences = “it doesn't matter which”

✅ Any time is fine.
✅ You can take any bus — they all go to the station.
✅ Anyone can learn this.

With this meaning any can also go with a singular countable noun (any bus, any time). A simple test: if you can say “it doesn't matter which” or “whichever”, you need any — even in a positive sentence.

someone / anyone, something / anything, somewhere / anywhere

The compound words follow the same rule:

✅ Someone called you. · Did anyone call?
✅ I bought something. · I didn't buy anything.
✅ My keys are somewhere in the car. · I can't find them anywhere.

A handy way to think about it: someone / something / somewhere point to a real but unnamed person, thing or place, while anyone / anything / anywhere leave it open — maybe there is one, maybe not. “Someone called” is a fact — someone; “Did anyone call?” is a question — anyone. It's not a perfect match every time, but it works as a guide. The same exception applies to offers: Would you like something to drink?

There are negative forms too — nothing, no one / nobody, nowhere. They already contain the negative, so the verb stays positive: I saw nobody. = I didn't see anybody.

A common mistake: the double negative

❌ I didn't see nobody.
✅ I didn't see anybody.
✅ I saw nobody. — the same idea.

In many languages a double negative is perfectly normal — word for word, “I didn't see nobody”, “I didn't buy nothing” — and that's exactly what people carry over into English. In standard English, though, one negative per sentence is enough: either not … any- or no-. (In some dialects and songs you'll hear I don't need nobody, but in exams, at work and in writing it counts as a mistake.)

❌ I don't want nothing.
✅ I don't want anything.
❌ There isn't some milk in the fridge.
✅ There isn't any milk in the fridge.

Check yourself

Word bank: some · any · someone · anyone · somewhere · anywhere

1. Sorry, I can't help you today — I don't have ___ time.
2. Can I have ___ sugar in my coffee, please?
3. ___ time is fine — I'm free all day.
4. There's ___ in the kitchen — I can hear footsteps.
5. I looked everywhere, but I couldn't find my glasses ___.
6. I didn't tell ___ about it — it's a secret.

Answers: 1. any — negative. 2. some — a request. 3. Any — “it doesn't matter which”. 4. someone (somebody is correct too) — positive. 5. anywhere — negative. 6. anyone (anybody is correct too) — and not nobody: we don't want a double negative.

One practical tip: for a few days, pay attention to how you say “I don't have” — use any in every I don't have… that's followed by an uncountable or plural noun (time, money, friends); with a singular countable noun, a/an stays: I don't have a car. This small habit gets rid of one of the most common mistakes. The natural next step is other quantity words — much, many and a lot of — and the articles a/an/the, which you need for a cup of tea instead of some tea. Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between some and any?

some is used in positive sentences (I have some money), any in negatives and questions (I don't have any money. Do you have any money?). Both work with uncountable nouns and with plural countable nouns.

When is some used in a question?

When you are offering or requesting something and expect a “yes”: Would you like some tea? Can I have some water? In a neutral question, where you don't know the answer, any is better: Do you have any questions?

Can any be used in a positive sentence?

Yes, when it means “it doesn't matter which”: Any time is fine. You can take any bus. With this meaning any can also go with a singular noun.

Is “I didn't see nobody” correct?

Not in standard English — it's a double negative. The correct forms are I didn't see anybody or I saw nobody. Many languages use a double negative normally, but in English one negative per sentence is enough.
