Phrasal verbs explained simply — 25 phrasal verbs for work and conversation 🧩

26 October 2026 · Nodar

There's one wall between textbook English and real English: phrasal verbs. You know postpone, but your colleague says put off; you learnt cancel, but in the meeting you hear call off. You understand both words separately, but not together. That's exactly where you feel that you know the rules, but the living language is another matter.

In this article I've collected 25 phrasal verbs — the ones you'll need most often both at work and in everyday conversation. Each one comes with a plain-English meaning and one natural example. But first, one rule without which a phrasal verb won't work properly.

What a phrasal verb is and why it can't be translated word for word

A phrasal verb is a verb + a particle (up, off, on, out, back…) which together have a new, often completely unexpected meaning. give means to hand something over, up means “upwards”, but give up means to stop trying. That's why translating the particle on its own is pointless: it changes the meaning, and the same verb can mean different things in different contexts. In one sentence pick up means answering the phone, in another collecting a friend from the airport, and in a third learning a language without studying it, just by hearing it (I picked up some Spanish in Madrid). The only reliable way is to remember them through examples, in a situation. That's exactly how the list below is organised.

One more thing textbooks rarely mention: phrasal verbs are part of living, spoken English. In a formal letter postpone and cancel sound more natural, but on a call or in a meeting it's put off and call off. You should know both registers, but in speech it's phrasal verbs that make you sound like “one of us”.

Separable and inseparable — pick it up, not pick up it

Most phrasal verbs are separable: the object can go between the verb and the particle or after them. But if the object is a pronoun (it, them, him, me), it always goes in the middle — this rule has no exceptions.

✅ Pick up the phone. / Pick the phone up. (both are correct)
✅ Pick it up.
❌ Pick up it.

Inseparable verbs, on the other hand, don't split — the object always comes after the particle, even a pronoun: look after the kids → look after them. Below I've marked these verbs with 🔒; the rest are either separable or don't take an object at all.

Meetings and email

1. set up — arrange, organise (a meeting, a call)
Could you set up a meeting with the client for Thursday?
2. put off — postpone
Let's put the meeting off until next week.
3. call off — cancel
They called off the event at the last minute.
4. get back to 🔒 — reply later, contact someone again
I'll check the numbers and get back to you by Friday.
5. bring up — raise or mention a topic
Can I bring up one more thing before we finish?

Tasks and deadlines

6. carry out — do, conduct
We carried out a survey among our customers.
7. hand in — submit (work, an application)
Please hand in your report by Monday.
8. catch up on 🔒 — deal with things that have piled up
I need to catch up on my emails after the holiday.
9. run out of 🔒 — have none left, use up
We've run out of time — let's finish tomorrow.
10. come up with 🔒 — think of (an idea, a solution)
She came up with a brilliant idea for the campaign.

Phone and communication

11. hold on — wait (on the line)
Hold on a second, I'll get a pen.
12. hang up — end a phone call
Don't hang up — I'm still here.
13. put through — connect (to another person or department)
Could you put me through to the sales department?
14. call back — phone someone back
Can I call you back in ten minutes?
15. speak up — speak more loudly
Could you speak up a bit? The line is bad.

Everyday conversation

16. get up — get out of bed
I usually get up at seven, even at the weekend.
17. pick up — collect (by car); lift, take
Can you pick me up from the airport?
18. look forward to 🔒 — wait for something with pleasure
I'm really looking forward to the weekend.
19. hang out — spend time (with friends)
We usually hang out at a café after work.
20. run into 🔒 — meet by chance
I ran into an old friend at the supermarket.

Problems and solutions

21. deal with 🔒 — handle, take care of an issue
I'll deal with the complaint myself.
22. sort out — fix, put in order
Don't worry, we'll sort it out by tomorrow.
23. figure out — work out, understand
I can't figure out why the file won't open.
24. turn down — refuse (an offer)
He turned down the offer because of the salary.
25. give up — stop doing something; stop trying
I gave up smoking two years ago. / Don't give up!

Typical mistakes — and why they happen

With these verbs learners tend to go wrong in almost the same places, and the reason is the same too — the English sentence gets built on the pattern of their own language. The four most common mistakes:

❌ Can you pick up me at six? / Call back me later.
✅ Can you pick me up at six? / Call me back later. — with a separable verb, a pronoun can only go in the middle.
❌ I look forward to meet you.
✅ I look forward to meeting you. — here to is a preposition, not part of the infinitive, so it's followed by -ing.
❌ I gave up to smoke.
✅ I gave up smoking. — give up takes -ing, never to + infinitive, even if your language uses an infinitive here; after a phrasal verb, a second verb is often in the -ing form.
❌ He turned off the job offer.
✅ He turned down the job offer. — wrong particle: turn off means switch off, refusing is turn down.

Phrasal verbs don't stick all at once — you need to review them at intervals of a few days. The method is described in the article on remembering words.

Check yourself

Fill in a suitable phrasal verb from this article (or part of one) — the meaning is given in brackets. In item 4, write the verb in brackets in the correct form.

1. We've ___ ___ ___ paper — can you order some more? (we have none left)
2. Could you hold ___ a second? I'll check the calendar. (wait)
3. I'm afraid we have to ___ ___ the meeting until next week. (postpone)
4. I'm really looking forward to ___ (see) you next month.
5. Don't ___ ___ — you're almost there! (don't stop trying)

Answers: 1. run out of. 2. on. 3. put off. 4. seeing. 5. give up.

One practical tip: don't memorise phrasal verbs as a list — find one sentence from your own life for each of them (think of your morning routine → I get up at seven every morning) and repeat it out loud for two or three days. Frequently asked questions

What is a phrasal verb?

A verb + a particle (up, off, on, out…) which together have a new meaning: give up — stop trying, put off — postpone. Translating the particle on its own doesn't work — you need to remember the verb in context, with an example.

What is the difference between separable and inseparable phrasal verbs?

With a separable verb the object can go in the middle or at the end (pick up the phone / pick the phone up), but a pronoun always goes in the middle: pick it up, not pick up it. Inseparable verbs (look after, deal with, run into) don't split — the object always comes at the end.

Which phrasal verbs will I use most often at work?

For meetings — set up, put off, call off, get back to; for tasks — carry out, hand in, catch up on, come up with; for the phone — hold on, put through, call back; for problems — deal with, sort out, figure out.

Why is it “look forward to meeting you” and not “to meet you”?

Here to is a preposition, not part of the infinitive, so it is followed by the -ing form: I look forward to meeting you, I'm looking forward to the weekend.
