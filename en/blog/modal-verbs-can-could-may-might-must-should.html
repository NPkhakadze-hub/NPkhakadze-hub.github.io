Modal verbs explained simply — can, could, may, might, must, should 🔑

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“I'm able to”, “it's allowed”, “I need to”, “it would be better to” — many languages cover all of these ideas with just one or two words, but English has a separate verb for each: can, could, may, might, must, should. That is exactly why they get mixed up so often — and exactly why it's worth sorting them out properly, once.

The good news: modal verbs have very simple grammar. First we'll look at three rules they all share, then at what each one means, and finally at one pair that causes the most dangerous mistake.

Three rules that work for every modal verb

1. No -s in the third person. This is the most common mistake in beginners' writing.

❌ She cans drive.
✅ She can drive.

2. The next verb comes without to. Have to and ought to don't fit this rule — they are not true modal verbs.

❌ You must to wear a helmet.
✅ You must wear a helmet.

3. Questions and negatives don't need do. The modal verb moves to the front by itself or takes not by itself.

❌ Do you can help me?
✅ Can you help me?

can and could — ability, requests, guesses

can says that you are able to do something or that something is possible: I can swim. · You can park here.

could does three different jobs. First — ability in the past: When I was a child, I could run for hours. Second — a polite request: Could you open the window, please? Third — a less certain guess: This could be the wrong address.

One detail that often comes up in exams: if you succeeded in doing something once, in a specific situation in the past, was able to or managed to sounds more natural than could.

⚠️ I could find my keys yesterday.
✅ I managed to find my keys yesterday. — a one-off result, not an ability.

may and might — permission and probability

may has two meanings: formal permission (May I come in?) and probability (It may rain later.). might is mainly about probability and expresses slightly less certainty than may.

Can I…? and May I…? are both correct; the difference is style. May I…? is more formal and fits work situations or talking to strangers, Can I…? fits everyday conversation.

must and have to — a difference that really matters

Both express obligation, but the source is different. must usually expresses the speaker's own attitude, have to an obligation that comes from outside: a rule, a law, the circumstances.

✅ I must call my mother tonight. — I myself feel I need to call her.
✅ I have to wear a uniform at work. — it's the rule, not my choice.

must has a second meaning too: a confident conclusion drawn from the facts.

✅ The lights are on, so she must be at home. — not a wild guess: you're almost sure.

mustn't or don't have to — the most dangerous pair

In the positive, must and have to are close. In the negative, though, they mean opposite things: mustn't is a prohibition (“it's not allowed”), don't have to means there is no obligation (“it isn't necessary, but you can if you want”).

✅ You mustn't take photos here. — taking photos here is forbidden.
✅ You don't have to take photos — I'll send you mine. — there's no need to take photos.

Remember this one pair, and you will no longer make the most costly mistake with modal verbs.

can't and couldn't — when you're sure something isn't true

For the opposite of the must conclusion, standard textbook English doesn't use mustn't — that role is taken by can't. (In American English, must not is still found with this meaning.) So: “it's almost certainly true” = must be, “it's impossible that it's true” = can't be.

❌ He mustn't be at home — his car isn't here.
✅ He can't be at home — his car isn't here. — you're sure he isn't there.

In the past, the same idea is expressed with can't have or couldn't have: She can't have seen me — I was in another room.

One detail about pronunciation: in fast speech, can and can't sound alike. In British English can't has a long vowel, while in American English the difference is mainly in stress — can't is more stressed. If you can't tell, it's better to ask again.

The politeness ladder for requests

English can make the same request on several levels — from the most direct to the most careful:

Can you help me? — ordinary, with people you know well. Could you help me? — polite, universal; fits at work and with strangers. Would you mind helping me? — the most careful.

The last form has one trap: after Would you mind… the verb takes the -ing form, and the answer is reversed too — “no” means you agree.

❌ Would you mind to open the window?
✅ Would you mind opening the window?

have to — past and future

can has a past form — could. must, should and may don't: they can't form a past or a future on their own. That's exactly why, for obligation, we need have to — it behaves like an ordinary verb and works in every tense.

Past: I had to leave early. Future: I will have to leave early. Third person: She has to leave early. Negatives and questions use do: I didn't have to pay. · Do you have to work on Saturday?

❌ Yesterday I must go to the doctor.
✅ Yesterday I had to go to the doctor.

should — advice and expected results

should gives advice or says what would normally be expected to happen: You should see a doctor. · The train should arrive at six. The negative is shouldn't.

Compared with must, should is much softer: You must stop smoking sounds like an order, You should stop smoking like advice.

The past: must have and should have

Guesses and regrets about the past are expressed with have + the third form (past participle). must have — a confident guess about the past; should have — something that should have been done but wasn't.

✅ She isn't here — she must have left early.
✅ I should have studied more. — and I didn't.

Test yourself

Fill in the correct form. Use each form exactly once: could, mustn't, don't have to, must, should, should have.

1. When I was a child, I ___ swim very well.
2. You ___ use your phone here — it's a hospital.
3. It's Sunday, so you ___ get up early. You can sleep in.
4. His phone is off and his bag is still here, so he ___ be in the building — there's no other explanation.
5. You look exhausted. Maybe you ___ go to bed earlier.
6. I failed the test. I ___ studied more.

Answers: 1. could — ability in the past. 2. mustn't — a prohibition. 3. don't have to — it isn't necessary (“You can sleep in” confirms this). 4. must — a confident conclusion based on facts (“there's no other explanation” rules out the other options). 5. should — advice (must is impossible after “maybe”). 6. should have — something that should have been done but wasn't.

A practical tip: you don't learn modal verbs from a list — you learn them from situations. Pick one day, and every time you think “I have to” or “I'm allowed to” in your own language, build the English version in your head too. Little by little, this habit makes the choice automatic. Frequently asked questions

Do mustn't and don't have to mean the same thing?

No, they mean opposite things. mustn't is a prohibition — “it's not allowed”; don't have to says there is no obligation — “it isn't necessary, but you can”. In the positive, must and have to are close, but in the negative they can't replace each other.

What is the difference between must and have to?

must usually expresses the speaker's own attitude (“I must call my mother”), while have to expresses an obligation from outside — a rule, a law or the circumstances (“I have to wear a uniform at work”). must also has a second meaning: a confident conclusion.

Can I or May I — which is correct?

Both are correct; the difference is style. May I…? is more formal and fits work situations or talking to strangers; Can I…? is the normal form in everyday conversation.

Does a modal verb take -s in the third person?

No. A modal verb has the same form for every person — “she can”, “he must”, “it should” — and the next verb comes without to. The exceptions are have to and ought to: they are not true modal verbs and keep to; have to also changes like an ordinary verb — “she has to work”.
