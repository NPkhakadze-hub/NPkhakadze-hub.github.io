“I have 25 years” — how to say your age in English 🎂

26 October 2026 · Nodar

In English, age goes with the verb be: I am 25. If your language works the same way and you say the equivalent of “I am 25”, that's good news — your first language isn't getting in the way here, it's helping you.

So where does “I have 25 years” come from? From other languages: in Spanish it's tengo 25 años, in French j'ai 25 ans — there it really is “I have”. Anyone who speaks one of these languages, or is learning English as a third language, often carries exactly this structure over into English.

❌ I have 25 years.
✅ I am 25.
✅ I am 25 years old. — this is completely normal too; it's just that in conversation we often choose the short form.

In English, age is a quality, not a possession — just like I am tall or I am tired.

Two small details that make you sound like a pro

1. years old, not year old — if the number is more than one.

❌ She is 7 year old.
✅ She is 7 years old.

2. But with hyphens — singular! When the age works as an adjective in front of a noun:

✅ a 7-year-old boy
✅ a 25-year-old student
❌ a 7-years-old boy

How to ask someone's age

❌ How many years do you have?
❌ How old do you have?
✅ How old are you?
✅ How old is your daughter?

In a formal situation it's better not to ask at all, or to choose a polite form: May I ask your age? In British and American culture, asking a stranger their age is considered awkward — even though in some other cultures it's a perfectly ordinary question.

A similar trap: have or be

With age, be is easy to remember, but in other places choosing between have and be really is a trap — especially with physical sensations:

❌ I have hungry. / I have cold.
✅ I'm hungry. · I'm cold.

❌ I have right.
✅ I'm right.

❌ I have 30 degrees in my room. — (in a weather forecast you may hear “we've got 30 degrees today”, but when you talk about a room, the standard form is It's…)
✅ It's 30 degrees in my room.

But be careful — sometimes have really is correct:

✅ I have a headache.
✅ I have a cold. — I'm ill with a cold. (With a, it means something completely different!)
✅ I have time.

Remember it like this: I'm cold = you feel cold. I have a cold = you're ill. One tiny a, and the meaning changes completely.

Useful phrases about age

✅ I'm in my early twenties. — I'm about 20–23.
✅ She's in her mid-thirties. — she's about 34–36.
✅ He's in his late forties. — he's about 47–49.
✅ I'm turning 30 next month. — I'll be 30 next month.

You'll hear the first three phrases very often in real conversation — especially when someone doesn't want to give an exact number — and when you use them, it's immediately clear that you have a good command of the language.

Test yourself

Fill in be, have or year(s) in the correct form:

1. — How old ___ your brother? — He ___ 19.
2. They have a 5-___-old daughter.
3. Close the window, please — I ___ cold.
4. I can't come to work today, I ___ a cold.

Answers: 1. is … is — age always takes be. 2. year — singular with hyphens. 3. am — you feel cold. 4. have ('ve got is also correct) — you're ill.

Age is one of those topics that's simple once you've got it — which is exactly why it's worth learning it properly once and never having to come back to it. And just learn I'm cold and I have a cold as a pair: one is a feeling, the other is an illness.

Frequently asked questions

How do I say how old I am in English?

I am 25 or I am 25 years old. “I have 25 years” is wrong — in English, age is expressed with the verb be.

“a 7-year-old boy” or “a 7-years-old boy”?

When the age is hyphenated and works as an adjective in front of a noun, it is always singular: a 7-year-old boy. On its own, it's He is 7 years old.

What's the difference between “I'm cold” and “I have a cold”?

I'm cold means you feel cold. I have a cold means you are ill with a cold. One small a, and the meaning changes completely.
