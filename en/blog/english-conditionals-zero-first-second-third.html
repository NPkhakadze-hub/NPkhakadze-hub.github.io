Conditionals · 10 min read

English conditionals explained simply — all four types on one page 🔀

26 October 2026 · Nodar

If I will have time… — I've heard this sentence from learners with all kinds of first languages, and it's completely logical: the time you're talking about is in the future, and in many languages you'd put the condition in the future too. English, though, follows a different rule here — and that's exactly why conditional sentences are one of the most confusing topics.

In reality, all four types follow one logic: how real the condition is and which time it refers to. Below we'll look at each one separately, and at the end we'll put them all in one list so you can tell them apart at a glance.

What a conditional sentence is made of

Every conditional sentence has two parts: the condition (the if-clause) and the result (the main clause). The order is free — you can start with if or with the result. One punctuation rule: when the if-clause comes first, it's followed by a comma; when it comes second, you don't need a comma.

✅ If it rains, we'll stay at home.
✅ We'll stay at home if it rains. — the same, just without the comma.

What separates the types is which tenses are used in these two parts — the “formula”. There are four basic formulas in all, and each one serves a different situation.

Zero conditional — what is always true

Formula: If + Present Simple, Present Simple.

This type is about facts, laws of nature and habits. Here if means almost the same as when: the result is the same every time.

❌ If you will heat ice, it melts.
✅ If you heat ice, it melts.
✅ If I drink coffee late, I don't sleep well.
⚠️ If you heat ice, it will melt. — this is correct too, but it's already a first conditional: not a general law, but one specific case.

First conditional — the real future

Formula: If + Present Simple, will + verb.

The condition is real and refers to the future: it may happen, or it may not. This is where the most common mistake is born. Both actions lie in the future — having time and calling — so your hand writes will twice all by itself: “if I will have time, I will call you”. In English, however, after if the future meaning is carried by the Present Simple; will only goes in the result.

❌ If I will have time, I will call you.
✅ If I have time, I'll call you.
❌ If it will rain tomorrow, we will cancel the trip.
✅ If it rains tomorrow, we'll cancel the trip.

In the result, instead of will you can have can, might, should or an imperative: If you see Ana, tell her to call me. The rule only concerns the if-clause — that's where will doesn't go. The same rule applies to when, as soon as and until. There is one rare exception — If you'll wait a moment, I'll check — but here will isn't the future, it's a polite request. Learn the main rule first.

Second conditional — the imagined present

Formula: If + Past Simple, would + verb.

Here the Past Simple doesn't mean the past — it means “unreal”: the situation is about the present or the future, but it's either impossible or unlikely. It's the classic “imagine if…” sentence: “If I had more money, I would buy a car.”

The second big mistake appears exactly here — would moves into the if-clause as well. In many languages the conditional form appears in both halves of the sentence, so would know feels natural after if — but in English would doesn't go after if. A simple check: would is only in the result; after if — an ordinary Past Simple.

❌ If I would know the answer, I would tell you.
✅ If I knew the answer, I'd tell you.
❌ If I would have more time, I would learn Spanish.
✅ If I had more time, I'd learn Spanish.

In standard English the verb be in this type takes the form were for every person — hence If I were you. In conversation you'll also hear If I was you, but in writing and in exams were is the safe choice.

⚠️ If I was you, I'd take the job. — common in speech, but informal.
✅ If I were you, I'd take the job.

Third conditional — the past that can't be changed

Formula: If + Past Perfect, would have + V3 (the third form of the verb).

This type is about the past — about something that didn't happen and that you can no longer change: regret, explanation, “if only”. “If I had known…”, “if she had come…” — the situation is over, and we're imagining a different version of it.

The formula is the longest, so the mistake comes in two kinds: either would gets into the if-clause (If I would have known), or a plain Past Simple stays where the Past Perfect should be (If I knew — which is already a second conditional and refers to the present).

❌ If I would have known, I would have come earlier.
✅ If I had known, I'd have come earlier.
❌ If I studied harder last year, I would have passed the exam.
✅ If I had studied harder last year, I'd have passed the exam.

In speech, had and would both contract to 'd: If I'd known, I'd have come. Train your ear so you don't mix up these two 'ds — the first is had, the second is would.

All four in one list

  • Zero — If + Present Simple, Present Simple. A fact, a law, a habit. If you heat ice, it melts.
  • First — If + Present Simple, will + verb. The real future. If it rains, we'll stay at home.
  • Second — If + Past Simple, would + verb. The imagined present / future. If I had more time, I'd learn Spanish.
  • Third — If + Past Perfect, would have + V3. The unreal past. If I had known, I'd have come.

The whole list comes down to one rule: after if, as a rule, there's neither will nor would. If you remember just this one thing, most of the mistakes above disappear by themselves.

What your first language tells you — and where it misleads you

Your first language most likely has its own way of telling real conditions from imagined ones — and that's good news. A real condition — “if I have time” — is something that may actually happen, and that corresponds to the first conditional. An imagined one — “if I knew”, “if I had known” — is a situation that isn't true, and that's the second or third conditional. So if the situation is imagined, in English you need would; if it's real — will (and for a general fact, in the zero conditional, simply the Present Simple).

But word-for-word translation misleads you in two places. The first is the future tense in the condition: if your language puts the condition in the future, you'll be tempted to write If I will have. The second is the result: in some languages one and the same form of “I would tell you” covers both situations — “if I knew, I would tell you” and “if I had known, I would have told you”. In English these are two different forms: I'd tell you and I'd have told you. So when you're talking about the past, add have in the result as well — translating word for word won't remind you.

Mixed conditional — when the past touches the present

Life doesn't always fit into one time: often a condition in the past has a result in the present. For this, the third and second types merge — the condition in the Past Perfect, the result with would + verb; or the other way round — a permanent condition in the Past Simple, the result with would have + V3.

✅ If I had listened to you, I wouldn't be in this mess now.
✅ If she weren't so shy, she would have asked him. — (a permanent quality → a result in the past)

This combination only comes naturally once all four basic types are automatic for you — so go back to the list above first, and leave the mixed conditional for B2 level.

would, could and might are modal verbs — I explained the difference between them in the article on modal verbs.

Test yourself

Put the verb in the correct form. Pay attention to the context — it tells you which type it is.

1. If you ______ (mix) blue and yellow, you get green.
2. If it ______ (rain) tomorrow, we'll stay at home.
3. If I ______ (be) you, I'd talk to her today.
4. If we ______ (leave) earlier yesterday, we wouldn't have missed the train.
5. If I ______ (have) more free time, I'd learn Spanish — but I don't.

Answers: 1. mix — a general fact, zero. 2. rains — the real future, first (is raining is also correct); not will rain. 3. were — the imagined present, second (in conversation you'll also hear was). 4. had left — the past, third; a plain left isn't enough. 5. had — second: but I don't tells you that I don't have it now, so it's an unreal present.

One practical tip: when you write or say an if-sentence, first ask yourself — is this a real possibility or an imagined one? Real = zero or first (will only in the result), imagined = second or third — and then decide just one more thing: is it the present or the past? If the temptation to put will after if won't leave you alone, the article on the three ways to talk about the future explains this rule from another angle; and the third form (V3) in the Past Perfect is the same as in the Present Perfect — if you haven't got it firmly in place yet, start with the article on the Present Perfect and the Past Simple. Frequently asked questions

How many types of conditional sentences are there in English?

Four main ones: zero (a fact — If you heat ice, it melts), first (the real future — If it rains, we'll stay at home), second (the imagined present — If I had more time, I'd learn Spanish) and third (the unreal past — If I had known, I'd have come). There is also the mixed conditional, which links a condition in the past with a result in the present.

Why is “If I will have time” wrong?

In English, after if the future meaning is carried by the Present Simple; will only goes in the result: If I have time, I'll call you. Because the situation is in the future, many learners add an extra will — but it doesn't belong in the if-clause.

Can I use would after if?

Usually not. If I would know and If I would have known are mistakes; the correct forms are If I knew (second conditional) and If I had known (third conditional). would only goes in the result part.

How do I know which conditional to use?

Ask two questions. Is the situation real (it may actually happen) or imagined? Real means zero or first: If I have time, I'll call you. Imagined means second or third. Then: is it about the present or the past? If I knew, I'd tell you (present) — If I had known, I'd have told you (past). The trap is the result: I'd tell you and I'd have told you are different forms.
