“I know it all, but I'm afraid to speak” — why it happens and what helps 🗨️

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“I know the grammar, I know the words — but when a native speaker talks to me, I get tongue-tied.” I hear this from almost every new student. The good news: it's not a talent problem, and it's not a memory problem either.

Why this happens

At school most of us learned English mainly with our eyes: rule, exercise, test. The brain learned to analyse English, not to produce it. Those are two different skills — like reading music and playing an instrument.

Then there's a second factor: the fear of making a mistake and looking silly. That fear “switches off” the part of the brain that produces language. The result: you forget even a word you know by heart.

Five things that actually work

1. Learn phrases, not single words. The brain doesn't build a sentence word by word — it uses ready-made chunks. “I was wondering if…”, “It depends on…”, “To be honest…” — a few dozen chunks like these will serve you better than hundreds of separate words.

2. Talk to yourself, out loud. Five minutes a day: describe what you're doing, what you're planning, what happened today. It sounds funny, but this is exactly how speaking becomes automatic — and nobody is listening.

3. Don't translate in your head. Translating from your native language takes far more time and produces constructions that are foreign to English. It's better to say simply what you already know how to say than to “carry over” a complicated thought from your own language.

🤔 You think (in your own language): “I wanted to say that, unfortunately, I won't be able to attend.”
😰 What comes out: I wanted to say that unfortunately I will not can to be present…
😌 Better: Sorry, I can't come. — the same idea, clean English.

4. Get used to the idea that mistakes are part of the process. Nobody learns a language without mistakes. A child who says “I goed” instead of “I went” is learning — not failing. The only difference between that child and an adult is that the adult feels embarrassed.

5. Find a regular, safe space. Speaking can't be learned from theory alone — it takes practice: saying things out loud, ready-made chunks and lots of listening. And ideally in an environment where a mistake isn't punished but corrected.

What to expect, and in what order

In my experience, the order is usually this: first the fear goes, then the words come, and last — speed and accuracy. The pace is different for everyone — it depends on how much you speak between lessons.

If you recognised yourself in this text — this is usually where we start. From the very first lesson, you do the talking, not me.

If you find it hard to start a conversation with a stranger, try the safest format — a short chat about the weather or the weekend. I've collected ready-made phrases in the small talk article.

Today's task

One concrete step you can take right now: take these five chunks and make one sentence with each about your own life — out loud.

1. I was wondering if…
2. It depends on…
3. To be honest,…
4. I'm not sure, but…
5. The thing is,…

All five are phrases that buy you time — while you're saying them, your brain is already building the rest. That's exactly why native speakers use them too.

If practising alone doesn't make the fear go away, it's better to speak in a safe environment — one where a mistake is working material and nobody interrupts you. Frequently asked questions

Why can't I speak if I know the grammar?

Analysing a language and speaking it are two different skills — like reading music and playing it. At school we mostly learn the first. You only learn to speak by speaking.

How long until my fear of speaking goes away?

There's no exact timeframe — the pace is individual. But the order is almost always the same: the fear fades sooner than your vocabulary grows.

What should I do if I forget a word in a conversation?

Say it more simply, using what you already know. “Sorry, I can't come” means the same as a long, odd translation — and it's much cleaner English.
