See, look or watch? Hear or listen? The difference explained 👀

6 November 2026 · Nodar

“Look this photo!”, “I looked a film yesterday”, “I like listening music” — mistakes like these are very common, because many languages divide seeing and hearing differently from English. English has three verbs for seeing: see, look and watch, and two for hearing: hear and listen. The problem is that they often don't match the verbs in your language one to one.

In this article I put all five verbs into one system, so you don't have to stop and think every time which one to choose.

The main idea: by chance or on purpose

The key to all five verbs is one question: are you trying to see or hear something, or is it just happening?

see — your eyes notice something by themselves
look — you turn your eyes towards something on purpose
watch — you look carefully, for some time, at something that moves or changes

hear — a sound reaches your ears by itself
listen — you pay attention to a sound

see — when you simply see

see needs no effort: your eyes are open and you notice something.

✅ I saw your sister in the supermarket. — I noticed her, I wasn't looking for her.
✅ Can you see the sea from your window? — is it visible from there?
✅ I can't see without my glasses. — the ability to see

see has two more very common meanings — “meet” and “understand”: I'm seeing a friend tonight (I'm meeting a friend this evening), I see what you mean (I understand what you mean).

look — when you turn your eyes

look means looking at something on purpose. When you say what you're looking at, look needs at:

❌ Look this photo!
✅ Look at this photo!
✅ She looked at me and smiled.
✅ Look! It's snowing. — no object, so no at

look has other meanings too: You look tired (you seem tired), I'm looking for my keys (I'm trying to find them).

watch — when you look for a long time and carefully

watch is used for anything that moves, changes or goes on over time: a film, a match, children playing.

❌ I looked a film yesterday.
✅ I watched a film yesterday.
✅ We watched the match on TV.
⚠️ I saw a film yesterday. — correct: it simply means you saw a film (especially at the cinema).

I marked the last example with ⚠️: see a film is perfectly natural when you just say that you saw a film (often at the cinema). watch TV, on the other hand, is a fixed phrase: you can't say see TV for watching television (although I saw it on TV is correct).

hear and listen — the same logic for sounds

hear = a sound reaches you by itself. listen = you pay attention. Before an object, listen needs to:

✅ Did you hear that noise? — the sound reached you
✅ I can't hear you. The connection is bad. — your voice doesn't reach me
❌ I like listening music.
✅ I like listening to music. — you pay attention to it
❌ Listen me, please.
✅ Listen to me, please. — pay attention to what I say

Two common phrases with hear: hear about — to learn about something, hear from — to get news from someone:

✅ Have you heard about the new café near the station? — do you know about it?
✅ I haven't heard from him for a month. — he hasn't called or written

In an online lesson or on a call, this is the sentence you need most often: Sorry, I can't hear you — not I can't listen to you, which means something quite different: “I can't listen to you right now, I'm busy” or “I can't listen to you any more, I'm tired of it”.

Verbs that aren't usually used in the Continuous

see and hear are verbs of perception, and they're mostly not used in the Continuous. Instead, we often add can:

⚠️ I'm hearing music from next door. — you may hear this in casual speech, but this is more usual:
✅ I can hear music from next door.
✅ I'm listening to music. — listen describes an action, so -ing is fine
⚠️ I'm seeing the doctor tomorrow. — correct: here see = “meet”, it's a planned visit.

I explain the details of this rule in the article Present Simple or Present Continuous.

Test yourself

1–3: see, look or watch? 4–6: hear or listen? (in the correct form)
1. We usually ___ TV for an hour after dinner.
2. ___ at the board, please.
3. I ___ an old friend at the airport yesterday. It was a nice surprise.
4. Speak louder, please. I can't ___ you.
5. She's ___ to a podcast in the car.
6. ___ to me! This is important.

Answers: 1. watch. 2. Look. 3. saw. 4. hear. 5. listening. 6. Listen.

A tip: for one day, describe in your head, in English, what you see, what you watch and what you hear: “I can see the bus. I'm watching the rain. I can hear birds.” This links the verbs to situations, and you won't need to translate any more. Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between see and look?

see — your eyes notice something by themselves; look — you turn your eyes towards something on purpose. Before an object, look needs at: Look at this photo.

watch a film or see a film?

Both are correct. watch a film focuses on the activity of watching (often at home); see a film simply says you saw it (often at the cinema). For television, though, only watch TV works, although I saw it on TV is correct.

On a call: I can't hear you or I can't listen to you?

I can't hear you — your voice doesn't reach me (for example, the connection is bad). I can't listen to you means something else: I can't listen right now (I'm busy) or I can't listen any more (I'm tired of it).

listen music or listen to music?

listen to music. Before an object, listen needs to: listen to me, listen to a podcast.
