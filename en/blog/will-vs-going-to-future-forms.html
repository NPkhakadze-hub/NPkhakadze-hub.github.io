will, going to or Present Continuous — three ways to talk about the future 🔮

26 October 2026 · Nodar

Many languages get by with one future form, or simply use the present tense for the future. English has three main forms (plus a bonus one for timetables), and each one expresses a different attitude — not a different time.

💭 will — I've just decided / I think so
📋 be going to — I've already decided / I can see it's going to happen
📅 Present Continuous — it's arranged: I know the time and place
🚆 Bonus: Present Simple — a timetable that doesn't depend on me: The train leaves at 6.

will — a decision made right now

✅ — The phone is ringing. — I'll get it! — (decided just now)
✅ I think it will rain tomorrow. — (a prediction)
✅ I'll help you, don't worry. — a promise

After I think, I'm sure, probably, maybe, I hope you'll most often hear will — although going to is perfectly correct too (I think it's going to rain).

be going to — decided in advance

✅ I'm going to study medicine. — (I decided this a long time ago)
✅ We're going to buy a house next year.

And one more very natural use: when you can see the signs right in front of you.

✅ Look at those clouds — it's going to rain. — I can see the clouds → rain is coming.
✅ Careful! You're going to fall!

In the clouds example, will sounds odd: the evidence is right in front of you, so this is no longer just a guess. (In a warning, though, “Careful! You'll fall!” is natural too.)

Present Continuous — a fixed arrangement

✅ I'm seeing the doctor at 5. — I have an appointment.
✅ We're flying to Berlin on Monday. — The tickets are booked.

The difference from going to is small but real: I'm going to see the doctor = I intend to. I'm seeing the doctor at 5 = I've booked it, the time is fixed.

Both are possible — but the meaning changes

✅ — I don't understand this word.
   — I'll explain it. — I've only just realised what the problem is, so I'll explain.

✅ — Why did you open the dictionary?
   — I'm going to explain this word. — I'd already planned to.

The trap: if and when

When if or when introduces a condition or a time, English does not use a future form after it — even though the meaning is clearly about the future, and even if your own language uses a future tense there.

❌ If it will rain, I'll stay home.
✅ If it rains, I'll stay home.

❌ I'll call you when I will arrive.
✅ I'll call you when I arrive.

This is one of the most stubborn mistakes — and one of the most noticeable.

One exception: when when means “at what time” and starts an indirect question, will stays — I don't know when he will arrive. (The same goes for polite requests: If you'll just wait here, … — here will means “be willing to”.)

When you're making a prediction about the future and you're not sure, might or may is often more accurate than will: It might rain tomorrow. More on these verbs in the article on modal verbs.

Test yourself

1. — I'm cold. — I ___ (close) the window.
2. Look! The bus ___ (leave)!
3. I ___ (see) my dentist tomorrow at 10.
4. When you ___ (finish), call me.

Answers: 1. 'll close — decided just now. 2. is going to leave (or is leaving) — I can see it happening. 3. 'm seeing — it's arranged ('m going to see is also acceptable; will see is grammatically correct but sounds less natural for a fixed appointment). 4. finish (or have finished) — no will after when; use the present or the Present Perfect.

You won't learn all three future forms in one day — and you don't need to. Start with one: stop using a future form after if and when. That one habit will change the way your English sounds more than anything else here.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between will and going to?

Use will when you decide right now or make a prediction (I think it will rain). Use going to when you had already decided, or when you can clearly see that something is about to happen: Look at those clouds — it's going to rain.

Why is it “If it rains” and not “If it will rain”?

When if or when introduces a condition or a time, English does not use a future form after it: If it rains, I'll stay home. But when when means “at what time” and starts an indirect question, will stays: I don't know when he will arrive.

When do we use the Present Continuous for the future?

When something is already arranged and the time is known: I'm seeing the doctor at 5. We're flying to Berlin on Monday. The difference from going to is small: I'm going to see the doctor = I intend to; I'm seeing the doctor at 5 = I have an appointment.
