Learning English after 40 — is it too late? An honest answer 🕰️

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“My brain is too old to learn” — I hear this a lot, and almost always in an apologetic tone. Someone is past forty, needs English and is afraid they've left it too late. If that's what you think too, let me tell you one thing right away: learning English after 40 isn't nearly as different as you imagine.

I'm not going to fill this article with reassuring phrases. Quite the opposite — I'll be honest about what really changes with age and what gets better, and then I'll suggest one simple plan that suits an adult learner.

The short answer: it's not too late

Learning English as an adult is possible — and that's neither my optimism nor an advertising slogan. People learn new languages at any age; this is well known in linguistics too. The famous “critical period”, the reason children supposedly learn better, mainly concerns one thing: accent. Vocabulary, grammar and the ability to hold a conversation don't shut down because of age.

What you call “my brain doesn't learn any more” is often something else entirely: tiredness after a working day, an unpleasant experience at school, or simply the wrong materials. I'll look at each of these three separately below.

What really changes with age

Accent. English learned after 40 almost always comes with an accent — and that's normal. The goal is to be understood, not to be taken for a Londoner. Your Indian, German or Brazilian colleague speaks English with an accent and doesn't apologise to anyone for it.

You get tired faster. In the evening, after a full day's work, taking in a new rule really is hard. That isn't your brain “breaking down”; it's ordinary tiredness — something to plan around, not something to be ashamed of.

You feel more embarrassed. A twenty-year-old student laughs off a mistake; a forty-five-year-old doctor or accountant feels awkward saying I go yesterday. This is the most real obstacle of all — but it's about pride, not your brain. I've written about it separately: the fear of speaking and what helps.

The traces of school. This isn't about age, but after forty it runs deeper: “English just isn't for me” is often a single sentence from a school teacher, said twenty-five years ago. Back then you memorised rules and got called up to the board; now you speak and correct mistakes on the spot. It's a different method, and that school experience has no power here any more.

What gets better after 40

Now the other side, which people talk about far less:

Discipline. An adult doesn't skip a lesson because they “didn't feel like it”. Once they've decided, they do it — and with a language, regularity is what decides everything.

A clear goal. A sixteen-year-old often doesn't know what they're learning for. You do: an email to a client, a visit to the doctor, a video call with your grandchild. A goal narrows down the material and speeds up learning.

Real-life vocabulary. You already know what an invoice, a diagnosis, a mortgage or a visa application is. In English you're only missing the word; the concept is already there. A child has to learn both at the same time.

Grammar as a system. An adult understands a rule explained once and sees the whole system: why it's I've been learning and not I'm learning once you add “for three months”. A child picks up the same thing through lots of repetition.

Strategies. You've already learned plenty in life — a profession, driving, new software at work. You know how you take notes, how you revise, and what time of day you're most productive. These skills will come in handy for learning English too.

Who usually starts after 40

Four situations I see often — you may recognise yourself in one of them:

👨‍👧 A parent whose child speaks better. My son speaks English better than me.
💼 A professional who needs it for their career. I'm learning English for my job.
✈️ Moving abroad to join family. I'm moving to Germany to be with my family.
🧳 Travelling independently. I'd like to travel without a translator.

In each of these four cases the first lessons start differently: the parent needs vocabulary for school and video calls, the professional needs emails and meetings, the person moving abroad needs English for visits to the doctor and government offices. That's exactly why I start with the goal, not with page one of the textbook.

A plan that suits adults

1. One goal. Not “I want English”, but “I need to write a reply to a client”. Everything else comes later.

2. Short but frequent sessions. 15–20 minutes a day gives you far more than three hours in one go on a Saturday. A tired brain copes much better with small doses.

3. Speaking from day one. Three sentences about yourself — out loud, with mistakes. “First I'll learn the grammar, then I'll speak” — that “then” usually never comes.

4. The words of your own life. Instead of the “fruit and vegetables” list — the words of your profession, your city, your family. What's part of your life is easier to remember.

5. Accent — accepted. Speaking so that people understand you — yes; getting rid of your accent completely — no. The energy you'd spend on your accent is better spent on vocabulary.

6. Materials for adults. A colourful textbook full of bunnies shouldn't be teaching a forty-five-year-old. The topics should match your age and your interests.

Phrases for the first week

These phrases are easy even for a beginner and will be useful in your very first conversation:

🗣️ About yourself:
I started learning English three months ago.
I've been learning for three months.
I'm a beginner, but I understand a little.
🆘 When you didn't understand:
Could you repeat that, please?
Could you speak more slowly, please?
Sorry, I don't understand. What does that mean?
🛒 Everyday errands:
I have a question about my order.
Is there a pharmacy near here?
Can I pay by card?

One detail from the phrases above: in many languages you'd say this with the present tense, but in English it's I've been learning for three months, not I'm learning for three months. When something started in the past and is still going on (for three months up to now), English uses the Present Perfect Continuous instead of the present — and this is the kind of rule an adult remembers after hearing it explained once.

Test yourself

Choose the correct option in brackets:

1. I'm learning English ___ my job. (for / to)
2. Could you ___ that, please? (repeat / repeating)
3. I ___ been learning English for three months. ('ve / 'm)
4. Sorry, I ___ understand. Could you speak more slowly? (don't / not)
5. My son ___ English better than me. (speak / speaks)

Answers: 1. for. 2. repeat. 3. 've. 4. don't. 5. speaks.

One last piece of advice: not an hour of English tonight — ten minutes tomorrow morning. Age doesn't stop learning; putting it off does. Frequently asked questions

Is it too late to learn English after 40?

No. People learn new languages at any age. The so-called “critical period” mainly concerns accent — vocabulary, grammar and conversation skills don't shut down because of age.

What really changes with age?

Three things: an accent almost always stays, you get tired faster after a working day, and mistakes feel more embarrassing. None of these means your brain can't learn — they're details to plan around.

What advantages does an adult have over a child?

Discipline, a clear goal, real-life vocabulary (you already know the concepts, you're only missing the words), the ability to understand a rule as part of a system, and learning strategies you've already developed in life.

How do I start learning English after 40?

With one concrete goal, short and frequent sessions, speaking from day one and the words of your own life. The first step with me is a free 20-minute intro call on Google Meet.
