since or for? How to say how long you've been doing something 📅

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“You moved here five years ago and you still live here” — a simple idea, but to say it in English you have to make two decisions at once: which tense, and which word — since or for. This is exactly where a lot of people slip — not because the rule is hard, but because their own language handles this situation with a different logic.

The good news is that the rule fits in two lines. The less good news is that you may have to unlearn a habit from your own language.

since — from when, for — how long

This is the one rule you really need to remember:

🔑 since + a starting point — from when? From 2019, from Monday, from childhood
🔑 for + a length of time — how long? Five years, two hours, a whole life
since: since 2019 · since Monday · since 9 o'clock · since last summer · since I was a child · since we met
for: for five years · for two hours · for a long time · for ages · for a week · for my whole life

If you're not sure, ask yourself: is this a point on the calendar or a stretch of time? “2019” is a point → since. “Five years” is a stretch → for. After since you can also have a whole clause — since I was a child, since we met — that's a starting point too.

❌ I've lived here since five years.
✅ I've lived here for five years.
❌ I've lived here for 2019.
✅ I've lived here since 2019.

The main trap: the present tense

Think about how you'd say “I've lived here for five years”, “I've been waiting since this morning” or “I've known him since we were kids” in your own language. In many languages, all three use the present tense. That's logical: I still live here, I'm still waiting. So your instinct pushes you towards the Present Simple. In English, though, this is one of the most common mistakes of all:

❌ I live here for five years.
❌ I live here since 2019.
✅ I've lived here for five years.
✅ I've been living here since 2019.

English logic works like this: the action started in the past and is still going on — that's Present Perfect territory. The Present Simple only says what is generally true — I live in Tbilisi (a plain fact, full stop). When for/since measures a period that runs from the past up to now, the Present Simple no longer works. (With habits, of course, for is fine: I go to the gym for an hour every day — that's how long each visit lasts, not “up to now”.)

(How the Present Simple differs from the other present tense is covered in a separate article — Present Simple and Present Continuous.)

Present Perfect Simple or Continuous? With verbs like live, work, study, both are correct: I've lived / I've been living here for five years. The Continuous puts the focus on the process — I've been waiting since 9, She's been working here for two years.

With state verbs (know, be, like), only the Simple works: I've known her since 2019, not I've been knowing. American and British English don't differ here — with for/since both use the Present Perfect.

for works with the Past Simple too

for isn't a “Present Perfect word” — it simply expresses a length of time, and finished actions have a length too:

✅ I lived in Batumi for five years. — I don't live there anymore: it's over.
✅ I've lived in Batumi for five years. — I still live there.

The difference is whether it's still going on. A quick test helps: can you add “and I still do”? I lived there = it's finished → Past Simple; I've lived here for five years = still true → Present Perfect. A full comparison of these two tenses has its own article — Present Perfect or Past Simple.

since — almost always with perfect tenses

since is a different story. Because it names a starting point and implies “from then until now”, it almost always goes with the Present Perfect (or the Past Perfect). I lived here since 2019 is wrong: if it's from 2019 until now — I've lived; if it's over — say how long it lasted: I lived here for three years.

❌ I lived here since 2019.
✅ I've lived here since 2019.

Two cases that might confuse you. First: a clause after since is usually in the Past Simple, because it names a point — I've known her since we met (we met — and I've known her ever since). Sometimes you'll also see the Present Perfect: since I've been in London.

Second: phrases like “It's ages since…” — It's ages since I saw him / It's been ages since I saw him (“I haven't seen him for ages”). Here the main verb is is or has been, and after since comes the Past Simple. This is perfectly natural English — just remember it separately.

“How long…?” — the question that ties it all together

To ask about duration up to now, English starts with How long…? And since the question is about something still going on, the Present Perfect is a must here too:

❌ How long do you live here?
✅ How long have you lived here?
✅ How long have you been learning English?

And the answer is exactly the two options we started with: For three years. or Since 2023. — both correct; one gives a stretch, the other a point.

ago — the third word people mix up

ago means “before now” and tells you when something happened — in other words, it names a point in the past. That's why it goes with the Past Simple and comes after the length of time, not before it: five years ago.

❌ I've moved here five years ago.
✅ I moved here five years ago. — a one-off fact in the past.
✅ I've lived here for five years.
✅ I've lived here since 2021.

Three sentences, one story — from three angles:

🔑 ago — when? (“… before now”, Past Simple): five years ago
🔑 for — how long? (length of time): for five years
🔑 since — from when? (starting point): since 2021

Test yourself

Fill in since, for or ago, and where there's a verb in brackets, put it in the correct form. Answers below.

1. I've known Nino ______ we were at school together.
2. They ______ (be) married for twenty years and they're still very happy together.
3. We moved to Kutaisi three years ______.
4. How long ______ you ______ (know) each other? — Since university.
5. I ______ (study) German for two years when I was at school, but I've forgotten everything.
6. She's been waiting for the bus ______ half an hour and it still hasn't come.

Answers: 1. since — “we were at school together” is the starting point. 2. have been — twenty years, and they're still together. 3. ago — “three years before now”, after the length of time.

4. have … known — know is a state verb, so the Continuous won't work. 5. studied — the school years are over → Past Simple, despite for two years. 6. for — “half an hour” is a stretch of time.

One practical tip: when you're about to say how long something has been going on, pause for a second and choose the tense first — if it's still going on, Present Perfect — and only then the word: a point → since, a stretch → for. That short pause is exactly where the old habit gets replaced by the new one.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between since and for?

since names the starting point, from when: since 2019, since Monday, since we met. for gives the length of time, how long: for five years, for two hours. A helpful question: is it a point on the calendar or a stretch of time?

How do I say that I started living here five years ago and still do?

I have lived here for five years, or I have been living here for five years. Many languages use the present tense here, but English uses the Present Perfect, because the action started in the past and is still going on. "I live here for five years" is a mistake.

Is for only used with the Present Perfect?

No. for simply expresses a length of time and also works with the Past Simple when the period is over: I lived in Batumi for five years (I don't live there anymore). since, however, almost always goes with perfect tenses; the exception is phrases like It's ages since I saw him.

How do I ask how long something has been going on?

How long have you…? For example: How long have you lived here? / How long have you been learning English? Answer with for or since: For three years. / Since 2023. "How long do you live here?" is wrong in this meaning.
