English levels A1–C2 — what they mean and which one you’re at 🎯

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“What’s my level?” — almost everyone asks me this in our very first conversation. And they often bring the answer with them: “probably zero”, “school level”, “intermediate, but I can’t speak”. The problem is that these words mean nothing — not to you, not to an employer, not to a university.

That’s exactly why the Common European Framework, CEFR, exists — six levels from A1 to C2. Here I’ll explain what each one means in practice, which level the most common goals require, and how to work out where you stand.

Why it’s better to think in levels

The CEFR doesn’t measure how many rules you know, but what you can do with the language: whether you can book a hotel, write an email, defend your opinion in an argument. That’s why the same label (for example B1) is understood the same way in Tbilisi, Berlin and London — in job ads, university requirements and language certificates.

There are three stages, each with two levels: A — basic user, B — independent user, C — proficient user.

A1 and A2 — the basics

A1 (beginner). You introduce yourself, say where you’re from and what you do, buy a coffee, ask for directions — as long as the other person speaks slowly and simply. Your sentences are short and memorised.

✅ I'm Nino. I'm from Georgia. I work in a bank.
✅ How much is this?

A2 (elementary). Everyday topics are already yours: family, work, shopping, plans. You talk about the past and the future, but still with simple structures, and in a long conversation you lose the thread.

✅ Yesterday I went to the market and bought some fruit.
✅ Next week we're going to visit my parents.

B1 and B2 — the independent user

B1 (intermediate). The most important threshold. You manage on your own when travelling, understand and answer work emails, tell a colleague abroad about a project. You watch a film with subtitles and get the main points. You speak, though sometimes you search for words and make mistakes — but that doesn’t get in the way of understanding.

✅ I think we should change the plan, because the client needs it earlier.
✅ Could you tell me what time the last bus leaves?

B2 (upper-intermediate). This is where English becomes a working tool. In a meeting you discuss and argue freely, you write a report or a cover letter, and you understand a university lecture mostly on your own. A conversation with a native speaker feels natural for both sides — they no longer have to speak slowly and simply for you.

✅ Although the first option is cheaper, I'd still go with the second one — it'll save us time in the long run.

C1 and C2 — proficiency

C1 (advanced). You sense the nuances: what’s formal, what’s friendly, where there’s irony. You read complex professional texts, write structured analysis, and in an academic setting you don’t just understand — you take part.

C2 (mastery). Close to the level of an educated native speaker: humour, wordplay, specialist terminology — all without trouble. It’s the highest exam level and is rarely needed for everyday goals.

✅ I wouldn't say the talk was bad — it just didn't quite live up to the hype. — i.e. it wasn’t bad, but it wasn’t as good as everyone said it would be.

Which level your goal needs

Travel — A2 is already enough for hotels, restaurants and transport; with B1 you won’t get lost even in an unfamiliar situation.

A job abroad or at an international company — B2 is often required: meetings, emails, job interviews. For some positions B1 is enough, especially where communication isn’t the main part of the job.

University on an English-taught programme — usually B2, for some programmes C1. The exact requirement is set by the specific university, often as an IELTS score or a score in a similar exam.

Emigration — countries have different rules, and some set a specific level for a visa or citizenship (often B1). Rules change, so always check an official source. In everyday life, though — at the doctor’s, at the bank, at your child’s school — English for emigrants at B1 really does the job.

How long one level takes

By the international rule of thumb, one level takes roughly 150–200 hours, and more at the higher levels. Check yourself

Tick the points you could do today, without preparation. Be honest — nobody will see this.

1. I watch a film with English subtitles and follow the plot, even if I don’t understand every word.
2. I write a work or hotel email on my own — without a translation app.
3. I can tell what happened yesterday and what I’m planning for tomorrow — in both the past and the future.
4. I can back up my opinion: I think… because…
5. I manage in a foreign country: a ticket, a room, a complaint in a restaurant.
6. When I meet an unknown word, I guess it from context and don’t stop the conversation.

Answers: 0–1 points — A1–A2, the foundation is still being built. 2–3 — A2, the transition towards B1. 4+ — signs of B1. And if you do all six freely, without “searching for every word”, and can argue too — that’s already heading towards B2.

This is a guide, not a test: often reading is at B2 and speaking at A2 — and both are true at the same time. You can check your grammar level in a 20-question test. Frequently asked questions

What do A1, A2, B1 and B2 mean?

They are the levels of the European CEFR framework. A1–A2 — the basics: introducing yourself, everyday topics. B1 — you manage on your own when travelling and with work emails. B2 — you discuss freely and work in an English-speaking environment. C1–C2 — proficient use with nuances.

What level do I need for a job abroad?

Often B2 — meetings, emails, interviews. For some positions where communication isn't the main part of the job, B1 is enough. Universities usually ask for B2, some programmes for C1.

How can I find out my level?

Check what you can actually do: write an email, follow a film with subtitles, back up your opinion. Reading and speaking are often at different levels, so the most accurate answer comes from a live conversation — for example, a free 20-minute intro call on Google Meet.

How long does one level take?

By the international rule of thumb, roughly 150–200 hours, more at the higher levels. The real time depends on how regularly you study and how much you work between lessons.
