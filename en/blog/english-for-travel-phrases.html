English for travel — 38 phrases from the airport to the restaurant ✈️

26 October 2026 · Nodar

The ticket is bought, the hotel is booked, the suitcase is packed. But one fear is still there: “What if they ask me something at passport control? Or what if I can't understand the waiter at the restaurant?” That fear is understandable, but for travelling you need far less English than you think.

When you travel, almost every situation follows the same script: the same questions, the same answers, the same polite formulas. Below I've sorted the phrases by situation — from the airport to emergencies — with short notes where they help.

One formula that works everywhere

In many languages, “Give me a coffee” sounds perfectly ordinary. In English, though, Give me… and I want… come across as blunt, almost like an order, when you say them to a stranger — especially to someone serving you. Grammatically these sentences are correct, so I don't mark them with ❌ — but the tone is wrong.

⚠️ Give me a coffee. — correct, but it sounds rude.
✅ Could I have a coffee, please?
⚠️ I want the chicken. — sounds childish or demanding.
✅ I'd like the chicken, please.
✅ I'll have the chicken, please. — the most natural form when ordering.

Remember three patterns — Could I…?, Could you…? and I'd like… — and the word please. With these four elements you can build a large part of your travel phrases yourself.

The airport and passport control

At passport control the questions are short, and your answers should be short too. No long explanations are needed: answer exactly what you're asked, and keep your documents (hotel booking, return ticket) to hand.

1. What's the purpose of your visit? — Tourism. I'm here on holiday.
2. How long are you staying? — Ten days.
3. Where are you staying? — At a hotel in the city centre.
4. Is this your first visit? — Yes, it is.
5. Do you have a return ticket? — Yes, here it is.
6. Anything to declare? — No, nothing to declare. — at customs.

Note: for question 2, Ten days is perfectly enough — you don't need a full sentence. An American would say vacation instead of holiday: I'm here on vacation.

At the hotel

7. I have a reservation under the name Beridze.
8. Is breakfast included?
9. What time is check-out? — when you have to leave the room.
10. Could I leave my luggage here until this evening?
11. What's the Wi-Fi password?
12. The air conditioning isn't working.

One trap: in Britain the ground floor is the floor at street level, and the first floor is the one above it. In America the first floor is the street-level floor. So if a British hotel tells you your room is on the first floor, it's one floor up from the entrance.

At a restaurant or café

13. A table for two, please.
14. Could we see the menu, please?
15. What do you recommend?
16. I'm allergic to nuts.
17. Could we have the bill, please? — in the US: the check
18. Can I pay by card?

A common mistake when ordering is using the present simple for a decision you're making right now:

❌ I take the fish.
✅ I'll have the fish, please.

A quick word on tipping (in English — a tip): in American restaurants leaving a tip is considered almost compulsory — it's an important part of the waiter's income. In Britain and many European countries the custom isn't so strict, and the bill often already includes a service charge. Check the bill before you pay, or ask: Is service included?

In town: directions, transport, shops

When you ask for directions, start with Excuse me — it's the polite way to get a stranger's attention.

19. Excuse me, how do I get to the train station?
20. Is it within walking distance? — can I walk there?
21. Which bus goes to the airport?
22. A return ticket to Oxford, please. — there and back.
23. Could you tell me when to get off?

In a shop, the most useful phrase is the one that politely tells the shop assistant you don't need help yet:

24. I'm just looking, thanks.
25. Do you have this in a smaller size?
26. Can I try it on?
27. How much is it?
❌ How much it costs?
✅ How much is it? / How much does it cost? — a question needs an auxiliary verb or is.
❌ Where is toilet?
✅ Excuse me, where's the toilet? — in the US: Where's the restroom?

Emergencies

Your suitcase hasn't arrived, you've lost your wallet, you're feeling unwell — these are the moments when you need phrases most and find them hardest to remember. So learn this part in advance.

28. My suitcase hasn't arrived.
29. Could I have a reference number, please? — at the lost-luggage desk.
30. I've lost my passport.
31. My wallet has been stolen.
32. Where's the nearest police station?
33. I'd like to report a theft.
34. I need to see a doctor.
35. Where's the nearest pharmacy?
36. Could you call an ambulance?

Emergency numbers: 112 in EU countries, 999 in the UK (112 works too), 911 in the USA and Canada.

And two phrases that will rescue you in any difficult conversation:

37. Could you speak more slowly, please?
38. Could you write it down for me?

If you've forgotten the word you need, describe it: the thing you use to…. I've collected phrases like this in the article “I've forgotten the word”.

British or American word?

People will understand most of these words in both countries, but the local version sounds more natural. One exception: in Britain a subway is a pedestrian underpass, and in London the metro is called the Underground or the Tube.

UK bill — US check — in a restaurant
UK toilet — US restroom, bathroom
UK lift — US elevator
UK queue — US line — people waiting one behind the other
UK single / return — US one-way / round-trip — ticket one way / there and back
UK underground — US subway — the metro
UK baggage reclaim — US baggage claim — where you collect your bags at the airport

Living in another country for a longer time — registering with a doctor, dealing with offices, work — calls for different vocabulary; I cover it in the article “English for emigrants”. If you want to strike up a conversation with a fellow traveller or another hotel guest, small talk phrases will come in handy.

Test yourself

Choose the correct or most natural option. Only one answer is correct in each item.

1. You ask the waiter for the bill: (a) Give me the bill. (b) Could we have the bill, please?
2. Officer: "What's the purpose of your visit?" — You: ___ (a) Tourism. I'm here on holiday. (b) Ten days.
3. Excuse me, ___ do I get to the museum? (a) how (b) where
4. I'm coming back tomorrow, so I'd like a ___ ticket to Oxford, please. (a) single (b) return
5. Shop assistant: "Can I help you?" — You don't need help yet: (a) I'm just looking, thanks. (b) No, I don't want.
6. How much ___ this jacket? (a) is (b) costs

Answers: 1. (b) — Give me sounds blunt. 2. (a) — “Ten days” answers a different question (How long are you staying?). 3. (a) — how do I get to is a fixed formula. 4. (b) — a return is a ticket there and back. 5. (a) — want needs an object here (I don't want anything), and an answer like that sounds blunt anyway. 6. (a) — How much costs this jacket? is wrong; the correct forms are How much is this jacket? or How much does this jacket cost?

One practical tip: before you leave, pick the ten phrases from this list that you'll need most and say them out loud a few times — imagine you're standing in front of the waiter or the border officer. A phrase you have already said once comes back much more easily when you need it. Frequently asked questions

What do they ask at passport control in English?

Most often: What's the purpose of your visit?, How long are you staying?, Where are you staying? and Do you have a return ticket? Keep your answer short and precise, for example: Tourism. I'm here on holiday.

Why does “I want a coffee” sound rude?

Grammatically the sentence is correct, but said to a stranger, especially to someone serving you, it comes across as demanding. The natural forms are Could I have a coffee, please? or I'd like a coffee, please.

How do I ask for the bill in a restaurant in English?

Could we have the bill, please? In America people say check instead of bill: Could we have the check, please?

What should I say if my luggage hasn't arrived?

At the lost-luggage desk say: My suitcase hasn't arrived. At the end, ask for a reference number too: Could I have a reference number, please?
