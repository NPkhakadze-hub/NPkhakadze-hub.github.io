How long does it take to learn English — realistic timelines by level 📈

26 October 2026 · Nodar

This is the first question almost everyone asks me. The honest answer: it depends on three things — where you start, where you want to get to, and how much you work between lessons.

In this article
  1. What the levels mean
  2. Rough timelines
  3. Why progress often stalls at B1

What the levels mean

A1 — I can introduce myself, order a coffee, make simple sentences.
A2 — I can talk about everyday topics, the past and the future.
B1 — I can manage when travelling, handle work emails, follow a film with subtitles.
B2 — I can discuss things freely and work in an English-speaking environment.
C1 — I can feel the nuances, argue a point, write professionally.

Rough timelines

According to international estimates (for example, Cambridge's guidance), one level takes roughly 150–200 hours (more at the higher levels). For example, three hours of lessons a week = about 12 hours a month, plus your own work.

If you also study on your own for 3–4 hours a week alongside your lessons (about 25–30 hours a month in total), the picture looks roughly like this:

📈 From zero to A2 — about 10–14 months (A1 and A2 both go relatively fast)
📈 A2 → B1 — 6–8 months
📈 B1 → B2 — 8–12 months (this is where progress tends to stall)
📈 B2 → C1 — a year or more (the longest stretch)

These figures are averages, not a guarantee. I've seen a student go from B1 to B2 in a few months — and others who needed more than a year. The difference almost always comes down to one thing: how regularly they work.

Why progress often stalls at B1

This is the stage where people most often give up. The reason makes sense: from A1 to B1, every new rule shows up in your results straight away. After B1, progress becomes “invisible” — you already understand and speak, just not very accurately.

That's exactly why measurable milestones matter at this stage — we regularly note where you've improved and what we'll work on next.

What speeds things up

Regularity > intensity. Two lessons a week for three months give you far more than a two-week daily “marathon”.

English in your daily life. Your phone in English, series with subtitles, a podcast on the way to work. An extra 20 minutes a day adds up to 10 hours a month.

A precise goal. “Learning English” is vague. “Passing a job interview in English in six months” is measurable — and you can build a programme around it.

What if I learned English at school?

That almost always means you already have a foundation — it's just “asleep”. Students like this usually move forward much faster than they expect: the first two or three weeks are often more remembering than learning.

Frequently asked questions

How long does one level take?

International estimates put one level at roughly 150–200 hours (more at the higher levels). Three hours of lessons a week is about 12 hours a month, so your real pace also depends on how much you work on your own. With lessons alone, it takes noticeably longer.

Why does progress stall at B1?

Up to B1, every new rule shows up in your results straight away. After B1, progress becomes “invisible” — you already understand and speak, just less accurately. That's why you need measurable milestones at this stage.

I learned English at school — will I progress faster?

Usually yes — you already have a foundation, it's just “asleep”, and the first two or three weeks are often more remembering than learning. The exception is ingrained mistakes (I'm agree, in Monday) — fixing them takes separate work.
