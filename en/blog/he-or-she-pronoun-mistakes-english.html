He or she? Why learners mix up gender in English pronouns 👫

26 October 2026 · Nodar

My mother is a teacher. He works at a school. — I hear this sentence in my lessons so often that it no longer surprises me. And it's not just beginners who say it — often it's a student who already uses the Present Perfect with ease.

If this happens to you too, know one thing first: it doesn't mean you don't know he and she. You do, and you know them well. The cause lies somewhere else entirely — in your first language — and that's exactly why it gets fixed with specific practice, not by learning the rule all over again.

Why this is hard for so many learners

Many languages don't mark gender in their pronouns at all. One word covers all three: he, she and it. Often the possessive is also a single word for his, her and its. “That person is a doctor” — a woman or a man? In those languages you don't even need to say: you work it out from context, or it simply doesn't come up. Other languages do have “he” and “she” — but they also give a grammatical gender to things, so a table or a laptop can be “he” too.

In English, though, you have to make this decision on every pronoun, in a fraction of a second: who am I talking about — a man or a woman, or a thing? A brain that thinks in a language without this distinction never asks the question, so the form that happens to be closest often slips out.

The table to learn once

  • Subject: he (a man) · she (a woman) · it (a thing, an animal, something abstract)
  • Possessive: his · her · its — belonging to him / her / it
  • Object: him · her · it — the person or thing the action is done to
  • Reflexive: himself · herself · itself

Notice two things. First, her does two jobs — possessive and object: I saw her. This is her bag. For a man the two forms are different: I saw him. This is his bag. Second, its is written without an apostrophe; it's is something else entirely, the short form of it is.

Mistake 1: mother, sister, wife — and still he

❌ My sister lives in Berlin. He is a nurse.
✅ My sister lives in Berlin. She is a nurse.

❌ My wife is a lawyer and he works a lot.
✅ My wife is a lawyer and she works a lot.

This is the most common version and the strangest one too: surely it's obvious that a mother is a woman? Of course it is — but if your language has one word for “he” and “she”, “mother” is linked in your head to that one genderless pronoun. The sentence ends with whichever pronoun comes out first. An English-speaking listener notices immediately, although they usually work out who you mean.

Mistake 2: his or her — whose it is, not what it is

In English, his/her follows the owner, not the thing that belongs to them. Giorgi's sister is his sister, because Giorgi is a man; the fact that a sister is female has no effect on the pronoun.

⚠️ Giorgi loves her sister. — grammatically correct, but it means something else: Giorgi loves another woman's sister.
✅ Giorgi loves his sister. — Giorgi loves his own sister.

⚠️ Nino is here with his husband. — this means something else too: Nino is here with some man's husband.
✅ Nino is here with her husband. — Nino is here with her own husband.

A simple check: first ask “whose is it?” — if the answer is a man, his; if it's a woman, her. In some languages the possessive agrees with the thing that is owned, or one word means “his own / her own” for everyone — so your instinct gives you no clue here. That's exactly why this mistake still turns up at B2 level.

Mistake 3: it for people, he/she for things

❌ This is my new colleague, Tamar. It is very friendly.
✅ This is my new colleague, Tamar. She is very friendly.

❌ I have a new laptop. He is very fast.
✅ I have a new laptop. It is very fast.

In English you use he or she for people (even small children, if you know their sex; more on they below) and it for things. If your language gives every noun a grammatical gender, “the laptop… he” can feel completely natural — but in English the grammar of your own language doesn't count: a thing is it. The exceptions are few and mostly stylistic: sailors call a ship she, and some people do the same with their car, but that's emotional colouring, not a rule. An animal is it if you don't know its sex; your own dog or cat, though, is almost always he or she.

they for one person — when it works and when it doesn't

In modern English they is also used for one person, and that's completely normal when the person's gender is unknown or doesn't matter: Someone called — they didn't leave a name. If a student is late, they should email me. It's also used for people who ask to be called they. This isn't new — singular they has existed in English for centuries.

But they is not a “safe way out” when you keep mixing up he and she. If you're talking about your mother and say they, the listener will be confused — they'll think you mean several people.

❌ My mother is a doctor. They work at a hospital. — the listener thinks you're talking about other people too.
✅ My mother is a doctor. She works at a hospital.
✅ Someone left their umbrella here. — I don't know who.

Why it happens — and why it isn't a lack of knowledge

When you speak, your brain is doing several things at once: searching for words, choosing a tense, building the sentence. If your first language doesn't distinguish he and she (or treats things as he or she), one more step is added — one you never take in your own language: deciding on the right gender for every pronoun. When your attention is on something else (a new word, tricky grammar, fear of speaking), that step is the first to go.

That's why this mistake is rare in a written test but common in conversation. And that's why rereading the rule doesn't help: you already know the rule. What you need is for the choice to become automatic.

How to practise — four steps

1. Attach the pronoun to the person, not to the rule. When you talk about someone in English for the first time, say the pronoun right after the name: my mother — she, Levan — he, my boss — she. Out loud, three times. That way the gender isn't decided while you're building the sentence — it's already “attached”.

2. Family stories out loud. Pick five people close to you, say three sentences about each one and pause for a second on every pronoun: My sister is 30. She lives in Kutaisi. Her husband is an engineer. This exercise takes two or three minutes and trains exactly the step that gets lost in conversation.

3. Monitored conversation. For one week, watch out for this mistake only — let everything else go for now. When you notice it, don't get flustered — just repeat the sentence correctly and carry on. Ask your teacher or conversation partner to correct only your he/she that week.

4. Reading aloud. Take a simple text with lots of characters and read it aloud. When you read, the choice has already been made — you just say the correct form and train your ear to hear how she… her… herself sounds for one person.

Test yourself

Fill in the correct pronoun:

1. My mother is a nurse. ___ works night shifts.
2. Giorgi has one sister. ___ name is Nino.
3. I bought a new phone last week, but ___ stopped working yesterday.
4. My brother wasn't careful and cut ___ while he was cooking.
5. Someone phoned for you, but ___ didn't leave a name — I don't know if it was a man or a woman.

Answers: 1. She — a mother is a woman. 2. Her — it's the sister's name, not Giorgi's. 3. it — a phone is a thing. 4. himself — a brother. 5. they — the person's gender is unknown (he or she is also correct, but sounds heavy).

This is one of those rare mistakes you can't fix with a book — only by speaking. So start today: pick one person, say three sentences about them out loud and pause on every pronoun. Frequently asked questions

Why do I say "he" about my mother or sister when I know perfectly well she's a woman?

Many languages use one pronoun for he, she and it, and others give a grammatical gender even to things. In English you have to choose the gender on every pronoun, and when your attention is on something else in conversation, that step is the first to go. It isn't a lack of knowledge, it's a question of automaticity.

his or her: which one when?

It follows the owner, not the thing: Giorgi's sister is his sister, Nino's husband is her husband. First ask "whose is it?": if it belongs to a man, his; if it belongs to a woman, her.

Can I use "they" for one person?

Yes, when the person's gender is unknown or doesn't matter (Someone called — they didn't leave a name), or when someone asks to be called they. But they doesn't work for your mother or brother: the listener will think you mean several people.

How do I fix my he/she mistakes when speaking?

Attach the pronoun to the person: say "my mother — she" out loud along with any new name. Talk about your family out loud and pause on every pronoun, watch for only this one mistake for a week, and read texts aloud.
