English for medical professionals — what doctors and nurses really need 🩺

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“I read journal articles in English easily, but when a foreign patient walked in, I couldn't say a word” — I've heard this from doctors many times. Nurses tell a different version: “I want to work in the UK, they told me about the OET, and I don't know where to start.” In both cases the question is the same: what is English for medical professionals, and how is it different from ordinary English?

In this article I'm speaking as a language teacher — I'm not a doctor and I'm not giving medical advice. But working with doctors and nurses, I've seen one thing clearly: the hardest part of medical English is not what everyone expects.

Where medical professionals actually need English

“Medical English” isn't one abstract skill. It's four specific situations, and each one asks for something different.

  • Literature and guidelines. UpToDate, PubMed articles, recommendations from professional associations — almost everything is in English. Here you need reading skills and terminology; speaking hardly at all.
  • Conferences and presentations. Giving a talk, answering questions, chatting with colleagues during the coffee break. Here you need confident speaking — and, as people often forget, small talk too.
  • Foreign patients and colleagues. A tourist who ends up in A&E; a patient at an international clinic; a foreign colleague at an online case conference. This is the most unpredictable situation — and the hardest.
  • Exams and emigration. OET, PLAB, USMLE, Germany — more on this below, because this is where most of the confusion lies.
✍️ Test yourself: which of these four do you need right now, this year? When did you last read a medical article in English without a dictionary? Could you explain to a patient in English why they need a test? The preparation plans for reading and for talking to patients look almost nothing alike — your answer changes the whole programme.

What English for medical professionals is made of

If we put it as a formula, medical English has three layers:

  • General English at B1–B2 level. Grammar, asking questions, tenses, everyday vocabulary. Without this, the terms are left hanging in the air.
  • Terminology. Hypertension, myocardial infarction, prescription, dosage — the professional words.
  • Phrases for talking to patients. How to ask where it hurts; how to explain why a test is needed; how to reassure.

And here's the main thing that tends to surprise doctors: terminology is the easiest part. You already know the Latin and Greek roots — tachycardia, gastritis, analgesic look almost the same in many languages. The pronunciation and spelling of these words fall into place relatively easily.

What's hard is the thing no textbook teaches properly — the patient's everyday language. A patient won't tell you I have vertigo; they'll say I feel dizzy or my head is spinning. A child's parent will call the abdomen a tummy and numbness pins and needles, and I've been feeling off lately can mean all sorts of different complaints.

That's why a doctor's study plan is often surprising: less time goes on terminology than on “ordinary” conversational English. That isn't a gap — it's the right priority.

Phrases between doctor and patient

Below are the phrases you need in the first five minutes with a patient. Notice how simple they are — and how natural.

What brings you in today?
Where exactly does it hurt?
How long have you had this?
Are you on any medication?
Do you have any allergies?
I'd like to run a few tests.
You'll need to fast before the blood test.
Take one tablet twice a day with food.
I'm going to refer you to a specialist.
Let me know if it gets worse.

And two phrases from the other side — this is how a patient talks, not a textbook:

I feel dizzy when I stand up.
I've had this dull pain in my lower back for about a week.

Notice: there's not a single “difficult” term in any of these phrases. This is B1-level grammar — have you had, you'll need to, let me know.

Typical mistakes medical professionals make in English

These mistakes don't appear because your English is poor — they're direct translations from your own language (or from another language you learned earlier).

“Analysis” ≠ blood test. In many languages — French, Spanish and Russian, for example — a blood test is literally an “analysis”. In English it's a blood test, and the results are test results or lab results. Analysis means analysing data or a situation.

❌ We need to make an analysis of your blood.
✅ We need to do a blood test.

“Cabinet” ≠ office. In some languages — Russian and Polish, for example — a doctor's consulting room is a “cabinet”. In English a cabinet is a cupboard (or a cabinet of ministers). A doctor's room is an office; in the UK, a GP's practice is a surgery, and the room itself a consulting room.

❌ Please wait near my cabinet.
✅ Please wait outside my office.

Say or tell. Tell is almost always followed by a person (tell me, tell the patient); say isn't. I have a separate article on this.

❌ The doctor said me to rest.
✅ The doctor told me to rest.

Word order in questions. Many languages form a question like “Where it hurts?” without any auxiliary verb; in English, questions with “where / how long / when” need an auxiliary — does/did/have — unless the verb is be: Where does it hurt?

❌ Where it hurts? / How long you have this pain?
✅ Where does it hurt? / How long have you had this pain?

Medicaments. The word exists, but you hardly ever hear it in real English; doctors and patients alike say medication, medicine or simply tablets.

⚠️ Are you taking any medicaments?
✅ Are you taking any medication?

Exams and emigration: OET, PLAB, USMLE and Germany

This is where there's the most confusion, so I'll keep it short and careful. These are pointers — always check the exact requirements on the official website of the specific country and organisation, because they change.

  • OET (Occupational English Test) — an English exam made specifically for healthcare professionals: each profession — doctors, nurses, dentists, pharmacists — has its own writing and speaking parts, while listening and reading are shared. There are four parts — listening, reading, writing and speaking; in writing you usually write a referral letter, and in speaking you act out a role-play with a patient. It's accepted in the UK, Ireland, Australia and other countries, often as an alternative to IELTS.
  • PLAB (UK) — the licensing exam for doctors, taken in English. Before you can sit it, you usually also need proof of your English — via IELTS or OET.
  • USMLE (USA) — entirely in English, with no separate “language section”: the questions are clinical scenarios, so reading long texts quickly has a direct effect on your score. However, a foreign doctor also needs ECFMG certification for residency in the US, and that requires passing OET Medicine — check the exact requirements on the ECFMG website.
  • Germany — here the main thing isn't English but German: usually B2/C1 level and the Fachsprachprüfung, the medical language exam. You'll need English in Germany too, but for literature and conferences — and with foreign colleagues until your German is good enough.

One practical conclusion: if you're aiming for the UK or Ireland, OET is often a more logical choice than IELTS — it resembles your everyday work, and preparing for it is also preparing for the job. If it's Germany, think about German first and keep your English going alongside it.

How a lesson for a medical professional is built

You can't prepare a doctor with an ordinary textbook: a text about “going to the library” bores a doctor as quickly as a waiting room bores a patient. My lessons with medical professionals rest on three elements.

  • The case. The lesson starts with a teaching case: a 45-year-old man, chest pain, two days. You describe it in English, ask questions and put together a plan; I correct your language — the medicine is your business.
  • Role-play. I'm the patient — sometimes talkative, sometimes frightened, sometimes the kind who describes their symptoms vaguely. You take the history, explain, reassure. This is where the phrases we read above come up — but this time you're the one saying them.
  • A real abstract. We read the abstract of a recent article in your specialty: vocabulary, structure (background, methods, results, conclusion), a short summary for a colleague. This trains reading and conference speaking at the same time.

Homework follows the same logic: not “fill in the gaps”, but a two-minute voice recording of how you'd explain to a patient why a test is necessary; or a short referral letter to a colleague. That's exactly what you'll do at work — and in the OET.

Test yourself

Choose the correct option. Only one answer is correct in each.

1. We need to do a blood ___ before we decide. (analysis / test)
2. Please take a seat outside my ___. (cabinet / office)
3. The nurse ___ me to come back on Monday. (said / told)
4. How long ___ this cough? (you have / have you had)
5. I feel ___ when I stand up too quickly. (dizzy / dizziness)

Answers: 1. test. 2. office. 3. told. 4. have you had. 5. dizzy.

And one last piece of advice: this week, “play through” one ordinary consultation in English — out loud, in an empty consulting room. Wherever your tongue stumbles, that's your first topic to work on.

Frequently asked questions

What is medical English?

Three layers: general English at B1–B2 level, professional terminology and phrases for talking to patients. Terminology is usually the easiest part, since doctors already know the Latin and Greek roots; the difficulty is the patient's everyday language.

Does a doctor in Germany need English?

The main language there is German — usually B2/C1 level plus the Fachsprachprüfung. You need English for scientific literature, conferences and talking to foreign colleagues. Check the exact requirements on the official websites.

OET or IELTS?

OET is made specifically for healthcare professionals: the writing task is a referral letter and the speaking test is a role-play with a patient. It's accepted in the UK, Ireland, Australia and other countries, often as an alternative to IELTS. Check which one a specific organisation accepts on its website.

How does an online English lesson for a doctor work?

Three elements: a teaching case in English, a role-play (the teacher is the patient, you take the history) and reading a real abstract from your specialty. Homework is practical too — a voice recording explaining something to a patient, or a short referral letter.
