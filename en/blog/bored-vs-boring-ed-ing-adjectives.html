Bored or boring? -ed and -ing adjectives in English 🥱

6 November 2026 · Nodar

At the end of a lesson, a learner says: “Sorry, I'm a little boring today.” What they mean is “I'm a bit bored today”, but what they actually said is “Sorry, I'm a bit of a dull person today”. 😄

Bored and boring come from the same verb, but they mean different things. The same rule works for many other pairs: interested / interesting, tired / tiring, confused / confusing. Once you understand the rule, all the pairs fall into place at once.

The rule: -ed — how you feel, -ing — what causes it

The -ed ending describes a feeling that a person has. The -ing ending describes what causes that feeling — a person, a thing or a situation:

✅ I'm bored. — (my state)
✅ This film is boring. — (the film causes the boredom)
✅ I was bored because the lesson was boring.

A simple question to check: “What does this person feel?” → -ed. “What effect does this have on others?” → -ing.

Why I'm boring sounds funny

When you use the -ing form about a person, you describe the effect they have on other people. Sometimes that's a real compliment, sometimes an awkward confession:

❌ I'm very interesting in history. (they meant: history interests me)
✅ I'm very interested in history.
⚠️ He's interesting. — correct, but it means: “he's an interesting person” (to others).
⚠️ I'm boring. — grammatically correct, but it means: “I'm a dull person”.

In the last two examples the problem isn't the grammar, it's the meaning. That's why I marked them with ⚠️: the sentence is correct if that's exactly what you want to say.

The most common pairs

bored / boring — feeling no interest / dull
interested / interesting — wanting to know more / catching your attention
tired / tiring — needing rest / taking a lot of energy
excited / exciting — happily looking forward to something / thrilling
confused / confusing — not understanding / hard to understand
surprised / surprising — not expecting it / unexpected
embarrassed / embarrassing — feeling awkward or ashamed / making you feel awkward
worried / worrying — anxious / causing worry
disappointed / disappointing — sad that something wasn't as good as you hoped / not as good as you hoped
relaxed / relaxing — calm and at ease / making you calm

Examples from everyday conversation:

✅ The trip was long and tiring. I was very tired.
✅ These instructions are confusing.
✅ I'm so excited about the trip!
✅ The news is worrying. Everyone is worried.
✅ The hotel was disappointing. We were disappointed.
✅ A warm bath is very relaxing. After it I feel relaxed.

excited — happy anticipation, not fear

In many languages one word covers both pleasant anticipation and nervous worry before something important. In English these are two different words:

✅ I'm excited about my new job. — (I can't wait.)
✅ I'm nervous about the interview. — (I'm a bit scared.)
❌ I'm exciting about the exam tomorrow.
✅ I'm nervous about the exam tomorrow.

Before an exam or a visit to the doctor, excited sounds strange — as if you were looking forward to it. In situations like that, use nervous or worried.

embarrassed — not “pregnant”

Spanish speakers sometimes mix this word up, because in Spanish embarazada means pregnant. In English, embarrassed means feeling awkward or ashamed in front of others:

✅ I was so embarrassed when I forgot his name.
✅ That was an embarrassing moment.
✅ She's pregnant. — (expecting a baby.)

There are many traps like this. Words that look similar in two languages but mean different things are called “false friends”.

How to pronounce -ed

In these adjectives, -ed is pronounced in three ways, just like in past tense verbs:

/d/ — bored, worried, surprised
/t/ — relaxed, embarrassed
/ɪd/ — interested, excited, disappointed

You pronounce -ed as a separate syllable (/ɪd/, like “id”) only when the verb ends in a /t/ or /d/ sound: interest → interested, excite → excited. In all other cases -ed doesn't add a syllable: bored has just one syllable. I explain this rule in more detail in the article on English pronunciation.

Test yourself

1. I'm really ___ in learning Japanese. (interest)
2. The football match was ___. Nobody scored and I almost fell asleep. (bore)
3. After ten hours at work, I'm always ___. (tire)
4. She was ___ when she saw the price — it was much lower than she expected. (surprise)
5. This map is so ___. I can't find our hotel. (confuse)
6. My parents were ___ because I didn't call them all night. (worry)

Answers: 1. interested. 2. boring. 3. tired. 4. surprised. 5. confusing. 6. worried.

A tip: at the end of each day, describe in one sentence how you felt and what caused it: “I was tired because the meeting was tiring.” Your brain will soon get used to thinking in pairs. Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between bored and boring?

Bored describes how you feel (I'm bored — nothing interests me right now), while boring describes what causes that feeling (The film is boring — it's dull).

Is I'm interesting in music correct?

No. If music interests you, say I'm interested in music. Interesting describes the thing that interests you: Music is interesting.

Can I say I'm excited before an exam?

Excited means happy anticipation. If you're anxious or scared, it's better to say I'm nervous or I'm worried.
