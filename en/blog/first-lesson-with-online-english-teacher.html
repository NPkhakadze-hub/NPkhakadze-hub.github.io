Your first lesson with an online English teacher — what to expect and ask for 🧑‍🏫

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“I have my first lesson with an online teacher tomorrow and I don't know what to expect.” I hear this sentence a lot at intro calls. Finding an online English teacher seems easy: dozens of profiles, all of them “experienced”, all of them with “a personalised approach”. But a profile can't tell you how this person will work with you in particular.

That's why the first meeting is a test — not of you, but of the teacher. Below I'll set out what should happen at the intro call and in the first lesson, what you should come away with afterwards and which signs are red flags. By the way, in this article “teacher” and “tutor” mean the same thing — a private teacher who works only with you during the lesson.

What an intro call should look like

A good intro call is neither a lesson nor a sales pitch. It's twenty to thirty minutes during which the teacher does two things: assesses and listens.

Checking your level. A few questions in English, a short free conversation, sometimes a short text or two or three sentences to correct. It isn't an exam — the teacher simply needs to understand where you are right now: what you can do and what you find hard. If the teacher doesn't check your level and jumps straight to “Right, let's start on Monday”, that's the first red flag.

Listening to your goal. “I want English” isn't an answer. A good teacher will dig deeper: what for? Do you need to talk to foreign colleagues at work, are you preparing for IELTS, are you planning to move abroad, or do you simply want to understand films without subtitles? It's these answers that decide what you'll learn in the first month — and what you won't.

An honest assessment. At the end of the call, ask the teacher to tell you what they see: roughly what level you're at, what you'll need to work on most and what pace will suit you. One warning: if at this stage they promise you an exact deadline — “you'll reach B2 in four months” — that isn't honesty, it's sales. An honest answer sounds like this: “One level is roughly 150–200 hours of work; how long it takes to build up those hours depends on your pace.”

Many teachers offer a call like this for free. That's how it works with me too: a free 20-minute intro call on Google Meet — not a lesson, but exactly what I've described above.

What you should come away with after the first lesson

The first full lesson is usually a continuation of the intro call, but now with real work. When it's over, you should have three things in hand:

  • A plan. Not a two-page document, but at least two or three sentences: what we'll work on in the first month, in what order and why. “We'll go over grammar” isn't a plan. “First we'll sort out the Past Simple and the Present Perfect, because you mix them up when you speak, and alongside that, vocabulary for work” — that's a plan.
  • Materials. Whatever you worked on should stay with you as a link or a file. In the online format, “I left my notebook at home” doesn't exist — everything is on screen, and saving it takes a second.
  • Homework that gets corrected. Homework on its own doesn't mean much. What matters is what happens to it in the second lesson: does the teacher go through it, mark your mistakes and explain why they're mistakes? If homework ends with “I had a look, it's fine”, it's simply a waste of time.

This is what corrected homework should look like: the mistake, the correct version and one sentence on why.

Red flags in the very first week

Some things show up at the very first meeting, others after two or three lessons. These four signs are the ones students most often turn a blind eye to — and then lose months.

  • One textbook for everyone. If an accountant, a school leaver and someone preparing to move abroad all start from the same page of the same book, the “personalised approach” exists only in the profile.
  • The teacher talks, you listen. A lesson isn't a lecture. If the teacher spends four-fifths of the lesson explaining, you're left with only a fifth to speak. Most of the time should be yours (less so with a beginner, but even then the teacher shouldn't be talking the whole time).
  • Nobody corrects your mistakes. “The main thing is to speak” is a good principle — but only if the mistakes do get corrected afterwards. A mistake nobody points out turns into a habit.
  • “You'll be speaking fluently in three months.” Nobody can honestly make you that promise. The result depends on your time, your starting level and how regular you are — and none of those three is in the teacher's hands. The more exact the deadline and the more categorical the promise, the more reason to be careful.

And the other way round — two signs that you're in the right place: the teacher repeats your last sentence back to you in its corrected form, and at the end of the lesson asks you what was difficult.

What you should bring

The first meeting is a test for both sides. The more precisely you answer three questions, the better the teacher can help you. Prepare — two minutes is enough.

  • Your goal — as specific as possible. Not “I want English”, but “I need to write emails to clients on my own”, “I need IELTS 6.5 for a master's in Germany”, “I don't want to need an interpreter when I travel”. A specific goal leads to a specific plan.
  • Time per week — realistic. Apart from the lesson, how many minutes do you have for homework? Two lessons and three 20-minute sessions is one plan; one lesson and zero is quite another. A teacher can help you in both cases, but in different ways — if you tell them the truth.
  • What didn't work before. This is the most useful information and, strangely enough, it's what most often goes unsaid. “I started twice and gave up both times in the third month”, “I was in a group and had no time to speak”, “I know the rules, but I can't open my mouth” — every sentence like this saves the teacher two weeks of observation.

One tip: ask questions too. “How will I know I'm making progress?”, “How do you correct homework?” — a good teacher is glad to hear these questions, not annoyed by them.

An online English teacher — what changes on screen

The online format doesn't make the teacher's job easier — it changes it. What used to be done at a table with a notebook and a pen needs different tools on screen. And if the teacher doesn't use those tools, an online lesson really does become a poorer version of meeting face to face.

Screen sharing. We both see the text, the exercise or the video at the same time. No more turning the book round and “look here” — everything is on one screen, and the teacher can mark a mistake the moment it appears.

A shared document. Lesson notes, new words, corrected sentences — everything collects in one Google Doc or similar document that's open to both of us, for reading and for writing. Three months later it opens with one tap on your phone. That's an advantage a notebook simply doesn't have.

Pronunciation recordings. On screen you can do something that's awkward at a table: the teacher says a sentence and records it, you repeat it and record yourself, then we listen to both recordings together. th and w — sounds that many learners stumble over — get fixed exactly like this: by comparing, not by explaining.

So at the intro call, ask directly: “What do we use in the lesson — just the camera, or a shared document too?” The answer will tell you a lot.

Five questions after the first lesson

✍️ Check for yourself: after the first lesson, answer these five questions with “yes” or “no”.
1. Did the teacher check your level — even briefly?
2. Did they ask you what you need English for?
3. Do you know what you'll be working on over the next few weeks?
4. Do you have homework — and do you know it will be corrected?
5. Did you do the talking for more than half of the lesson?
Four or five “yes” answers — you're in the right place. Three or fewer — one more intro call with someone else won't cost you anything.

In short

The first lesson is less a test of you than a test of the teacher — and that's normal. A good teacher doesn't mind checking your level, listening to your goal and correcting your homework, because that's their job.

Frequently asked questions

What should happen at an intro call with an online teacher?

Three things: the teacher briefly checks your level, asks what you need English for and tells you honestly what they see — without promising a deadline. With me, this is a free 20-minute intro call on Google Meet.

What should I come away with after the first lesson?

A short plan (what you'll work on in the first month and why), the materials as a link or a file, and homework that gets corrected in the second lesson, with an explanation of why each mistake is a mistake.

Which signs are red flags?

The same textbook for everyone; a teacher who does most of the talking in the lesson; mistakes nobody corrects; and promises like “you'll be speaking fluently in three months”.

What should I prepare before the first meeting?

Three answers: a specific goal (what you need English for), the real time you have each week for homework, and what didn't work before. This saves the teacher two weeks of observation.
