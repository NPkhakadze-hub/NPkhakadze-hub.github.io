Conversational English — how to learn to speak, not just the rules 💬

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“I've learned the grammar, I do well on tests — but when I have to speak, I can't put two sentences together.” If you've ever said this too, the problem isn't your knowledge: it's simply that what you were taught wasn't conversational English.

In this article I'll explain what conversational English really is, what it's made of and how it's learned — both in lessons and between them.

What conversational English is — and what it isn't

Conversational English isn't a separate “dialect” or a separate grammar. It's a skill: being able, in a real situation — in a café, at an online meeting, at the doctor's, with a friend from abroad — to understand what people are saying to you and to answer in a way that keeps the conversation going. In other words, two skills at once: listening and speaking.

That's why learning conversational English isn't a grammar course. You still need rules, but they're a support, not the goal. The goal is that each day you manage one more conversation than the day before.

Why we know the rules and still stay silent

At school and university most of us mainly read and wrote English. Our brain learned to check a sentence, not to build one quickly and out loud. In a conversation you don't even have two seconds to remember a rule — you need ready-made phrases that come out on their own.

On top of that comes fear, which “blocks” an already small stock of phrases; I've written about it separately — on the fear of speaking. Here, though, let's talk about the technical side: what learning to speak English is made of.

What a conversation lesson looks like

In my conversation lessons most of the time goes to dialogue, not explanation. You choose the topics — work, travel, the series you're watching — because you need the words you'll actually use. I correct mistakes in context: I make a note, I don't stop the conversation, and at the end we look together at where you struggled and which phrase would have helped.

Conversational English can be learned online almost as naturally as in the same room: on Google Meet you can hear the voice, see the face and write phrases on the screen.

Six building blocks

1. Ready-made phrases. I was wondering if…, The thing is…, It depends. — chunks you don't build word by word but take as a whole.

2. Question forms. Conversations live on questions: Do you…? Have you ever…? What kind of…? Whoever asks the questions steers the conversation.

3. Reactions and fillers. Really? That's great! Well… Let me think. — little words that show you're listening and give you time to think.

4. Small talk. The weather, the weekend, work — short, light topics that almost every conversation starts with. More detail in small talk in English.

5. Asking again and clarifying. Sorry, could you say that again? — this isn't a sign of weak English but a sign of a good conversation partner.

6. Listening and shadowing. You listen to a short clip of video or audio and repeat it out loud almost at the same time — with the same intonation and rhythm. That way you train your ear and your mouth at the same time.

20 conversational phrases you can use tomorrow

The first ten — for starting a conversation and reacting:

How's it going? — an informal “How are you?”
Long time no see!
What do you do? — a question about someone's job
How was your weekend?
That sounds great!
Really?
No way! — surprise: “I can't believe it!”
I see.
That makes sense.
Fair enough. — “OK, I accept that” (you agree with the other person's point)

The second ten — for when you didn't understand, need time to think or have to end the conversation:

Sorry, could you say that again?
Could you speak a bit more slowly?
What do you mean?
How do you say … in English?
Let me think…
Well, it depends.
By the way, …
Anyway, … — to move on or get back to the main point
I'd better go. — a polite way to start ending the conversation
It was nice talking to you.

Typical mistakes in conversation

❌ What means "fair enough"?
✅ What does "fair enough" mean?
❌ Explain me, please.
✅ Explain it to me, please.
❌ Are you agree?
✅ Do you agree?
❌ Can you borrow me your pen?
✅ Can you lend me your pen? — (or: Can I borrow your pen?)
❌ How do you call this in English?
✅ What do you call this in English?
⚠️ I'm boring. — A correct sentence, but it means “I'm a boring person”.
✅ I'm bored.

Mistakes like these often appear when you translate word for word from your own language; I've collected the most common ones separately — the ten most common mistakes learners make in English.

How to practise between lessons

Out loud, every day, a little. Use the phrases above to describe what you're doing right now — thinking silently doesn't count. One clip a day: a short video or one minute of a podcast — first you listen, then you repeat it out loud. Three new phrases a week: deliberately work them into your conversations until they come out on their own. Everyone has their own pace — what matters is regularity, not speed.

If you're still close to zero, conversational English still starts on day one — with a few simple phrases; I've written about this in more detail in the article for beginners.

Check yourself

Choose the natural reply or correct the sentence:

1. A: How's it going? — B: ___ (a) I'm going to work. (b) Not bad, thanks. And you?
2. Correct it: What means “by the way”?
3. Correct it: Sorry, what you said?
4. Correct it: Are you agree with me?
5. A: I passed my exam! — B: ___ (a) That's great! (b) Never mind.

Answers: 1. (b) Not bad, thanks. And you? 2. What does "by the way" mean? 3. Sorry, what did you say? 4. Do you agree with me? 5. (a) That's great!

One last tip: pick five of these 20 phrases and use them out loud today — even if only with yourself. Frequently asked questions

What is conversational English?

The ability to understand what people say to you in a real situation and to answer in a way that keeps the conversation going — speaking and listening together. It isn't a grammar course: rules are a support, the goal is conversation.

Why do I know the grammar but can't speak?

Because your brain learned to check sentences, not to build them quickly and out loud. In a conversation there's no time to remember a rule — you need ready-made phrases, reactions and question forms that come out on their own. Often fear adds to it.

What does a conversational English lesson look like?

Most of the lesson is dialogue on your topics, not explanation. Mistakes are corrected in context: the teacher makes a note, doesn't stop the conversation, and at the end you look together at where you struggled and which phrase you need.

Can conversational English be learned online?

Yes — on Google Meet you can hear the voice, see the face and write phrases down, so the dialogue flows almost as naturally as in the same room. What matters is that you do the talking in the lesson and practise out loud between lessons.
