How to choose an online English tutor: 10 questions to ask 🧭

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“I need an English tutor” — that's how the search begins, and ten minutes later you've got twenty profiles open: one promises “fast results”, another a “unique method”, a third simply says “online English teacher, all levels”. How do you work out which one is right for you?

I'm a private English teacher and mentor myself, so in this article I'm not going to sing my own praises — I'll write down the questions I would ask any tutor if I were looking for one for someone close to me. Ten questions, with honest answers.

1–2. Who asks whom first?

1. Did they find out your level and your goal before offering you a programme? A good English tutor mostly listens at the first meeting: why you need English, where you use it, what you find hardest, what level you're at right now. If the “programme” is already prepared and all that's left is picking the days — that's the first warning sign.

2. Is there an intro meeting before you decide? Not a “trial lesson”, but a short conversation where both sides can see whether the pace, the personality and the way of explaining are a match. A tutor is someone you'll be spending months with — if the two of you don't click, even the best method won't save it.

3–4. Who talks more in the lesson?

3. Who does the talking for most of the lesson — you or the teacher? This is probably the most important question. The main advantage of a one-to-one lesson is that the speaking time is all yours. If the teacher explains and you listen — that's a lecture, not a lesson. A simple check: after the lesson, do you remember what you said? If you only remember what they said — something needs to change. The fear of speaking doesn't go away precisely because you don't get any speaking time.

4. Is the material prepared for you, or is it one textbook for everyone? A book isn't bad in itself — a good textbook gives you structure. The question is what gets added: vocabulary from your field, exercises built around your mistakes, dialogues tailored to your goal. If today you and another student have exactly the same page — that's not enough for a one-to-one lesson.

5–6. What happens between lessons?

5. Is your homework corrected with explanations? “The third sentence is wrong” — that isn't a correction. A correction is when they write down why:

❌ I have seen him yesterday.
✅ I saw him yesterday. — (yesterday points to a specific time in the past — here we need the Past Simple)

One explanation like this is worth more than ten silent “✗”s: now you understand the reason, and next time you'll catch the mistake yourself.

6. Do you get progress summaries? Regularly — what you worked on, what's going better, what's still left to do. Without this, especially after B1, progress becomes “invisible” and motivation fades (I wrote about this in the article “How long does it take to learn English”). Ask your teacher: “Where am I now, and what are we working on next?” — they should have a specific answer.

7–8. Tutor or group course? Online or face to face?

7. Group or one-to-one? A group has its advantages: you listen to others, you talk together, there's less pressure. The downside — the pace is set for the average student, and your speaking time is shared among everyone. If your goal is specific (a job interview, moving abroad, an exam) or your level doesn't match the group's — one-to-one will suit you better. If you want English without a specific goal, just for yourself, and you enjoy company — a group is a good choice.

8. Online or face to face? Honestly: with the camera on, the difference almost disappears, and online has its own advantages: screen sharing, a shared document, a recorded explanation you can look back at later. What really matters is a stable connection, cameras on both sides (your face and mouth movements need to be visible) and a quiet place. An online English tutor is also convenient because you don't choose them by city, but by who you actually work well with.

9. Native speaker, or a teacher who knows your language?

Both have their place — just at different stages. A native speaker gives you natural pronunciation and real-life, everyday expressions, and from B2 upwards that's very valuable. But they often can't see why you say I'm agree or why you mix up say and tell — that it comes from your first language. A teacher who knows how your language works sees the cause of the mistake and can explain in one sentence what someone else will correct several times and still not be able to tell you why. At A1–B1, an explanation in your own language saves time; from B2 upwards, conversation practice with a native speaker complements this rather than replacing it.

10. Warning signs

10. Do they promise you guaranteed results? This is the one question where a “yes” is a bad sign. And here are a few more signs that should worry you:

🚩 “You'll speak fluently in three months” — nobody can name a deadline without knowing your level and how busy you are; anyone who promises you an exact deadline is promising more than they know.
🚩 They haven't checked your level, but the programme is already prepared.
🚩 The lesson is the teacher reading slides aloud — you could do that yourself, and for free.
🚩 The answer to “why?” is “that's just how it is”.
🚩 When you're speaking, they cut you off and finish your sentences for you.

Test yourself

All ten questions together. Think of the tutor you're already learning with, or the one you're planning to start with, and answer “yes” or “no” to each:

1. They found out my level and goal first, then offered me a plan.
2. There was (or they offered) an intro meeting before I decided.
3. In the lesson, I do most of the talking.
4. The material is built around my goal and my mistakes.
5. My homework is corrected with explanations, not just with “✗”.
6. I regularly get a summary — where I am and what we're working on.
7. The format (group or one-to-one) matches my goal.
8. Everything works online: camera, connection, shared document.
9. They explain the cause of my mistakes, not just correct them.
10. Nobody is promising me “fluent English” within a specific time.

Result: 8–10 “yes” — you're in good hands, stay and keep working. 5–7 — the foundation is good; for the points where you said “no”, talk to your teacher directly — a good tutor will welcome that conversation. 0–4 — it's probably time to look at other options.

Frequently asked questions

How do I choose an English tutor?

Check a few things: do they find out your level and goal first, do you do most of the talking in the lesson, is the material prepared for you, is your homework corrected with explanations, and do you get progress summaries. An intro meeting before you start will show you a lot.

Online tutor or group course — which is better?

It depends on your goal. A group is good if you want general English and enjoy learning with others. One-to-one fits a specific goal more precisely (a job interview, moving abroad, an exam), and the speaking time is all yours.

Is a native speaker better, or a teacher who knows my language?

Each is better at a different stage. At A1–B1, a teacher who can explain why you make a mistake, in terms of your own language, saves time. From B2 upwards, conversation practice with a native speaker is very valuable — as an addition, not a replacement.

What are the warning signs when choosing a tutor?

A promise of “fluent English” within a set time, a ready-made programme without a level check, lessons that consist of reading slides, and corrections without explanations. A good tutor listens to you, asks you questions and has an answer to “why?”.
