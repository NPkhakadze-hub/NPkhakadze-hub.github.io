Reported speech explained simply — how to pass on what someone said 💬

26 October 2026 · Nodar

Yesterday your friend Nino told you, “I'm tired.” Today you're telling someone else about it. In many languages you would simply keep the tense she used: “Nino said she is tired.” That feels natural — but in English you would normally say: Nino said she was tired.

Passing on what someone else said is called reported speech (or indirect speech) in English. In this article we'll go step by step through when the tense changes and when it doesn't, how to report questions and requests, and why said me is a mistake. There's a short test at the end.

What reported speech is and why it's tricky

Direct speech is an exact quote: Nino said, "I'm tired." Reported speech gives the same content, but in your own words: Nino said (that) she was tired. In everyday speech, that is often left out.

Many learners find reported speech hard because their own language works differently. In a lot of languages you report what someone said without changing the tense at all — you keep it exactly as it was said. That's why so many of my students keep the present in English too and say: She said she is tired. Sometimes that's correct, sometimes it isn't. Below we'll see which is which.

“Shifting the tense back”

When the reporting verb is in the past (said, told, asked), the tense usually moves one step “back”. This is called backshift. The main pairs are:

Direct speechReported speech
"I work here."She said she worked there.
"I'm waiting."He said he was waiting.
"I've finished."She said she had finished.
"I saw him."He said he had seen him. / He said he saw him.
"I'll call you."She said she would call me.
"I can help."He said he could help.
"It may rain."They said it might rain.
"You must sign this."She said I had to sign it. / She said I must sign it.

A few clarifications. In everyday speech the Past Simple often stays unchanged if the order of events is clear anyway — that's why the table gives both options. With must, both forms are correct too. This is a special feature of reported speech: normally must doesn't work when you talk about the past (Yesterday I had to…, not I must…). would, could, should, might and the Past Perfect, as a rule, don't change: they can't be shifted back any further.

✅ "I should go." → He said he should go.

If any of these tenses still confuses you, have a look at the articles on the Present Perfect and the Past Simple and on the Past Simple and the Past Continuous.

When the tense doesn't change

Backshift is the usual rule, but it isn't always required. In two situations the tense can stay the same.

1. What was said is still true. In this case both options are correct. By keeping the present, you stress that it's still the case today.

✅ He said he lives in Batumi. — (and that's still true today)
✅ He said he lived in Batumi. — (neutral reporting)

If what was said is no longer true, the present doesn't work:

❌ Last year she said she is single, but now she's married.
✅ Last year she said she was single, but now she's married.

2. The reporting verb is in the present. After says, tells or asks, the tense doesn't change.

✅ She says she's running late.
✅ My boss always tells me I work too much.

Pronouns, time and place

When you report what someone said, “who”, “when” and “where” change, because you're speaking at a different time and in a different place. The pronoun changes depending on who is telling whom: I often becomes he/she, you becomes I/me, my becomes his/her. Words for time and place change like this:

tomorrow → the next day · yesterday → the day before · today → that day · next week → the following week · ago → before · here → there · this → that.

"I'll send it tomorrow." → He said he would send it the next day.

This isn't a mechanical rule. If you report it on the same day, tomorrow is still “tomorrow” and doesn't need to change: Giorgi said he'd call you tomorrow. First ask yourself: “For me, right now, which day is this?”

Questions: word order and if/whether

This is where the most common mistake appears. In a reported question the word order is that of a statement: subject first, then the verb. The auxiliary do/does/did disappears, and there's no question mark at the end.

"Where do you live?"
❌ She asked me where did I live.
✅ She asked me where I lived.
❌ He asked what was my name.
✅ He asked what my name was.

If the question has no question word (the answer is “yes” or “no”), use if or whether instead. Both are correct, but if is more conversational.

"Are you ready?" → She asked if I was ready.
"Have you been to London?" → He asked whether I had been to London.

Requests and orders: to + infinitive

The simplest way to report a request, advice or an order is: tell / ask + person + to + base form of the verb. In the negative, not goes before to.

"Wait here." → He told me to wait there.
"Please don't call after ten." → She asked me not to call after ten.
❌ The doctor told me don't eat sugar.
✅ The doctor told me not to eat sugar.

With this pattern you don't need to think about tenses at all: after to the verb is in its base form.

said or told

Another common mistake in reported speech is said me. In short: tell is followed directly by a person, say is not.

❌ She said me that she was busy.
✅ She told me (that) she was busy.
✅ She said (that) she was busy.

I explain this in detail in the article on say, tell, speak and talk.

Test yourself

Choose the correct option. Each item has one correct answer.

1. Last month Ana said she ___ me the next day, but she never did. (will call / would call)
2. The receptionist asked me where ___ . (did I live / I lived)
3. Giorgi ___ me that the meeting was cancelled. (said / told)
4. The doctor asked me ___ I smoked. (if / that)
5. My mum told me ___ late again. (don't be / not to be)

Answers: 1. would call — she made the promise in the past and didn't keep it. 2. I lived — a reported question uses statement word order, without did. 3. told — a person (me) follows directly, and only tell can do that. 4. if — it's a yes/no question. 5. not to be — an order: tell + person + not to + base form.

A practical tip: in the evening, use two or three sentences in English to retell who told you what during the day — My colleague said…, My sister asked me if…. That way you'll practise these structures on familiar, everyday stories. I go through must and similar verbs in the article on modal verbs. Frequently asked questions

Do I always have to change the tense in reported speech?

No. When the reporting verb is in the past (said, told), the tense usually moves one step back: is → was, will → would. If what was said is still true today, keeping the present is also correct: He said he lives in Batumi. If the reporting verb is in the present (She says…), the tense doesn't change.

said or told — what's the difference?

tell is followed directly by a person: She told me she was busy. say is not followed directly by a person: She said she was busy. That's why said me is a mistake — use told me (or said to me) instead.

Why is there no do/did in a reported question?

Because a reported question isn't a question any more — it's part of a statement. So the word order is that of a statement: She asked me where I lived, not where did I live. In a yes/no question, use if or whether: He asked if I was ready.

How do I report a request or an order?

tell or ask + person + to + base form: He told me to wait. In the negative, not goes before to: She asked me not to call.
