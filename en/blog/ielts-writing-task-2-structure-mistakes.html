IELTS Writing Task 2: the structure, and 5 mistakes that cost you marks ✍️

26 October 2026 · Nodar

Of the four parts of IELTS, Writing is the one where the score most often falls short of expectations. The reason is often neither vocabulary nor grammar: the essay is written in good English, just not the essay the task asked for. That's exactly what makes Task 2 tricky — it tests your understanding of the question more than you might think.

Below is a structure that fits every type of task, and five mistakes I come across most often when students prepare. At the end there's a short exercise — each sentence contains one mistake.

What Task 2 is and why it counts for more

The Writing paper consists of two tasks, and you have 60 minutes in total. Task 2 — an essay of at least 250 words — counts twice as much as Task 1 in the overall score, so the time split looks like this: about 20 minutes on Task 1 and 40 minutes on Task 2.

250 words is a minimum, not a target. Write fewer and you lose marks straight away. Write a lot more and the risk of mistakes goes up, with no time left for checking. A practical guide: 260–290 words.

The four criteria your score is based on

The examiner marks the essay on four criteria, and each one carries equal weight:

Task Response — answer exactly what the question asks and state a clear position. Coherence and Cohesion — the paragraphs are organised logically and linked to each other. Lexical Resource — vocabulary that is varied and precise. Grammatical Range and Accuracy — a range of structures, with few mistakes.

Remember one thing: in the last two criteria (Lexical Resource and Grammatical Range and Accuracy) the key word is “precise”, not “complicated”. A difficult word used wrongly costs you marks; a simple word used correctly doesn't.

A structure that fits every type of task

Four paragraphs — this is the most reliable framework:

1. Introduction (2–3 sentences). Restate the task in your own words and give your position. 2. and 3. Body paragraphs. One paragraph — one idea: the first sentence says what the paragraph is about, the next one explains it, then a concrete example. 4. Conclusion (1–2 sentences). Sum up the same position without adding a new argument.

The task type only changes what you write in the two middle paragraphs. If the question asks for two views, one paragraph covers one side and the other covers the other side; you state your own position in the introduction and repeat it in the conclusion. If the question asks for your opinion, both paragraphs support your argument.

The five task types — and what changes in each one

Task 2 questions fall into five types. Recognising the type is the first step, because it tells you what belongs in the two middle paragraphs.

1. Opinion (To what extent do you agree or disagree?) — one clear position and two arguments in its favour. Taking a middle position (“I partly agree”) is allowed, but then you need to make clear in the introduction what you agree with and what you don't.

2. Discussion (Discuss both views and give your own opinion) — one paragraph for one view, the other for the other view, and your own position goes in the introduction and conclusion. This is the type where marks are lost most often: many people argue for one side only and leave half of the task undone.

3. Advantages and disadvantages — one paragraph for each side. Pay attention to the end of the question here: if it says Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?, you must answer that too — a simple list isn't enough.

4. Problem and solution (What are the causes and what measures could be taken?) — the first paragraph is about causes, the second about solutions. A solution has to be specific: who does what.

5. Two-part question — two separate questions in one task. A simple rule: one body paragraph for each question. If you skip one question, the top of the Task Response scale is closed to you straight away.

Five mistakes that cost the most marks

1. Answering a different question. The task says: Discuss both views and give your own opinion, but the essay argues for one side only. In Task Response that means an immediate loss of marks, even if the language is flawless.

2. A paragraph without an example. “Education is important because it opens up opportunities” — that's a statement, not an argument. One concrete example is what proves the paragraph.

3. A memorised introduction. In this modern era of globalization, it is a highly debatable topic… — a phrase like this says nothing about the task. Examiners recognise it and don't give it any credit.

4. One big paragraph. A text without paragraphs loses marks in Coherence, however correctly it is written. The four paragraphs should be visible on the page too.

5. Vocabulary for show. A word taken from a dictionary whose context you don't know more often hurts than helps.

❌ This essay will discuss about the advantages of public transport.
✅ This essay will discuss the advantages of public transport. — discuss doesn't need about. (Stylistically, though, “announcing” the essay is weak — state your position directly in the introduction.)
❌ Nowadays, people is more dependent on technology than before.
✅ Nowadays, people are more dependent on technology than before. — people is plural.

What a good introduction looks like

Let's say the task is: Some people think that children should start learning a foreign language at primary school. Others believe it is better to start at secondary school. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

The introduction does two jobs — it restates the task in your own words and gives your position:

✅ Opinions differ on the best age to begin studying a second language: some argue for the primary years, while others prefer secondary school. In my view, an early start brings clear advantages, provided that lessons stay enjoyable. — the idea of the task in your own words, then a clear position.

Notice that the words of the task haven't been copied: primary school became the primary years, a foreign language became a second language, think became argue. That is the paraphrasing that Lexical Resource rewards.

How to build a body paragraph

Every body paragraph has four steps: idea → explanation → example → link. The first sentence says in one thought what the paragraph is about; the second explains why; the third gives a concrete example; and the last one brings the paragraph back to the question in the task.

✅ An early start also makes pronunciation easier. Young children imitate sounds without worrying about mistakes, so unfamiliar consonants cause them little difficulty. Children who begin before the age of ten, for example, rarely struggle with the English "th" sound. This suggests that primary school is the better starting point. — idea, explanation, example, link — all four steps in one paragraph.

An example doesn't have to be a statistic. In IELTS, an invented figure (“according to a 2019 study, 87%…”) doesn't earn you marks and carries a risk. A personal or general, convincing example works much better: In my own experience… · In many European countries…

Paragraph length matters too. Four to six sentences — that's the range in which an idea is fully developed and the text is still easy to read. A two-sentence body paragraph counts as underdeveloped.

Splitting the 40 minutes

Most often people run out of time because they try to write a perfect final version straight away, with no planning. It's better like this: 5 minutes — analysing the question and noting down two ideas; 30 minutes — writing; 5 minutes — checking.

In those last five minutes you look for three things: third-person verb endings, the plural -s and articles. These are the mistakes that steal the most marks and are the easiest to fix.

Test yourself

Each sentence contains exactly one mistake. Find it and correct it.

1. In conclusion, I am agree with this statement.
2. The internet contains a lot of informations about this topic.
3. Governments should to invest more money in public education.
4. Some people believes that online learning is more effective.
5. This problem can be solve by better planning.

Answers: 1. I agree — agree is an ordinary verb and doesn't need to be. 2. a lot of information — information is uncountable and has no plural (in formal writing much information is also correct). 3. should invest — a modal verb isn't followed by to (or ought to invest — unlike should, ought to does need to). 4. believe — some people is plural. 5. be solved — the passive needs the past participle.

One tip that shows results fastest: before you start writing, read the task twice and say out loud what it is asking for — “two views plus my opinion” or “just my opinion”. This one habit saves you from the most expensive mistake in Task Response. Frequently asked questions

How many words should I write in IELTS Writing Task 2?

The minimum is 250 words, and writing fewer costs you marks. A practical guide is 260–290 words: you are safely above the minimum, the risk of mistakes doesn't grow, and you still have time to check.

How much time should I spend on Task 2?

Out of the 60 minutes for Writing, about 40 belong to Task 2, because it counts twice as much as Task 1 in the overall score. Within those 40 minutes, it's best to spend 5 on analysing the task, 30 on writing and 5 on checking.

Will a memorised introduction work?

No. General phrases that don't address the specific task are easy for the examiner to spot and earn no credit. In the introduction you should restate the task in your own words and give your position.

What costs the most marks?

An essay that answers a different question: the task asks for two views, but the essay argues for one side only. Even an essay with flawless language loses marks in Task Response if it doesn't fully complete the task.
