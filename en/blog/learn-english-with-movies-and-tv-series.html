Learning English with films and TV series — a method that works 🎬

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“I've been watching series in English for years, but I still can't speak” — this is probably the most honest complaint I've ever heard from a student. And it's true: thousands of hours of watching can add up to almost nothing if all you do is watch.

At the same time, films and series are one of the best sources of English there is: real phrases, natural speed, intonation you won't find in any textbook. Below I explain why “just watching” doesn't work and which method turns the very same episode into a real lesson.

Why “just watching” doesn't work

When you watch a series with subtitles in your own language, your brain takes the shortest route: it reads the text, and the sound stays in the background. So your “two hours of English” are really two hours of reading in your own language with a soundtrack. You follow the plot, but not the phrases.

The second reason goes deeper. Listening is a passive skill — you recognise what you already know. Speaking is active — you have to build the phrase yourself, at the right moment, with the right intonation. Passive watching only develops the passive skill: you hear "no way" in the series again and again, but in your own conversations you still say "it's impossible", because you've never used the phrase yourself.

And third: speed. A character in a series says dozens of phrases a minute. You won't remember a single one unless you stop, repeat and write it down. English doesn't “rub off” on you from a video — you have to pull it out.

⚠️ I watch English movies every day, so my English gets better. — grammatically correct, but the promise is false: watching doesn't improve your speaking on its own.
✅ I pick out a few phrases from every episode and use them the next day. — pick out = choose, select.

The core of the method: one episode, three times

The main principle is simple — less material, more depth. Instead of five seasons, one episode that you squeeze dry. One 20-minute episode is split into three stages, each with a different subtitle mode.

Stage 1 — subtitles in your language. You watch the episode once, normally: the plot, the characters, what's going on. The only goal is understanding — you don't write anything down at this stage. This is the “map” without which the next two stages get much harder.

Stage 2 — English subtitles. You watch the same episode with English subtitles. Now you already know what's happening, so your attention shifts to how they say it. This is where the main work starts: whenever you like a phrase or it surprises you, pause and write it down. Five or six phrases from one episode are more than enough.

Stage 3 — no subtitles. You watch the episode a third time, without subtitles. You know the plot and the phrases — now your ear is training. If you can't make out a bit, go back and switch the English subtitles on for a second. This stage shows you where your listening still has gaps.

You don't need to do all three stages in one day: three days — three stages. One episode a week this way gives you far more than a new episode every day with subtitles in your own language.

Repeating a 2-minute scene — the most powerful exercise

At stage two, pick one short scene — up to two minutes, where two characters talk naturally, with no chases or explosions. This scene is your “material” for the whole week.

First watch it three times with English subtitles. Then switch the subtitles off and follow the character at the same time — at the same speed, with the same intonation, even if you can't keep up in places. This is called shadowing — following like a shadow. After that, pause the scene at the end of each line and repeat it on your own, without the character.

Finally — the most awkward and the most useful part: record yourself on your phone and compare it with the original. Where the intonation dropped, which word slipped back into your native accent — your ear will tell you this better than any explanation.

❌ I repeated the scene ten times, but I didn't say nothing aloud.
✅ I repeated the scene ten times, but I didn't say anything aloud. — standard English doesn't use double negatives; you'll hear them in series, but don't copy them.

Phrase cards — so the phrase makes it into your speaking

Turn the phrases you wrote down at stage two into cards — on paper or in a phone app, it doesn't matter. What matters is what goes on the card. One side: the English phrase with the scene's context — who said it and in what situation. The other side: the meaning in your own words, not a word-for-word translation.

Cards for single words don't work here — the word way has ten meanings, but "no way" has one specific meaning. A card is always a phrase, ideally a whole line.

And one rule that keeps a card alive: every card should have a date on it — when you used it in a real conversation: in a lesson, with a friend, even out loud to yourself. A phrase you've used three times is yours. A phrase that's only written on a card isn't yet.

Which film or series to choose

The most common mistake is choosing the series you love most rather than the one that matches your level. Three simple criteria:

Everyday life, not a specialist world. Medical, legal and science-fiction series are full of terms that even native speakers don't use in everyday conversation. Family, friends, the office, a café — a phrase you take from these will be useful tomorrow.

Short episodes. A 20-minute sitcom can take three viewings; a 60-minute drama hardly can. Laughter helps too: a phrase that made you laugh is easier to remember.

⚠️ I'm watching a serial about doctors.
✅ I'm watching a series about doctors. — in everyday English, a TV show is a series. Serial does exist (a story told in parts, mostly British), but you'll meet the word far more often in serial number or serial killer.

Clear diction. At A2–B1 level, choose series where the characters speak clearly and not too fast. Whispering, strong dialects and noisy scenes can wait until after B2.

❌ I prefer to watch the films in English than dubbed.
✅ I prefer watching films in English to watching them dubbed. — prefer X to Y: when comparing, use to; than only appears in the pattern prefer to watch… rather than….

Phrases you'll hear in series all the time

These are the kind of short phrases that deserve a card of their own — they turn up in almost every modern series and you hear them in real conversations every day.

✅ "No way!" — That can't be true! / I don't believe it!
✅ "What's the catch?" — What's the hidden problem? (when an offer sounds too good)
✅ "I'll take it from here." — I'll continue from this point / I'll handle the rest.
✅ "Don't get me wrong." — Don't misunderstand me.
✅ "It's not a big deal." — It's not important.
✅ "Let's call it a day." — That's enough for today, let's stop.

Notice: most of these phrases mean something completely different if you take them word by word. "What's the catch?" read literally — “what is the thing that catches?” — tells you nothing; “what's the hidden problem?” tells you everything. That's exactly why the card carries the meaning, not a translation.

What films can't do, and what else you'll need

To be honest: a series won't teach you the system of grammar. You'll hear the Present Perfect a thousand times, but why it's used there instead of the Past Simple — that needs explaining. And most importantly — a series doesn't listen to you. It can't tell you that you said "I'll take it from here" correctly, but that your intonation in the shadowing still sounded like your native language.

So learning with films works best when it's an addition and not your only source: you bring phrases from the series, use them in conversation with a real person, and fix your mistakes on the spot. One episode a week + one conversation using those phrases — that's already a system, not a hope.

A new word you heard in a film will soon be forgotten unless you review it. I wrote about how to set up a review system in my article on remembering words.

Check yourself

Choose the correct option. Each item has one answer.

1. In the three-stage method, at which stage do you write phrases down? (a) with subtitles in your language (b) with English subtitles (c) without subtitles
2. "Let's call it a day" means: (a) let's meet today (b) let's stop for today (c) let's name the day
3. "I prefer watching sitcoms ______ watching dramas." (a) than (b) to (c) from
4. Speaking along with a character, at the same speed, is called: (a) shadowing (b) dubbing (c) skipping
5. "That's not a big ______." (a) deal (b) affair (c) case

Answers: 1. b — at stage two, when you already know the plot and your attention is on the phrases. 2. b. 3. b — prefer X to Y. 4. a — dubbing means replacing the voices, skipping means leaving something out. 5. a — not a big deal is a fixed expression; with affair or case this phrase doesn't exist.

One practical tip: this week, pick one 20-minute episode and go through just these three stages — nothing more. Frequently asked questions

Can I learn English just by watching series?

Not just by watching. Watching is a passive skill and doesn't develop speaking on its own. A series works when you write phrases down, repeat them out loud and then use them in real conversation — and you still need grammar explanations and feedback separately.

Which subtitles should I use — in my language or in English?

Both, but in order: first subtitles in your own language for the plot, then English subtitles to write phrases down, and finally no subtitles as listening practice. One episode watched three times gives you far more than three new episodes with subtitles in your own language.

What is shadowing?

Following a character like a shadow: without subtitles, you speak along with them at the same speed and with the same intonation. It works best on a short scene of up to two minutes: repeat it several times, then compare your own recording with the original.

Which series should I choose for learning English?

Everyday topics (family, friends, the office), short episodes that can stand three viewings, and clear diction. Medical, legal and fantasy series are full of terms, and save strong dialects for after B2.
