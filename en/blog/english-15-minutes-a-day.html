English in 15 minutes a day — a realistic plan for busy people ⏱️

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“I need English, but I don't have time” — I hear this most often from people who work. Job, family, commute — and in the evening, when you finally have a moment, there's no energy left to open a book.

If you're wondering how to learn English while working full-time, this article is for you. I'll talk about the minimum that fits into even the busiest day: 15 minutes. I'll tell you honestly what this time will and won't give you, suggest a weekly plan that covers every skill and show you where to find these minutes without extra effort.

What 15 minutes will give you — and what it won't

Let's start with what it won't give you. One level takes roughly 150–200 hours (more at the higher levels), and 15 minutes a day adds very little to that total. In other words, at this pace alone your level won't rise quickly — and anyone who promises otherwise simply isn't doing the maths.

On the other hand, learning English on your own — at home or on the go, 15 minutes every day — will definitely give you three things. First, a habit: English becomes part of everyday life and stops being a “project” you restart every Monday. Second, continuity: you forget less of what you've learned, because you come back to it every day. Third, an easy start: a 15-minute session is easy to begin, while an hour-long one on a tired evening is almost impossible.

So look at these minutes this way: as the foundation everything else is built on — lessons, more time at the weekend, real conversation. I've written about how long one level takes and what speeds up the process in a separate article.

The weekly plan: one skill per day

The main rule is this: one day — one skill. In 15 minutes you can't work on every skill at once: it would all stay superficial. When you spread the skills across the days, by the end of the week you've practised each one at least once.

DaySkillWhat you do in 15 minutes
MondayListeningA short clip from a podcast or video; on the second listen, note down 2–3 phrases
TuesdayVocabularyPut Monday's phrases on flashcards and review your old cards
WednesdayReadingA short text about your profession or hobby; look up unknown words only at the end
ThursdaySpeakingShadowing: repeat a short recording out loud along with the speaker
FridayWriting5–6 sentences about your week — using the week's new words
Saturday, SundayA film or restOne episode of a series with English subtitles — or a complete rest

One exception: if you use a flashcard app, review the cards the app has scheduled for that day on every weekday, in 2–3 minutes — over your coffee, for example; give the rest of the time to the day's skill. On Tuesday you add new cards.

The days build on each other: on Tuesday you memorise Monday's phrases, and on Friday you use the whole week's words in your writing. That way the skills connect with each other and don't stay separate, isolated exercises.

The weekend is free on purpose. If you have the energy, watch a film or a series — how to turn that into learning, I've explained in detail in the article on learning with films and series. If not — rest. Rest is part of the plan, not a break from it.

Where do I find 15 minutes a day?

The hardest part isn't finding the time — it's getting started. So don't look for separate time for the new habit: attach it to something you already do every day. This technique is called habit stacking — tying a new habit to an old one.

☕ With your coffee. — Pour your coffee and open your flashcards. When the coffee's finished, so is the practice.
🚌 On the way. — For listening day, the bus, the metro or the passenger seat is ideal: you have your headphones with you anyway.
🌙 Before bed. — Reading and writing suit a quiet evening: one short text instead of social media.

Plan precisely: not “when I have time”, but “after my coffee, in the kitchen”. A vague plan falls apart easily; a concrete one, much less often.

And one simple rule about missed days: you can miss one weekday, but try not to miss two in a row (the weekend break doesn't count as a missed day). One missed day doesn't destroy a habit; what destroys it is one day quietly turning into a week.

How do I practise speaking without a conversation partner?

Speaking on your own may feel a little strange, but it's perfectly possible. Three techniques fit easily into 15 minutes.

Shadowing — repeating “like a shadow”. Take a short recording, ideally one with a transcript. Listen to it first, then play it again and speak along with the speaker, a split second behind: follow their rhythm and intonation. This exercise improves both your pronunciation and your speaking speed. I've written about other techniques in the pronunciation article.

Reading aloud. Choose one paragraph from Wednesday's text and read it out loud. Wherever you stumble, repeat that bit two or three times.

Talking to yourself. Describe what you're doing — out loud, even in a whisper. Start with sentences like these:

✅ I'm making coffee, and then I'm going to check my email.
✅ I need to call the bank before lunch.
✅ I should have left earlier — the traffic is terrible.

If you can't remember a word, don't stop — describe it in other words and keep going. If you want to devote a whole month to speaking, the 30-day speaking plan is a natural continuation of this weekly rhythm: there, every day is devoted to speaking practice.

Which tools are useful?

For 15 minutes you don't need many apps. Three types of tool are quite enough.

Flashcards with spaced repetition. Apps like these (Anki, for example) work out for you which word to review when. You can do the same in a notebook if you write the dates yourself. I've explained what makes a flashcard really work in a separate article on memorising words.

A podcast for learners. Look for one where people speak slowly and clearly and that comes with a transcript — that will be very useful for shadowing. When it gets easier, switch to a regular podcast on a topic you find interesting.

Your phone's voice recorder. The simplest and one of the most useful tools. Record your Thursday speaking and once a month listen to an old recording: change isn't visible from day to day, but when you compare months, it's usually noticeable.

What you definitely don't need is five apps at once. Every new app takes time to figure out — and that time comes straight out of your 15 minutes.

When 15 minutes is no longer enough

Practising on your own has two weak spots — and I'll tell you about them directly.

Live conversation. Shadowing and talking to yourself prepare your tongue, but they can't teach you to answer an unexpected question quickly. For that you need a real conversation partner.

Error correction. You rarely notice your own mistakes. A wrong form you repeat for months gradually becomes a habit — and then fixing it is harder than learning it correctly from the start.

If either of these is holding you back, or you have a specific goal — a job interview, an exam, moving abroad — a teacher should be added to your 15 minutes. Even then these minutes aren't wasted: they're exactly what keeps what you've learned alive between lessons. Test yourself

This time there's no grammar test: this exercise is for putting together your own weekly plan. Answer five questions, ideally in writing. There are no right or wrong answers here, so there's no answer key either: the right answer is whatever fits your day.

1. Which moment of the day will you attach your 15 minutes to — coffee, your commute, the time before bed or something else? ___
2. Which skill do you find hardest, and which day of the week will you give it? ___
3. Where will you find Monday's listening material? ___
4. What topic will you write your 5–6 sentences about on Friday? ___
5. What will you do if you miss a day? ___

Keep your answers and look at them again in a month: what worked, and what's worth changing.

One last piece of advice: don't start the plan on Monday — start it today, even with a single podcast clip. If you think you've left it too late because of your age, read what I write about English after 40: short but frequent practice suits adults especially well.

Frequently asked questions

Is 15 minutes a day enough for English?

For building a habit and keeping what you've learned — yes. For raising your level quickly — no: one level takes roughly 150–200 hours (more at the higher levels). That's why 15 minutes works best combined with lessons or extra study time.

Is it better to study 15 minutes every day or two hours once a week?

Usually short daily practice: you come back to what you've learned often and forget less. A two-hour session is tiring, and if you miss that one day, the whole week is lost.

How can I learn to speak on my own?

Three techniques fit even into 15 minutes: shadowing (repeating a recording along with the speaker), reading aloud and talking to yourself. Live conversation and error correction, though, need a conversation partner or a teacher.
