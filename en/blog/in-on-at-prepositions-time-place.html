in, on or at? Prepositions of time and place 📍

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“On Tuesday”, “in the evening”, “on the table” — in many languages you don't need a separate little word for this: a case ending, one all-purpose preposition or nothing at all does the job. In English, though, you have to choose in, on or at every single time. The good news is that there's logic here, not chaos.

One picture that explains prepositions of place

Imagine a zoom: from big to small.

🌍 in — big, inside: a city, a country, a room, a car
📅 on — on a surface: a table, a wall, a street, a bus
📍 at — an exact point: an address, a door, a specific place

Time

Time has its own rule — the “zoom” logic works here too (in 2026 → on Monday → at 7), but with exceptions (at night, at the weekend — and a weekend is longer than a Monday, after all):

🗓️ in — a period we see from the inside: a year, a month, a season, a part of the day
📆 on — a day or a date
⏰ at — a clock time, or a stretch of time we treat as a point: at 7, at night, at Christmas
✅ in 2026 · in July · in the morning · in winter · in two hours
✅ on Monday · on 5 May · on my birthday · on Friday evening
✅ at 7 o'clock · at noon · at night · at the weekend · at Christmas

One exception to remember: for night in general, say at night (I can't sleep at night). In the night exists too, but it's used about one particular night, meaning “during the night”: I woke up in the night. And one difference: at the weekend is British, on the weekend is American — both are correct.

Watch out for one trap: once you add a day, in the morning becomes on Monday morning.

✅ I get up early in the morning.
✅ I have a lesson on Monday morning. — a day appears → on

Place

✅ in Tbilisi · in Georgia · in the car · in my bag · in the kitchen
✅ on the table · on the wall · on the bus · on Rustaveli Avenue · on the second floor
✅ at home · at work · at school · at the door · at 15 Chavchavadze Street

Why in the car but on the bus? The logic is simple: you get into a car and sit down, but on a bus you can stand up and walk around — as if on a platform. The same goes for planes, trains and ships: on the plane, on the train.

The most common mistakes

❌ I arrived to Tbilisi.
✅ I arrived in Tbilisi. — a city or country: arrive in
✅ I arrived at the airport. — a specific place: arrive at
❌ I'm in home.
✅ I'm at home. — at home is a fixed phrase (in American English also I'm home).
❌ See you in Monday.
✅ See you on Monday.
⚠️ I live in Chavchavadze Street. — correct in British English.
✅ I live on Chavchavadze Street. — American English — and the safest choice in an international setting.

in vs. within — how long

Lots of people get this nuance wrong in business emails:

✅ I'll finish it in two hours. — two hours from now (after two hours).
✅ I'll finish it within two hours. — during the next two hours, i.e. no later than two hours from now.
⚠️ I finished it in two hours. — in the past, in is about duration: it took me two hours.

Test yourself

1. The meeting is ___ 3 o'clock ___ Tuesday.
2. She was born ___ 1998 ___ Kutaisi.
3. There's a nice picture ___ the wall.
4. I'll be ___ work until six.

Answers: 1. at 3 o'clock on Tuesday. 2. in 1998 in Kutaisi. 3. on the wall. 4. at work.

Prepositions are one of those topics where knowing the rule isn't enough — you need repetition until the choice becomes automatic. That's why in my lessons we don't study them separately; we notice them in every text and keep coming back to them.

Frequently asked questions

How do I remember in, on and at?

For place, imagine zooming from big to small: in is big and inside (a city, a country, a room), on is on a surface (a street, a table), at is an exact point (an address, a specific place). For time: in is a period (a year, a month, a part of the day), on is a day or date, at is a clock time and fixed phrases (at night, at the weekend, at Christmas).

How do I say that I'm at my house right now?

I'm at home. "In home" is wrong, because at home is a fixed phrase. The same logic gives at work and at school. But in the kitchen, in my room, when you mean a specific room.

Why "in the car" but "on the bus"?

You get into a car and sit down: it's a closed space. On a bus you can stand up and walk around, as if on a platform. The same goes for planes, trains and ships: on the plane, on the train, on the ship.
