IELTS Speaking — the three parts, questions and sample answers 🎤

26 October 2026 · Nodar

The question I get asked most about IELTS Speaking is: “What should I say to get a high score?” The answer is surprising: for the examiner, what you say matters far less than how you say it. Speaking is a language test, not a test of ideas: what's assessed isn't how clever your opinion is, but how fluently, accurately and clearly you express it in English.

Below I go through all three parts one by one: what you'll be asked, what a natural answer sounds like, and which mistakes I see most often when I prepare students for this exam.

Three parts and 11–14 minutes

Speaking is a live conversation with an examiner. It is recorded and lasts 11–14 minutes in total. This module is sometimes scheduled on a different day from the other three. The parts follow one another, and each one is a little harder than the one before.

Part 1 — interview (4–5 minutes). The examiner introduces themselves, checks your identity and asks you short questions on familiar topics: work or studies, your hometown, your home, your free time.

Part 2 — long turn (3–4 minutes). The examiner gives you a card (cue card) with a topic and a few bullet points. You have one minute to prepare: you'll be given paper and a pencil to jot down briefly what you're going to say. Then you speak for up to two minutes. When the time is up, the examiner will stop you and may ask you one or two short questions.

Part 3 — discussion (4–5 minutes). The questions are linked to the Part 2 topic, but they are more general and abstract: they're less about your personal experience — the conversation is about society, causes and the future, although a personal example can still help to support your argument. Here the examiner expects you to justify your opinion, compare things and speculate about the future.

What the examiner assesses: four criteria

Speaking is assessed on four official criteria, and each one makes up 25% of the overall score.

Fluency and Coherence — whether you speak smoothly, without long pauses or restarts, and how logically you link your ideas. Lexical Resource — how varied and precise your vocabulary is, and whether you can paraphrase when you forget the word you need.

Grammatical Range and Accuracy — whether you use a range of structures (complex sentences, different tenses) and how rarely you make mistakes. Pronunciation — how easy you are to understand: sounds, word stress, intonation.

One important detail: an accent in itself doesn't cost you points. You don't need to imitate a British or American accent; what matters is that the examiner can easily understand what you're saying.

Part 1: questions and a natural answer

Part 1 questions are simple, and that's exactly why many people answer with just one sentence. A good guide is 2–4 sentences: an answer, a reason or detail and, perhaps, a small example. Typical questions: Do you work or are you a student? · How do you usually spend your weekends? · Do you enjoy cooking?

This is what a natural, extended answer sounds like — not too “bookish” and not too short:

Do you enjoy cooking?
✅ Yeah, I do, actually. I'm not a great cook, but I like making simple things at the weekend — mostly Georgian dishes my mum taught me. It's a nice way to switch off after a busy week. — Answer, detail, reason: three sentences and not a single memorised phrase.

Notice actually and switch off: conversational expressions like these are a plus for Lexical Resource, as long as you use them in the right place.

Part 2: the cue card and the two-minute talk

The card has a task roughly like this:

Describe a skill you learned that you are proud of.
You should say:
what the skill is
when and how you learned it
how difficult it was
and explain why you are proud of it.

Don't write full sentences in that one minute — you won't have time. Note down 4–5 key words, one or two per bullet point, and decide which story you're going to tell. For example: driving — 3 years ago — new job, bus — failed test — nearly gave up. Based on these notes, the start of your answer could sound like this:

✅ I'd like to talk about learning to drive, which is something I put off for years. I finally signed up for lessons about three years ago, mainly because I'd just started a new job on the other side of the city and the bus journey took forever. To be honest, it was much harder than I expected. The theory was fine, but driving in real traffic was pretty stressful at first — I remember stalling at a traffic light while everyone behind me was beeping. I failed my first practical test, which was a bit embarrassing, but I passed the second time. I think I'm proud of it mainly because I nearly gave up… — A story with concrete details; the Past Simple, Past Continuous and Past Perfect alternate naturally.

The rest of the answer covers the last bullet point: why you're proud and what changed afterwards. A story like this fills two minutes easily, because it naturally has a beginning, a difficulty and a result. If you feel you've run out of things to say but there's still time left, add one more thing: what you would advise someone who is just starting to learn it.

Part 3: discussion and justifying your opinion

Part 3 questions continue the Part 2 topic, but more broadly. The card above might be followed by a question like this: Which skills will be most important in the future? A short answer is no longer enough here: you need a position, a reason, an example and, ideally, an acknowledgement of the opposite view.

Do you think older people find it harder to learn new skills than young people?
✅ I think it depends on the skill, really. With technology, younger people probably do have an advantage, because they've grown up with it. But I'm not sure age itself is the main problem. Older people are often more patient and better at organising their time, so if they're motivated, they can learn just as well. I suppose the bigger obstacle is confidence — a lot of adults are afraid of looking silly, so they don't even try. — A position, two sides, reasons and, at the end, speculation (I suppose).

Five mistakes I see most often

1. Memorised answers. The internet is full of “Band 9 answers”, and memorising them seems logical. The problem is that a memorised text has its own rhythm — fast, “clean”, monotonous — and the examiner recognises it easily. An answer like that doesn't count as evidence of real language ability. It's better to learn topics and phrases rather than answers, so you can adapt them to any question.

2. One-word answers in Part 1.

⚠️ Do you like reading? — "Yes."
✅ Yes, quite a lot. I usually read before bed, mostly detective novels — they help me forget about work. — “Yes” is grammatically correct, but it gives the examiner nothing to assess.

3. Silence while thinking. A long pause costs you points in Fluency, and a filler word from your own language sounds out of place in an English conversation. Thinking is normal — just fill those seconds with an English phrase. If the pause comes from nerves, the tips on the fear of speaking English will help here too.

4. Not filling the two minutes in Part 2. If you finish after a minute and a half, that doesn't automatically mean a lower score, but you give the examiner less material to assess. Practise with a timer: one minute to prepare, two minutes to speak — and record yourself.

5. Translating from your own language. When you think in your own language first and then translate, you carry its structure over into English:

❌ I very like my job.
✅ I really like my job. — To say you like something a lot, use really like or like … very much; very doesn't go before a verb like this.
❌ I live in Tbilisi since 2015.
✅ I have lived in Tbilisi since 2015. — Many languages use the present tense here, but in English since needs the Present Perfect (or have been living).
❌ Kutaisi is very old city.
✅ Kutaisi is a very old city. — Not every language has articles, but in English a singular countable noun usually needs one.

Phrases that buy you time and organise your ideas

When you need time to think: That's an interesting question — I've never really thought about it. · Let me think about that for a second. · Well, it's hard to say, but… · Sorry, could you repeat the question, please?

When you give your opinion: Personally, I'd say… · I think it depends on… · On the one hand… on the other hand…

When you speculate in Part 3: I'd imagine that… · It's quite likely that… · In the future, we might see… · It could be because…

One warning: a phrase is a bridge, not an answer. If you repeat That's an interesting question for every question, it becomes a template. It's better to know a few options and rotate them.

Test yourself

There are two options in brackets — choose the one that fits the gap.

1. In Part 2 you have ___ to prepare. (one minute / two minutes)
2. I have lived in Batumi ___ 2019. (since / for)
3. I ___ like cooking, especially at the weekend. (very / really)
4. Part 3 questions are linked by topic to ___ . (Part 1 / Part 2)
5. Mtskheta is ___ very old town. (a / the)

Answers: 1. one minute — the talk itself lasts up to two minutes. 2. since — since is followed by a point in time (2019), for by a length of time (for five years). 3. really — very doesn't go with a verb like this. 4. Part 2 — Part 3 takes the topic of the long turn further. 5. a — a singular countable noun in the sense of “one of many”.

One habit that really pays off: every day, record a two-minute talk on your phone on any Part 2 topic and listen back — you'll hear where you get stuck and which words you're missing. Frequently asked questions

How long does IELTS Speaking take?

11–14 minutes in total. Part 1 (interview) is 4–5 minutes, Part 2 (long turn with a cue card) is 3–4 minutes including one minute of preparation, and Part 3 (discussion) is 4–5 minutes.

What criteria is Speaking assessed on?

Four: Fluency and Coherence, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range and Accuracy, and Pronunciation. Each makes up 25% of the overall score. An accent in itself doesn't cost you points — what matters is that you are easy to understand.

How much time do I have to prepare and speak in Part 2?

You have one minute to prepare (you'll be given paper and a pencil for notes) and up to two minutes to speak. After two minutes the examiner will stop you; it's best to use the full time.

Can I memorise my answers?

I don't recommend it. The examiner can easily recognise a memorised text, and it doesn't count as evidence of real language ability. It's better to learn topics and phrases that you can adapt to any question.
