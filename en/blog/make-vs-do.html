make or do? The difference, with a simple trick 🔨

26 October 2026 · Nodar

In many languages one verb covers both. In English, though, do a cake sounds as strange as “carrying out a cake” would.

🔨 make — you create something new (you're left holding a result)
⚙️ do — you carry out an action, a task, a duty

  2. do — you get a job done

make — something comes into being

✅ make a cake · make coffee · make dinner — food
✅ make a decision · make a plan · make a choice — decisions
✅ make a mistake · make progress · make an effort
✅ make money · make friends · make noise · make a mess
✅ make an appointment · make a phone call · make a suggestion

do — you get a job done

✅ do homework · do the washing-up · do the shopping · do housework
✅ do a job · do business · do research · do an exercise
✅ do your best · do someone a favour · do harm / do good
✅ do the dishes · do your hair · do exercise — physical exercise

The traps people fall into most often

❌ I must do a decision.
✅ I must make a decision.

❌ Don't do mistakes.
✅ Don't make mistakes.

❌ I make my homework every evening.
✅ I do my homework every evening.

❌ I need to do a phone call.
✅ I need to make a phone call.

Same noun, different verbs

Sometimes the same noun goes with different verbs — and each one means something different. This is where the nuance really matters:

✅ make a plan — you draw the plan up
✅ carry out the plan — you put it into action

✅ set an exam — the teacher writes it
✅ take / do / sit an exam — the student is the one being tested

In business English

You'll meet these combinations all the time, in emails and in meetings:

✅ give a presentation — you deliver it (the most natural choice)
✅ do a presentation — the same, more conversational
✅ prepare a presentation — you get it ready
✅ make an offer · make a profit · make a deal
✅ do a deal — also common in British English
✅ do paperwork · do the accounts

How to remember it

With my students, this formula works best: make — something appears that wasn't there before (a cake, a decision, noise, a mistake). do — you carry out a task or duty that already exists (homework, the dishes, a job).

A cake appears → make a cake. A mistake appears → make a mistake. Noise appears → make noise. The dishes were already there, you're just washing them → do the dishes. You were already given the homework → do homework.

The rule doesn't cover every case — language is alive, and there will always be exceptions. But most of the time it will give you the right answer.

Test yourself

Fill in make or do in the correct form:

1. Sorry, I ___ a mistake.
2. Have you ___ your homework?
3. She wants to ___ friends here.
4. He ___ me a big favour.

Answers: 1. made (or have made). 2. done. 3. make. 4. did (or has done).

make and do are one of those topics where the rule helps, but in the end repetition settles everything. My advice is simple: when you come across a new combination in a text, write down the whole thing — not make, but make an appointment. The same logic works for say/tell too. That way your brain remembers the whole phrase, not a rule.

Frequently asked questions

How do I remember make and do?

If something new is created and you're left holding a result, use make (make a cake, make a decision). If you're simply carrying out a task and nothing new appears, use do (do homework, do the dishes). The rule works in most cases; the rest are fixed phrases.

Is it "make a mistake" or "do a mistake"?

The correct form is make a mistake. A mistake "appears", so something new comes into being, which means make. The same logic gives make a decision, make a plan, make progress. "Do a mistake" isn't used in English.

Which is correct: "do exercise" or "make exercise"?

do exercise means physical activity. do an exercise means completing an exercise from a book. "Make exercise" is wrong. But make an effort is correct, because an effort is "created".
