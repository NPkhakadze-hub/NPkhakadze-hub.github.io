Present Perfect or Past Simple? How to choose the right tense ⏳

26 October 2026 · Nodar

People often say these two tenses “mean the same thing”, because in many languages they translate the same way. They don't — and once you see the difference, it stops being scary. The distinction is simpler than it looks:

🕰️ I saw him yesterday (a finished moment in the past) → Past Simple
🔗 I have seen him before (experience, linked to now) → Present Perfect

“I saw him yesterday” and “I've seen him” mean different things. The first tells you about a specific event; the second tells you about your experience.

So why do we get it wrong?

There are three reasons — and each one leads to a different kind of mistake.

1. Your language may have just one past form. In many languages, one past tense covers both “I saw” and “I have seen”. English splits that space in two, and the Present Perfect covers more situations than learners expect. So we translate with the logic of our own language — and get it wrong.

2. The form looks familiar — but means something else. Some languages have a “have + past participle” form that looks just like the Present Perfect but is simply the everyday past tense. If that's your language, “I have seen him yesterday” sounds perfectly natural to you. In English it's a mistake: the Present Perfect doesn't go with a finished time. Other learners avoid the Present Perfect altogether because it feels strange to them.

3. The main trap: the present tense. When an action is still going on, many languages use the present tense — “I live here for five years”. In English you need the Present Perfect here. This is the most common mistake of all, and we'll look at it separately below.

🔑 Past Simple — a finished fact in the past. Often with a specific time.
🔑 Present Perfect — a link to the present: experience, an action that is still going on, or a result you can see now.

The same story, two tenses

Imagine you've lost your keys. Both sentences are correct, but they mean different things:

✅ I lost my keys yesterday. — maybe I've found them since. I'm talking about the past.
✅ I've lost my keys. — I lost them and I still don't have them. (The problem exists right now.)

See the difference? The Past Simple tells a story. The Present Perfect says: “This is the situation now.”

Three situations where you need the Present Perfect

1. Experience — whether you've ever done something. Nobody cares when, only whether.

✅ Have you ever been to London?
✅ I've never eaten sushi.

2. An action that is still going on — with for and since.

❌ I live here for five years.
✅ I've lived here for five years. — and I still live here.
✅ I've known her since 2019.

This is the trap we talked about at the start. Many languages use the present tense for something that started in the past and is still going on — English needs the Present Perfect for that. Your first language won't help you here; you simply have to learn it.

3. It has just happened, and the result is visible.

✅ She's just arrived. — she's here.
✅ I've already finished the report. — it's ready.
✅ He hasn't called yet.

Words that tell you which tense to use

This is the most practical trick of all: some words almost always go with one tense.

Past Simple: yesterday · last week/year · in 2019 · two days ago · when I was a child
Present Perfect: ever · never · already · yet · just · so far · since · for (if it's still going on) · recently

One basic rule: if the time period is finished (yesterday, last week, in 2019, two days ago) — Past Simple. If the period isn't over yet (today, this week, this year) or you're using since — usually the Present Perfect.

❌ I've seen him yesterday.
✅ I saw him yesterday.

American and British English

Americans often use the Past Simple where British speakers use the Present Perfect: Did you eat yet? (US) and Have you eaten yet? (UK). Both are understood. In exams (IELTS, CAE) both are accepted — the main thing is to stick to one style throughout your writing.

For past habits — “I lived in the city back then”, “as a child I would play football” — English often uses used to. More on that in the article on used to.

Test yourself

Put the verb in the correct tense. Answers below.

1. I ______ (finish) my homework. Can I go out now?
2. We ______ (go) to Batumi last summer.
3. How long ______ you ______ (work) here? You seem to know everyone.
4. She ______ (never / try) Georgian wine — she's only been here a week.

Answers: 1. have finished — the result matters now (in American English finished is also acceptable). 2. went — last summer is a specific time. 3. have … worked (or have … been working) — the context shows the person still works here. 4. has never tried — experience.

If you got all four right, you've already got the basic logic. If you got two or more wrong, that's completely normal.

One last practical tip: when you're unsure, ask yourself — am I talking about a finished time, or about now? In most cases the answer points you to the right English tense. There are two groups where your first language is most likely to mislead you, and they're worth memorising separately: sentences with for/since (where many languages use the present tense) and the “result visible now” group — I've lost my keys, She's just arrived, I've already finished. Here many languages simply use their ordinary past tense, but English needs the Present Perfect. These two groups are a matter of memorising — and that's exactly what we work on in lessons, using your own examples.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between the Present Perfect and the Past Simple?

The Past Simple describes a finished fact, often with a specific time (yesterday, last week, in 2019): I saw him yesterday. The Present Perfect keeps a link to the present: experience, an action that is still going on, or a result you can see now: I have seen that film.

Isn't the Present Perfect just the normal past tense with "have"?

No. In some languages a have + participle form is the everyday past tense, but in English the Present Perfect always connects the past with now. That is why it can't be used with a finished time: you say I saw him yesterday, not I have seen him yesterday. The other big difficulty is where many languages use the present tense, for example to say how long you have lived somewhere.

Can I say "I have seen him yesterday"?

No. If the sentence contains a specific past time (yesterday), you need the Past Simple: I saw him yesterday.

How do I say I moved here five years ago and still live here?

I have lived here for five years. since almost always needs the Present Perfect. for needs it only when the action is still going on: I've lived here for five years (I still live here) is different from I lived there for five years (I don't live there any more).
