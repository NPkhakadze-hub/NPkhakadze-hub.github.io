Forgot the word? 15 phrases to keep your English conversation going 💭

26 October 2026 · Nodar

You're talking, the conversation is going well, and suddenly, in the middle of a sentence, one word is gone. You stop, the other person waits, the pause gets longer — and in the end you say: “Sorry… I don't know.” And that's the end of the conversation.

This happens to everyone — in their native language too. The difference is that in your own language you automatically find another way round it: “the thing you use to…”, “you know, that thing…”. You can do exactly the same in English. It's a separate skill, and unlike new vocabulary, you don't develop it by memorising — you develop it by practising.

🎯 The goal isn't to find the exact word, it's to get your idea across.
— The other person doesn't need the word you've forgotten. They need your idea.

Why a whole conversation stops on one word

The reason is the habit of translating. You build the sentence in your head in your own language, then translate it word by word, and when one word won't come, the whole sentence stops. In reality, you already know enough English to say the same thing another way — you just haven't practised it yet.

Below are four techniques. Each comes with ready-made phrases — fifteen in total.

1. Buy time — four phrases for thinking

The first step is to fill the silence. When you say that you're thinking, the pause becomes natural and the other person waits calmly:

1. How can I put it…
2. What's the word…
3. It's on the tip of my tongue.
4. Give me a second.

Native speakers of English use these phrases all the time, too. That's why they sound natural and nobody sees them as a sign of weakness.

2. Describe it — five ready-made frames

This is the main technique. Instead of the word, you say what it is, what it's used for or what it's like:

5. It's a kind of…
6. It's something you use to…
7. It's like…, but…
8. It's the opposite of…
9. It's a person who…

Here's what it looks like in a real conversation:

A: I need to buy a… how can I put it… it's something you use to dry your hair.
B: Oh, a hairdryer?
A: Yes, a hairdryer!
— You couldn't remember the word hairdryer, but the conversation didn't stop. And now you'll remember the word much better.

A few more examples with the same frames:

It's like a sofa, but it opens out into a bed. — a sofa bed
It's the opposite of generous. — mean / stingy
It's a person who fixes pipes. — a plumber

3. Swap it for something simpler

Sometimes you don't even need to describe anything — it's enough to take a simple word instead of a difficult one. There are three ways to do it:

A general word. thing, stuff, place, person — with these words you can describe almost anything: a place where you can buy medicine (instead of “pharmacy”), the thing on the wall that shows the time (instead of “clock”).

very + a simple adjective. If enormous won't come, say very big. If exhausted won't — very tired. It sounds a little less elegant, but it's perfectly clear.

not + the opposite. It wasn't very friendly — when you can't think of hostile or rude. It's not expensive — when you can't think of affordable.

4. Ask for help — it's not a weakness

If the other person is right there, they'll be happy to help you. The key is to ask in a way that keeps the conversation going:

10. What do you call the thing that…?
11. Is there a word for…?
12. Sorry, I don't know the word in English.
13. Do you know what I mean?
14. What I mean is…
15. In other words…

The last two phrases come in handy when you did say the word but can see you weren't understood. Then you simply say the same idea again, in different words.

Three habits that kill a conversation

1. Thinking in silence. Five seconds of silence is far more awkward for the other person than one How can I put it…

2. Switching to a word in your own language. If the other person doesn't speak your language, this won't help you at all — it's better to describe the word in English.

3. Apologising too often. One sorry is enough. When you start every sentence with an apology, both you and the other person start thinking that something is going wrong. And that makes the fear of speaking stronger — I have a separate article on the fear of speaking.

How to practise — the “describe it, but don't say it” game

Pick five objects in your room. Describe each one out loud in English without saying its name — 30 seconds per object. Use the frames above: It's something you use to…, It's like…, but…. If you record yourself and listen back later, you'll clearly see where you got stuck.

This is exactly the skill you need most in real conversation. That's why we practise it in conversational English lessons too, and the most useful everyday phrases are collected in the conversational English article.

Test yourself

Which word does each sentence describe?

1. It's something with two blades that you use to cut paper.
2. It's a person who gives injections and looks after patients in a hospital, but isn't a doctor.
3. It's the opposite of cheap.
4. It's like a hotel, but cheaper, and you often share a room with strangers.
5. It's a place where you can borrow books for free.

Answers: 1. scissors 2. a nurse 3. expensive (pricey or costly are also correct) 4. a hostel 5. a library

Notice: every description is built from simple words. When a word slips your mind, this is exactly what you can do too — instead of stopping.

Frequently asked questions

What should I do if I forget a word while speaking English?

Don't stop. First buy time (“How can I put it…”), then describe the word (“It's something you use to…”) or replace it with a simpler one. The other person doesn't need the exact word — they need your idea.

What can I say when a word is almost there but won't come?

It's on the tip of my tongue. Native speakers use this phrase in exactly that situation, and it buys you a few seconds to remember.

Is it rude to ask the other person for a word?

No. “What do you call the thing that…?” or “Is there a word for…?” are completely natural questions, and a word you learn this way stays in your memory better.

How can I practise this skill on my own?

Pick five objects in your room and describe each one in English without saying its name — 30 seconds per object. If you record yourself and listen back, you'll see where you got stuck.
