Business English: 40 phrases for emails, meetings and presentations 📧

26 October 2026 · Nodar

You understand English, but the moment you start a work email, your hand freezes: Dear or Hi? In a meeting you have something to say, but you don't know how to say “I disagree” politely. In this article I've put together 40 phrases, sorted by situation: email, meetings, presentations, small talk.

Where the register matters, I've noted it: formal — with someone you don't know, a client, your boss; neutral — with colleagues and partners you know. Phrases for job interviews are in a separate article.

Email — openings and closings

In English business correspondence there's one main rule: after the greeting and one polite phrase, say in the very first sentence why you're writing. The rest is formulas.

1. Dear Mr Brown, / Dear Ms Brown, — formal; with the surname (in American English with a full stop: Mr. / Ms.). Ms works for any woman, whatever her marital status.
2. Hi Anna, / Hello Anna, — neutral; for colleagues and partners you know.
3. I hope you're well. — neutral; the formal version: I hope this email finds you well.
4. Thank you for your email. / Thanks for getting back to me.
5. I'm writing to ask about… / confirm… — a formal opening.
6. Please find attached the invoice. — formal; neutral: I've attached the invoice.
7. Please let me know if you have any questions.
8. I look forward to hearing from you. — formal; -ing after look forward to!
9. Kind regards, / Best regards, — a universal sign-off; with colleagues, Best, is enough

Email — requests, reminders, apologies

In English, the bigger the request, the longer and more “conditional” the sentence. Send me the file is an order; Could you please send me the file? is a request.

10. Could you please send me the updated file? — neutral.
11. I would be grateful if you could confirm the dates. — a formal request.
12. Would it be possible to move the deadline? — when you're asking for a lot.
13. I'm just following up on my email from last week. — neutral; Did you get my email?! sounds pushy.
14. Just a quick reminder that the deadline is Friday.
15. Sorry for the late reply. / Apologies for the delay. — neutral / formal.
16. I'm afraid I can't make it on Monday. — I'm afraid here means “unfortunately”, not “I'm scared”.

Meetings and calls

In an online meeting the most useful skill is joining the conversation politely: if you didn't catch something, ask; if you disagree, say so — but gently.

17. Can everyone hear me? — Sorry, I was on mute.
18. Thanks for joining, everyone. Shall we get started?
19. I agree with Nino on this. / I completely agree. — agree is a verb — “I'm agree” is a mistake.
20. I see your point, but… — polite disagreement.
21. I'm not sure that would work. — a soft “no”.
22. Sorry, could you repeat that?
23. Sorry, I didn't catch that. — when the sound cut out or someone spoke too fast.
24. So, if I understand correctly, you mean…?
25. Could you go into a bit more detail?
26. To sum up, we've agreed on two things.
27. So, the next steps are… I'll send a summary after the call.

Presentations — signposting phrases

In a presentation, your audience needs “signposts”: where we are and what comes next. That's exactly what these phrases are for (in English, a presentation usually goes with the verb give or deliver — make a presentation is also correct, while do a presentation is informal).

28. Today I'm going to talk about our results for the last quarter.
29. I've divided my talk into three parts.
30. Let's start with the numbers.
31. As you can see from this chart, …
32. This brings me to my next point.
33. Moving on to the budget…
34. In conclusion, … — formal; neutral: To sum up,
35. Thank you for your attention. I'd be happy to take any questions.

Small talk — the two minutes before a meeting

In British and American settings, a meeting rarely starts straight with business. Two or three light phrases are enough — plus one to move on to the business at hand.

36. How was your weekend? — a Monday classic.
37. How was your trip? / Did you have a good flight?
38. How are things at your end? — neutral.
39. Good to see you again. / Nice to meet you. — the second one is for the first time you meet.
40. Right, shall we get down to business? — the move from small talk to business.

What not to say

I come across these forms in business emails all the time — although one of them isn't a mistake at all.

❌ I'm agree with your proposal.
✅ I agree with your proposal.

❌ Dear Mr John,
✅ Dear Mr Smith, / Dear John, — Mr/Ms go only with the surname.

❌ I will inform to you tomorrow.
✅ I will inform you tomorrow. / I'll let you know tomorrow. — inform, just like tell, is followed directly by the person, without to.

❌ I am waiting your reply.
✅ I'm waiting for your reply. — wait for; more formally: I look forward to your reply.

❌ Please revert me.
✅ Please get back to me. — in British and American English, revert means going back to an earlier state, not “reply” — that meaning is found mainly in the business English of India and South-East Asia.

⚠️ Kindly do the needful. — not a mistake: it's an idiom of Indian business English (“please do what's necessary”). For a European or American reader, it's better to write: Could you please take care of this?

Test yourself

Choose the more suitable option or correct the sentence:

1. An email to a client you've never met, Ms Anna Beridze: (a) Hi Anna! · (b) Dear Ms Beridze,
2. I'm agree with your plan. → ?
3. A request to your boss: (a) Send me the report. · (b) Could you please send me the report?
4. I will inform to you tomorrow. → ?
5. I look forward to ___ (hear) from you.

Answers: 1. (b) — with someone you don't know, a formal greeting with the surname. 2. I agree with your plan. 3. (b) — Could you please is a request, Send me is an order. 4. I will inform you tomorrow. (or: I'll let you know tomorrow.) 5. hearing — after look forward to, use -ing.

My advice: from these 40 phrases, pick the ten you really need in your job and consciously use them in your next email and your next meeting — a phrase you've used in a real situation stays in your memory far better. Frequently asked questions

How do I start a business email in English?

With someone you don't know or a client: “Dear Mr/Ms + surname”; with colleagues: “Hi Anna”. In the very first sentence, say why you're writing: “I'm writing to ask about…”.

What should I say in a meeting when I didn't catch what someone said?

“Sorry, could you repeat that?” or “Sorry, I didn't catch that.” To check you've understood: “So, if I understand correctly, you mean…?”. Asking is almost always better than staying silent.

Is “I'm agree” correct?

No. “agree” is a verb, so it's “I agree with you”, without “am”. Likewise: “I will inform you”, not “inform to you”.

Is “Kindly do the needful” a mistake?

No, it's an idiom of Indian business English. With a European or American reader, “Could you please take care of this?” works better.
