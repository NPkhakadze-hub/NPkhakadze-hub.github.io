English for adults — how to start at 30, 40 or 50 🌿

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“I'm over forty — when would I find time for English now?” Or: “I didn't learn it at school, so what chance do I have now?” If you recognise yourself in these sentences, this article is for you. Learning English as an adult is neither an exception nor an act of heroism — it's perfectly ordinary, and I see it in my lessons all the time: people of 30, 40, 50 who are starting, or starting again.

Below I'll write honestly about the myths that hold you back, what really is different for adults, what the first lesson and the first week look like — no promises, just steps.

Three myths I hear most often

“It's too late.” You can be too late for a train, not for a language. An adult has one thing a 14-year-old student doesn't: you know exactly why you need English. That's more than motivation — it's a compass that shows you what to learn first.

“My memory isn't what it used to be.” Memorising words mechanically is often harder for an adult than for a child. On the other hand, adults learn far better what is connected to their own lives: an accountant hears the word invoice once and remembers it, because they hold one in their hands every day. It's not about memory — it's about choosing the right material.

“I didn't learn it at school.” At school you learnt rules, not how to speak — and those two need different skills. What you “didn't learn” often isn't lost at all: I am, I have, I like are still in there somewhere and just need waking up.

What really is different for adults

Less time. Work, family, everyday chores — an adult doesn't have several hours a day for English. That's why English for adults has to be focused: not “a little bit of everything”, but exactly what you need, in the right order.

Fear of making mistakes. A child makes a mistake and carries on; an adult feels embarrassed — especially if they're a professional in their own field and suddenly speak “like a child”. This fear is normal, and I've written about it separately: fear of speaking and how to overcome it.

“School English” that needs waking up. Many adults don't have to start from zero but from “minus one”: they remember words, but together with mistakes. That's neither bad nor good — it's simply how it is, and in the first lessons we work out what to keep and what to correct.

What actually helps adults

In my experience, English for adults brings results when six things come together:

1. A clear goal. Not “I want English”, but “I need to write emails to colleagues” or “I want to talk to my grandchild on video calls”. The goal decides where you start.

2. Your topics. Not the textbook's “John and his dog” — your job, your city, your travel plans.

3. Speaking from day one. Even if it's just three sentences. “First I'll learn, then I'll speak” — in that plan, “first” never ends.

4. Material built around you. Exercises based on your mistakes, words from your field.

5. Homework with explanations. Not just “right / wrong” but why — in plain words, so you don't make the same mistake again.

6. A teacher who explains things simply when you need it. A rule you can grasp in one plain sentence shouldn't take a ten-minute lecture.

Typical situations adults start from

Almost every adult comes with a specific reason — and that reason becomes the topic of the first lessons:

💼 Work emails: Could you send me the report by Friday?
✈️ Travel: I have a reservation for two nights.
🏫 Your child's school abroad: How is my daughter doing in class?
🧳 Moving abroad: I'd like to make an appointment. (at the doctor's, at a public office)
👵 Grandchildren abroad: What did you do at school today?

“School English” habits we correct first

Almost every adult beginner has these mistakes, and none of them is anything to be ashamed of. We simply start by correcting them:

❌ I am agree with you.
✅ I agree with you. (agree is a verb — it doesn't need am)
❌ He don't like it.
✅ He doesn't like it.
❌ I am interesting in this job.
✅ I am interested in this job. (interesting describes the thing, interested describes how you feel)
❌ Can you explain me?
✅ Can you explain it to me?
❌ I very like it.
✅ I really like it. (or: I like it very much)

What the first lesson looks like

There's no lecture in the first lesson. First we talk — in plain, simple words, as much as you need — about why you need English and where you want to use it. Then, with a few simple sentences, we see what has stayed with you: My name is…, I work as…, I live in… And by the end of the lesson you're already saying four or five sentences about yourself — even with mistakes. That's often exactly what didn't happen at school.

Before that, we meet for a free 20-minute intro call on Google Meet — it isn't a test, we just work out where you're starting from and what you need. To find out which level matches what you remember, see the article on English levels.

The first week — steps, not deadlines

I won't give you a timeline, because everyone learns at their own pace. But with adults the order is usually like this:

1. You and your work — I am…, I work as…, I live in… — four or five sentences that you say out loud.

2. The first phrases for your goal — the opening of an email, a hotel dialogue or a question for your grandchild; whatever you need.

3. A review of your “school English” — we see what you remember correctly and what with mistakes; we fix one or two of the habits listed above.

4. Your first homework — short, on your topic, with explanations; in the next lesson you say out loud what you wrote.

If you're starting completely from zero and remember nothing, the exact list of first phrases is in this article: English for beginners. English from zero for adults starts with the same verbs — be, have, like, want, can — the examples simply come from your own life.

Check yourself

Choose the correct option in brackets:

1. I ___ with you. (agree / am agree)
2. My husband ___ speak German. (don't / doesn't)
3. I am ___ in this course. (interesting / interested)
4. I ___ this film. (very like / really like)
5. My daughter ___ in London now. (live / lives)

Answers: 1. agree. 2. doesn't. 3. interested. 4. really like. 5. lives.

One last tip: today, write down one specific situation where you need English — an email, a call, a trip — and say out loud three sentences you'd need there. Frequently asked questions

Isn't it too late to learn English after 40 or 50?

No. Adults often find mechanical memorising harder, but they know exactly why they need the language and learn much better what is connected to their work and life.

I studied English at school but learnt nothing — do I have to start from zero?

Usually not — more like from “minus one”: the basics (I am, I have, I like) are still there somewhere, you just remember them together with mistakes. In the first lessons we work out what to keep and what to correct.

How is learning English different for adults?

Adults have less time, so lessons have to be built around their specific goal; they are also more afraid of mistakes and often have “school English” that needs waking up. Everything else is the same — speaking from day one.

What does the first lesson look like for an adult?

No lecture: we talk in plain, simple words about why you need English, see from a few simple sentences what has stayed with you, and by the end of the lesson you are already saying four or five sentences about yourself. Before that, there is a free 20-minute intro call on Google Meet.
