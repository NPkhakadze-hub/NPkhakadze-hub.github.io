“I'm agree” — one of the most common mistakes English learners make 🤔

26 October 2026 · Nodar

If you've ever said “I'm agree with you”, you're not alone. It's probably the number one mistake I hear from my students, and the reason is logical: in many languages agreement is expressed with “to be” — literally “I am in agreement” — but in English agree is a verb, just like like or want. You wouldn't say “I'm like pizza”, would you? Well, “I'm agree” sounds exactly like that.

❌ I'm agree with you.
✅ I agree with you.
❌ She isn't agree.
✅ She doesn't agree.
❌ Are you agree?
✅ Do you agree?

The rule in one line: agree = a verb → in negatives and questions you need do / does / did, not am / is / are.

One more nuance: agree with a person or an opinion (I agree with Nino), agree to a plan or a proposal (They agreed to the plan). And if you really want to use “am”, there is the form “I'm in agreement” — but it's formal and sounds odd in everyday conversation.

✍️ Mini quiz: correct the mistake: “My parents aren't agree with me.” (Answer: My parents don't agree with me.)

Where this mistake comes from

In many languages agreement is expressed as “I am” + an adjective or a phrase, something like “I am in agreement”. Your brain automatically looks for the same structure in English and builds “I am agree”. It's a logical mistake, not a careless one — and that's exactly why it's so stubborn.

Interestingly, the same trap appears with a few other words: disagree is exactly the same case (it's a verb and doesn't need be), while with interested and worried it's the other way round — here you do need be, but the form has to end in -ed:

❌ I'm disagree. → ✅ I disagree.
❌ I'm interesting in music. → ✅ I'm interested in music.
❌ I'm worry about it. → ✅ I'm worried about it.

agree with, agree to or agree on

Once you're using the verb correctly, the next step is the preposition — and here there are three options:

✅ agree with + a person or an opinion · I agree with Nino. / I agree with your idea.
✅ agree to + a proposal, a plan · They agreed to the plan.
✅ agree on + the point you reached agreement about · We agreed on a price.

How to agree nicely

“I agree” is correct and natural — but in real conversation these options are useful too:

✅ Exactly. / Absolutely.
✅ That's a good point.
✅ I couldn't agree more. (the strongest option)
✅ I see what you mean. (partial agreement)

The last phrase is especially useful when you don't fully agree but don't want to sound rude: I see what you mean, but…

Check yourself

1. ___ with you about the price. (agreement, I, Present Simple)
2. She ___ agree with the decision yesterday, and she still doesn't. (negative, Past Simple)
3. ___ you agree with me now? (Present Simple question)
4. They finally agreed ___ a date.

Answers: 1. I agree. 2. didn't. 3. Do (Don't is correct too — the key thing is do, not are). 4. on (upon is correct too; to is possible if one side accepted a date the other proposed, but on is the usual choice).

This one mistake is a good example of how a language habit works: I know the rule, but when I speak, “am” still slips out. That's why one explanation isn't enough — you need to say I agree several times in real conversation in the same week. That's when the habit changes.

Frequently asked questions

Why is “I'm agree” wrong?

Because in English agree is a verb, just like like or want. You wouldn't say “I'm like pizza”, would you? The correct form is: I agree with you.

How do I say that I don't agree?

I don't agree or I disagree — both are correct. “I'm not agree” is wrong, because agree is a verb and the negative needs do/does. A softer option: I see what you mean, but…

agree with or agree to?

agree with a person or an opinion (I agree with Nino). agree to a proposal or a plan (They agreed to the plan). agree on the point you reached agreement about (We agreed on a price).
