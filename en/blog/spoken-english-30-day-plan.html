Spoken English for adults — a 30-day plan 🗣️

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“I know the grammar, but I can't speak” — this is the most common sentence I hear from adult learners. It isn't a knowledge problem; it's a habit problem. Below is a thirty-day plan that builds exactly that habit.

Two honest notes first. One: in thirty days you won't become a fluent speaker — anyone who promises you that is lying. Two: in thirty days you can break down the block that stops you from starting to speak. That is exactly the goal of this plan.

⏱️ 15–20 minutes a day. You don't need more.
— What matters is frequency, not length.

Why we can't speak even though we know the rules

The reason is that there's a long way between knowledge and automaticity. When you remember a rule, building a sentence takes a few seconds: first you think in your own language, then you translate, then you check the tense, then the preposition. In a real conversation those seconds don't exist — the other person expects an answer right away.

That's why speaking practice isn't about collecting new knowledge. It's about speeding up what you already know: using the same rule so many times that it no longer needs thinking.

And one more thing: what often gets in the way of speaking is fear, not lack of knowledge. Fear of a mistake creates the same pause as an unknown word — it just happens in your head. The first week of this plan is devoted to exactly that part.

Week 1 — breaking the silence

The only goal of the first week is to make speaking English out loud an ordinary thing to do. We're not thinking about accuracy yet.

  • Days 1–3. Choose one everyday topic (yesterday, your job, your favourite dish) and speak about it out loud for 2 minutes. Record it on your phone. Don't listen back.
  • Days 4–5. The same, but now listen to the recording. Note only the places where you stopped — not the mistakes.
  • Days 6–7. At each place where you stopped, find the word you were missing. Write them in a list — this is your first personal dictionary.

By the end of the week something small but important usually happens: speaking out loud no longer feels awkward.

Week 2 — ready-made chunks

Real speech isn't built from separate words — it's made of ready-made chunks. That's exactly why native speakers talk fast: they don't build every sentence from scratch.

✅ To be honest, …
✅ It depends on …
✅ I was about to …
✅ The thing is, …
✅ I'd rather …
  • Days 8–10. Take two chunks a day and say five sentences with each — about your own life, not in the abstract.
  • Days 11–12. Go back to the topics from week 1, but now work the chunks in.
  • Days 13–14. A two-minute monologue on a new topic. Goal: use at least three chunks naturally.

Week 3 — no stopping

The key skill this week is not to stop talking when you forget a word. We forget words in our native language too — we simply say it another way.

🔁 Can't remember “screwdriver”? — the tool you use to fix a chair
🔁 Can't remember “landlord”? — the person who owns the flat I rent
— This technique is a separate skill, and it can be trained.
  • Days 15–17. Choose five difficult words and describe each one in English without using the word itself.
  • Days 18–19. A two-minute monologue with one rule: “no stopping”. If you forget a word — describe it and keep going.
  • Days 20–21. A three-minute monologue. Record it and listen back: now you note the mistakes as well.

Week 4 — real situations

The last week is about specific situations that really happen, or will happen, in your life.

  • Days 22–24. Three situations: a request, a disagreement, an explanation. For each, a dialogue of 5–6 lines — you play both sides yourself.
  • Days 25–27. A “difficult” situation: sorting out a problem on the phone, clearing up a misunderstanding, saying no politely.
  • Days 28–29. Repeat the topic from day 1 — the same two minutes, the same topic.
  • Day 30. Listen to your first and your last recording one after the other.

This last step is the most important. Progress isn't visible from day to day — you only see it when you compare. That's exactly why it's worth keeping your first recording.

Three mistakes that slow the plan down

1. Practising silently. Building a sentence in your head isn't speaking. The muscles of your voice, your breathing and your rhythm only get trained out loud — so silent reading doesn't count in this plan.

2. Refusing to listen to the recording. Almost everyone hates hearing their own voice. But it's the recording that shows you what you don't notice while speaking: where a thought breaks off and which word you're missing.

3. “I'll learn first, then I'll speak.” This is the most common trap. Speaking isn't the stage that comes after knowledge — it teaches you things a book can't.

What to do if you miss a day

You will miss one — that's normal. The main thing is not to “make up” for it the next day with a double load: a 40-minute session can't replace what two separate days do.

Just carry on from where you stopped. If you miss more than three days in a row, go back to the last task of the previous week and continue from there — that one day isn't a loss, it's revision.

What isn't in this plan, and why

New grammar. These thirty days are about the speaking habit. Grammar runs in parallel, separately — if you're already studying it.

Word lists. The only list we make is your personal one: the words you were missing in your own speaking.

Feedback. This is the real weak spot of the plan, and it's worth saying directly: you can't fix a mistake you don't notice. That's exactly why a live conversation partner — a teacher or a language partner — gives you what practising on your own can't.

A good starting point for practising real situations is small talk — the short conversation that almost every interaction starts with in an English-speaking environment. How to start it, keep it going and end it, I've explained in a separate article.

What to expect after 30 days

The realistic result looks like this: starting to speak won't be hard any more, the pauses will get shorter, and you'll have five or six phrases that come to you automatically.

What won't happen: your accent won't disappear, your vocabulary won't grow dramatically, and difficult topics will still be hard. That's normal too — this plan is a first step, not the whole journey.

If after this you want to work systematically, there's more about one-to-one conversational English lessons on a separate page. And I have a separate article on the fear of speaking.

Frequently asked questions

Will I speak fluently in 30 days?

No — and anyone who promises you that is lying. In 30 days you can break down the block that stops you from starting to speak: the pauses get shorter and a few phrases start coming to you automatically.

How much time does it take per day?

15–20 minutes. What matters is frequency, not length: seven short sessions give you far more than one long “catch-up” a week.

Why can't I speak if I know the rules?

Because there's a long way between knowledge and automaticity. In a real conversation you don't get the seconds it takes to remember a rule. Speaking practice doesn't add new knowledge — it speeds up what you already have.

Do I really have to listen to the recording?

Yes. It's the recording that shows you what you don't notice while speaking: where a thought breaks off and which word you're missing.

What should I do if I forget a word?

Describe it in other words and keep going — we do the same in our native language. It's a separate skill, and week 3 of this plan is devoted to it.
