Used to, be used to, get used to — the difference explained ⏳

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“As a child I used to spend my summers in the village”, “I'm already used to getting up early”, “I'm slowly getting used to my new job” — in many languages these three ideas are expressed in three completely different ways. In English, though, all three are built on the same used to. That's exactly why these three structures get mixed up so often.

Below we'll go through each one, and compare used to with would and the Past Simple. At the end — a short quiz.

used to — what was true before and isn't any more

used to + the first form of the verb (V1) refers to something that happened or was true in the past but is no longer the case — whether it's a repeated action (a habit) or a long-lasting state.

✅ I used to smoke, but I quit five years ago.
✅ We used to live in Batumi. — (We don't live there any more.)
✅ There used to be a cinema on this corner.

Form: positive, negative, question

In positive sentences used to is the same for every person (I/she/they used to). In negatives and questions, as in the Past Simple, did appears. Because did already carries the past tense, used loses its -d: didn't use to, Did you use to…?

✅ She didn't use to like coffee.
✅ Did you use to play football at school?
⚠️ She didn't used to like coffee. — People often write it this way, but didn't use to is considered the standard form. Use it in exams.

Pronunciation: used to is pronounced /ˈjuːstə/ — with an s, not a z (whereas the verb use, “to make use of”, is /juːz/). The -d disappears into the following t, so use to and used to sound the same. In writing, though, don't drop the -d.

used to or would

For repeated actions in the past you can use would as well as used to — especially in memories and storytelling.

✅ Every summer we used to go to the village. We would swim in the river all day.

But would only works with actions. With state verbs (live, be, have, know, like) it doesn't fit — here you need used to.

❌ I would live in Batumi when I was a child.
✅ I used to live in Batumi when I was a child.

Besides, would needs context: first used to or the Past Simple shows that we're talking about the past, and then would carries the story on. Without context, I would play football is understood as a conditional: “I would play football (if…)”. I write about other meanings of would, such as polite requests, in the article on modal verbs.

used to or Past Simple

used to means a habit or a long-lasting state. For a one-off action, or when you say how many times something happened, you need the Past Simple.

❌ I used to visit London three times last year.
✅ I visited London three times last year.
✅ I used to visit London three times a year. — (Frequency, i.e. a habit — used to is correct here.)

That said, the Past Simple is also correct for past habits if the context shows the action was repeated: When I was a student, I walked to university every day. So used to isn't compulsory here — it simply stresses that things aren't like that any more.

be used to and get used to — “accustomed to” and “becoming accustomed”

be used to means “to be accustomed to”: something is normal for me and no longer difficult. get used to means the process of getting accustomed: something unusual gradually becomes normal.

The key rule: here to is a preposition, not part of the verb. So it is followed by a noun, a pronoun or a verb in the -ing form. V1 doesn't go here.

✅ I'm used to the cold.
❌ I'm used to wake up early.
✅ I'm used to waking up early.
✅ She isn't used to working at night.

get used to works in every tense, like any other verb:

✅ I'm getting used to my new job.
✅ I got used to the noise after a few weeks.
✅ Don't worry — you'll get used to it.

Note: This knife is used to cut bread is correct, but it's something else — the verb use in the passive: “people use this knife to cut bread”. It has nothing to do with getting accustomed.

How to tell them apart by meaning

A quick meaning check helps here: each structure answers a different question. used to ≈ “in the past, regularly or for a long time — but not now”; words like before, back then, when I was a child often go with it. If your language uses one past form for both habits and actions in progress, be careful: an action that was in progress at a moment in the past is not used to but the Past Continuous — I was watching TV when you called. What points to used to is the idea of “before”, “regularly” or “not any more”.

be used to ≈ “accustomed to” — a state. get used to ≈ “becoming accustomed” (a process), “got used to” (the result), “will get used to” (the future).

Quick summary

used to + V1 — it was like that before, not any more: I used to work at night.
be used to + -ing / noun — I'm accustomed to it: I'm used to working at night.
get used to + -ing / noun — I'm becoming accustomed / I became accustomed: I'm getting used to working at night.
would + V1 — a repeated past action (not for states): We would play outside for hours.

Typical mistakes

1. use to for present habits. used to refers only to the past. A present habit is expressed by the Present Simple, often together with usually.

❌ I use to get up at seven.
✅ I usually get up at seven.

2. A missing be or -d. “I'm accustomed to it” can't be said without be; and the -d isn't heard, but it is written.

❌ I used to the noise now.
✅ I'm used to the noise now.
❌ I'm use to it.
✅ I'm used to it.

3. The -ing form after used to. When used to expresses a past habit or state — that is, when there's neither be nor get in front of it — it is followed only by V1.

❌ I used to living in a village.
✅ I used to live in a village.

Check yourself

Choose the correct option or put the verb in the right form. Each item has one answer.

1. When I was a child, we ___ in a small flat in Kutaisi. (a) would live (b) used to live (c) use to live
2. I've lived in London for ten years, so I ___ the rain. (a) used to (b) am used to (c) use to
3. "___ you use to play an instrument at school?" (a) Do (b) Did (c) Were
4. It was hard at first, but after a month I got used to ___ (drive) on the left.
5. Last summer I ___ to Italy twice. (a) used to go (b) went (c) would go
6. Tom ___ like vegetables, but now he eats them every day. (a) didn't use to (b) wasn't used to (c) doesn't use to

Answers: 1. b — live is a state verb, so would doesn't fit; use to without the -d is a mistake. 2. b — “accustomed to” = be used to. 3. b — a question about the past: Did + use to. 4. driving — after get used to the verb takes the -ing form. 5. b — an exact number (twice), so the Past Simple. 6. a — he didn't like vegetables before, but now he eats them every day: didn't use to + V1.

A practical tip: write three sentences about yourself — one about something you did before but don't any more (I used to…), one about something you've already got used to (I'm used to…), and one about something you're still getting used to (I'm getting used to…). Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between used to and be used to?

used to + V1 refers to something that happened or was true in the past but isn't any more: I used to smoke. be used to means “to be accustomed to” and is followed by a noun or a verb in the -ing form: I'm used to waking up early.

Is it didn't use to or didn't used to?

didn't use to is considered the standard form: did already carries the past tense, so used loses its -d. didn't used to is common but non-standard — write didn't use to in exams. The same goes for questions: Did you use to…?

When can I use would instead of used to?

For repeated past actions, especially in memories and storytelling: We would swim in the river all day. With state verbs (live, be, have, know, like) would doesn't fit — I would live in Batumi when I was a child is a mistake; the correct sentence is I used to live in Batumi when I was a child.

Why is “I'm used to wake up early” wrong?

In be used to and get used to, to is a preposition, not part of the verb, so it is followed by a verb in the -ing form: I'm used to waking up early, I'm getting used to working at night.
