English pronunciation — difficult sounds, word stress and how to practise 👄

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“I knew the words, I built the sentence correctly, and the other person still didn't understand me” — I've heard this story from students many times. The reason often isn't grammar but pronunciation. In many languages a word is read almost exactly as it's written, so we read English words letter by letter too. In English, though, spelling and pronunciation often don't match. On top of that, some English sounds simply don't exist in many other languages.

Before we start, one important thing: an accent is not a mistake. People all over the world speak English with an accent and understand each other perfectly well. The goal isn't to sound like a British or American speaker, but to be easy to understand. Below are the sounds and traps that trip learners up most often, and at the end — ways to practise.

th — a sound many languages don't have

English th stands for two sounds: voiceless /θ/ (think, three, month) and voiced /ð/ (this, they, mother). Many languages have neither, so learners often replace them with a familiar sound: /θ/ with t or s, /ð/ with d or z. And with that kind of swap you sometimes end up with a completely different word.

❌ /tɪŋk/, /sɪŋk/ → ✅ /θɪŋk/ think — /sɪŋk/ is a different word: sink
❌ /triː/ → ✅ /θriː/ three — /triː/ = tree
❌ /deɪ/ → ✅ /ðeɪ/ they — /deɪ/ = day
❌ /zɪs/ → ✅ /ðɪs/ this

How to say it: lightly touch the back of your upper front teeth with the tip of your tongue (or put it slightly between your teeth) and let the air out slowly. Don't press your tongue hard, or you'll get t or d. /θ/ is said without voice, /ð/ with voice: put your hand on your throat and you'll feel a vibration on /ð/.

w or v — wine and vine

In many languages there's only one sound in this area — a v, or something between v and w that changes with its position in the word. In English, /v/ and /w/ are two separate sounds, and they distinguish words from each other.

When you say /v/, your upper teeth touch your lower lip. When you say /w/, your teeth do not touch your lip: round your lips as if you were saying “oo”, and move straight on to the next vowel.

wine /waɪn/ · vine /vaɪn/
west /west/ · vest /vest/ — an undershirt (UK), a waistcoat (US)
wet /wet/ · vet /vet/ — an animal doctor

/w/ appears in very common words: we, what, where, when, why, will, would, work, want. Sometimes you can't even see it in the spelling: one /wʌn/.

p, t, k — with a puff of air

Consonants like p, t, k can be said in two ways: aspirated, with a small puff of air after the sound, or unaspirated, without that puff. In English, p, t, k at the beginning of a word and at the start of a stressed syllable are followed by a puff of air — they're aspirated.

In some languages, such as Spanish, French or Russian, p, t, k are normally said without that puff of air, and speakers carry the habit over into English. As a result, to an English-speaking ear park, tea or pen can sound a bit like bark, dee or Ben.

⚠️ pen, tea, key — without a puff of air — people will understand you, but it sounds foreign
✅ pen ≈ /pʰen/, tea ≈ /tʰiː/, key ≈ /kʰiː/ — aspirated: a small breath of air right after p, t, k

Test it with a sheet of paper: hold it in front of your mouth and say pen. If the paper moves, you're saying it right. One exception: after s (speak, stop, sky) the puff of air almost disappears, so the rule doesn't apply here.

Long and short vowels — ship and sheep

Many languages have fewer vowels than English, and in many of them vowel length doesn't change the meaning of a word. In English, though, /iː/ and /ɪ/, /uː/ and /ʊ/ are different sounds. The difference isn't only in length: /iː/ is a long, tense “ee” (spread your lips slightly, as if smiling), while /ɪ/ is short and relaxed — roughly between “ee” and “e”. Likewise, /uː/ is a long “oo”, while /ʊ/ is short and relaxed.

ship /ʃɪp/ · sheep /ʃiːp/
live /lɪv/ · leave /liːv/
full /fʊl/ · fool /fuːl/

Context often saves you, but I want to live here and I want to leave here are completely different things: staying and going.

Schwa /ə/ — why English isn't read the way it's written

In many languages every vowel is pronounced in full: banana is said with three clear “a” sounds. In English banana, though, only the stressed vowel is pronounced in full: /bəˈnɑːnə/ (UK), /bəˈnænə/ (US). The other two a's turn into schwa — /ə/.

Schwa is a short, relaxed, unclear vowel that appears only in unstressed syllables. It's the most common vowel in English, and it can be spelt with any vowel letter: about /əˈbaʊt/, police /pəˈliːs/, support /səˈpɔːt/ (UK), /səˈpɔːrt/ (US). Some syllables disappear altogether:

❌ “ve-ge-ta-ble” → ✅ /ˈvedʒtəbl/ vegetable — usually three syllables
❌ “com-for-ta-ble” → ✅ /ˈkʌmftəbl/ comfortable — usually three syllables, stress on the first
❌ “cho-co-la-te” → ✅ /ˈtʃɒklət/ (UK), /ˈtʃɑːklət/ (US) chocolate — usually two syllables

The same thing happens to small words in a sentence — articles, prepositions, conjunctions, auxiliary verbs. a cup of tea sounds like this: /ə ˈkʌp əv ˈtiː/. This matters for listening too: in I can swim, can is weak — /kən/, while in I can't swim, can't is stressed and full: /kɑːnt/ (UK), /kænt/ (US).

Silent letters

In English many letters are written but not pronounced. Some of them follow a rule:

kn- at the beginning of a word — you don't hear the k: knife /naɪf/, know, knee
wr- at the beginning of a word — you don't hear the w: write /raɪt/, wrong
-mb at the end of a word — you don't hear the b: climb /klaɪm/, thumb /θʌm/

Others you simply have to remember:

✅ island /ˈaɪlənd/ — you don't hear the s
✅ listen /ˈlɪsn/ — you don't hear the t
✅ Wednesday /ˈwenzdeɪ/ — you don't hear the first d or the second e, so the word has two syllables

Word stress — PHOtograph, phoTOgraphy

In some languages stress is weak or always falls in the same place, and it doesn't change the meaning of a word. In English the stressed syllable is said louder, longer and at a slightly higher pitch, while unstressed syllables are weakened — often down to schwa. That's why stress on the wrong syllable makes a word very hard to recognise, even if you say every sound correctly.

Even in words with the same root, the stress moves:

PHOtograph /ˈfəʊtəɡrɑːf/ (UK), /ˈfoʊtəɡræf/ (US) — a picture
phoTOgraphy /fəˈtɒɡrəfi/ (UK), /fəˈtɑːɡrəfi/ (US) — the art or activity
photoGRAphic /ˌfəʊtəˈɡræfɪk/ (UK), /ˌfoʊtəˈɡræfɪk/ (US) — the adjective

Notice: in photography the first o is already a schwa, because the stress has moved to the second syllable.

In some pairs the stress shows the part of speech: the noun often has the stress on the first syllable, the verb on the second.

✅ a REcord /ˈrekɔːd/ (UK), /ˈrekɚd/ (US) — a recording; the best result ever (e.g. a world record)
✅ to reCORD /rɪˈkɔːd/ (UK), /rɪˈkɔːrd/ (US) — to make a recording

The -ed ending: /t/, /d/ or /ɪd/

The Past Simple -ed is pronounced in three ways — depending on the previous sound, not the letter:

/t/ — after a voiceless consonant (except /t/; e.g. /p/, /k/, /f/, /s/, /ʃ/, /tʃ/): worked, stopped, laughed, washed, watched
/d/ — after a voiced consonant (except /d/) and after a vowel: played, lived, called, opened
/ɪd/ — only after /t/ and /d/: wanted, needed, started, decided

The main mistake is an extra syllable: worked and played have one syllable.

❌ /ˈwɜːkɪd/ → ✅ /wɜːkt/ worked (US /wɝːkt/)
❌ /ˈpleɪɪd/ → ✅ /pleɪd/ played
✅ /ˈwɒntɪd/ wanted (US /ˈwɑːntɪd/) — two syllables are correct here

How to practise

1. Minimal pairs. That's the name for pairs of words that differ by just one sound: ship/sheep, think/sink, wine/vine, wet/vet. First learn to tell them apart by ear, then to say them. To check yourself, switch on English voice typing on your phone and say the word: if it types sink instead of think, you'll know what to work on.

2. Dictionary audio. Look up a new word in the Cambridge Dictionary or Oxford Learner's Dictionaries: both have British and American audio and an IPA transcription. Write down the stress mark (ˈ) along with the word — it comes before the stressed syllable.

3. Shadowing. Listen to a short recording and follow the speaker almost at the same time — at the same speed, with the same stress and intonation. I write about this method in more detail in the article on learning with films and series.

4. Recording your own voice. Record yourself reading a paragraph and compare it with the original — the next day you'll notice your mistakes more easily.

And finally: don't work on everything at once. First pick the thing that most often stops people from understanding you, and practise a little, but every day.

Check yourself

Choose the correct option. Each item has only one correct answer.

1. In which verb is -ed pronounced /ɪd/? (a) worked (b) played (c) needed
2. How many syllables are there in watched? (a) one (b) two
3. Which syllable is stressed in photography? (a) PHO (b) TO (c) GRA
4. Can I record our lesson? — here record is a verb. Which syllable is stressed? (a) RE (b) CORD
5. Which letter is silent in island? (a) i (b) s (c) d
6. Our dog is ill — we need to take him to the ___. (a) wet (b) vet
7. Which word has the voiced /ð/? (a) think (b) three (c) mother
8. What happens with the p at the start of English pen? (a) no puff of air follows it (b) a small puff of air follows it

Answers: 1. c — need ends in the sound /d/, so /ˈniːdɪd/. 2. a — /tʃ/ is voiceless, so -ed = /t/: /wɒtʃt/ (UK), /wɑːtʃt/ (US). 3. b — phoTOgraphy. 4. b — the verb has the stress on the second syllable: reCORD. 5. b — /ˈaɪlənd/. 6. b — a vet is an animal doctor, while wet means not dry. 7. c — think and three begin with voiceless /θ/. 8. b — at the start of a word p is aspirated.

A practical tip: pick out five words from this article that you used to pronounce differently, listen to them in a dictionary and say them out loud several times a day — in a sentence, not on their own. Frequently asked questions

Why is the English th so hard to pronounce?

The sounds /θ/ and /ð/ don't exist in many languages, so learners often replace them with t, s, d or z. Lightly touch your upper front teeth with the tip of your tongue and let the air out slowly: /θ/ is said without voice, /ð/ with voice.

How is the -ed ending pronounced?

In three ways: /t/ after a voiceless consonant other than /t/ (worked), /d/ after a voiced consonant other than /d/ and after a vowel (played), /ɪd/ only after /t/ and /d/ (wanted). An extra syllable appears only in the third case.

Do I need to speak English without an accent?

No. An accent is not a mistake — what matters is that people understand you easily. For that, the sounds that change the meaning of a word and correct word stress are especially important.

How can I practise pronunciation on my own?

Listen to words in the Cambridge or Oxford online dictionary, practise with minimal pairs (ship/sheep, think/sink), use shadowing and record your own voice to compare it with the original.
