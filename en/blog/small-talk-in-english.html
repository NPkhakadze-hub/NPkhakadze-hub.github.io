Small talk in English — how to start, keep going and end a conversation ☕

26 October 2026 · Nodar

In a lift, in the office kitchen, before an online meeting starts or with a stranger at a party — in the English-speaking world, a conversation almost always starts with small talk. It's a short, light chat about the weather, the weekend, work.

Many people find this strange: “Why should I talk about the weather?” But small talk isn't about exchanging information — it's about building a connection. People who are good at it are easy to talk to, both at work and when living abroad.

Every piece of small talk has three parts: starting, keeping it going and finishing. Each one has its own phrases.

1. How to start

The easiest way to start a conversation is with something you both share: the place, the weather, the event you're both at.

Lovely day, isn't it?
Is this your first time here?
How do you know Anna? — (at a party — Anna is the host)
Have you tried the coffee here?
Busy day?

Notice: none of these questions needs a long answer. Their only job is to get the conversation started.

2. How to keep it going — answer, detail, question

The most common problem isn't starting a conversation — it's that the conversation ends at the second sentence. The reason is a short answer:

A: Do you like living here?
B: Yes.
— Grammatically correct, but the conversation stops here: the other person has to think of a new question.

The solution is a simple formula: answer + one detail + a question back.

A: Do you like living here?
B: Yes, I do — it's quiet, and the people are really friendly. What about you? Have you lived here long?
— Now the other person has something to say, and the conversation keeps going on its own.

And a few more phrases that keep a conversation alive:

Oh, really? How come? — (= Why?)
What do you like most about it?
That must be interesting.
So what got you into that? — (= What made you interested in it?)

“How are you?” — a common trap

In English, How are you? is often just a greeting, not a real question about your health. The answer is short and positive — and it's followed by a question back.

⚠️ I'm normal. — Native speakers don't answer like this, and it sounds odd: it can even be heard as “I'm a normal person”.
✅ Pretty good, thanks. And you?
✅ Not bad, thanks.
✅ Can't complain. — (= I'm fine.)
✅ A bit tired, to be honest, but good.

Topics small talk avoids

The point of small talk is for both people to feel comfortable. That's why, with a stranger or a new colleague, people don't usually bring up salary, age, politics, religion or private life. Questions like “How much do you earn?” or “When are you getting married?” often come across as awkward in English-speaking settings.

Safe topics: the weather, travel, films and series, food, sport, weekend plans, work (in general, without mentioning pay).

3. How to finish

Ending a conversation politely is a skill in itself. A sudden OK, bye sounds abrupt; it's better to use one transition phrase:

Anyway, I'll let you get back to work.
I should get going.
It was great to meet you.
See you around!

Small talk in an online meeting

The first minute or two of an online meeting, while everyone is joining, is classic small talk time. Three phrases will come in handy:

Where are you joining from today?
How's the weather over there?
Did you have a good weekend?

Other work phrases — opening a meeting, giving your opinion, summing up — are collected in 40 business English phrases.

Test yourself

Choose the most natural answer:

1. A: Lovely day, isn't it? — B: ___ (a) Yes, it is! Perfect for a walk. (b) Yes, I am.
2. A: How are you? — B: ___ (a) I'm normal. (b) Pretty good, thanks. And you?
3. A: Where are you joining from today? — B: ___ (a) From Tbilisi — it's really hot here today! (b) Tbilisi.
4. Which ending is more polite? (a) OK, bye. (b) Anyway, I should get going. It was great talking to you.
5. A: I've just moved here from Berlin. — B: ___ (a) Oh, really? How come? (b) Oh, really? How come you?

Answers: 1. (a) 2. (b) 3. (a) — “Tbilisi.” is correct, but it ends the conversation right there; (a) adds a detail and gives the other person something to respond to. 4. (b) 5. (a)

You can't learn small talk from a book — only by talking. Frequently asked questions

What is small talk?

A short, light conversation about the weather, the weekend or work — in a lift, at the office, at a party or before an online meeting starts. Its goal isn't to exchange information but to build a connection.

How should I answer “How are you?”

Briefly and positively, with a question back: “Pretty good, thanks. And you?” or “Not bad, thanks.” People don't answer with “I'm normal” — it sounds odd.

How do I stop a conversation ending at the second sentence?

Use the formula: answer + one detail + a question back. For example: “Yes, I do — it's quiet. What about you?”

Which topics are avoided in small talk?

With a stranger or a new colleague, people don't usually bring up salary, age, politics, religion or private life.
