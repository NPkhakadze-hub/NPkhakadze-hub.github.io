How to ask questions in English — word order and do/does/did ❓

6 November 2026 · Nodar

In many languages, asking a question is easy: you keep the same word order as in a statement and just change your intonation. In English this works only for quick checking questions (You're leaving?); a normal question needs a different word order. Where you live? is understandable, but it's wrong — it's missing a small word: do.

Questions are one of the first things I check in a lesson, because this mistake shows up at almost every level. In this article I'll explain one pattern that fits most English questions, and three cases where the pattern changes.

One pattern for all tenses

This is the basis of an English question:

(question word) + auxiliary verb + subject + main verb + …?

The auxiliary verb moves in front of the subject. Which auxiliary you need depends on the tense:

✅ Do you speak English? — Present Simple
✅ Is she working today? — Present Continuous
✅ Did they call you? — Past Simple
✅ Have you ever been to London? — Present Perfect
✅ Will you come tomorrow? — future
✅ Can I ask a question? — modal verb

The question word (what, where, when, why, how, who) goes at the start of this pattern, and usually nothing else changes (the exception is below): Where do you work? Why is she working today?

do, does, did — the most common mistake

In Present Simple and Past Simple statements, the auxiliary verb is invisible: You live in Tbilisi. She works here. They called you. In a question, it has to appear:

❌ Where you live?
✅ Where do you live?
❌ What time she starts work?
✅ What time does she start work?
❌ Why you didn't tell me?
✅ Why didn't you tell me?

An important detail: does and did already show the person and the tense, so the main verb stays in its base form — no -s and no past form:

❌ Does she works here?
✅ Does she work here?
❌ Did you went to the party?
✅ Did you go to the party?

to be — its own auxiliary

Am, is, are, was, were do the job of the auxiliary themselves, so do isn't used here. The verb simply moves in front of the subject:

❌ Do you are tired?
✅ Are you tired?
❌ Where did you were yesterday?
✅ Where were you yesterday?

Who? What? — when the question is about the subject

Here the pattern changes. When who or what is itself the subject — when we're asking who did something — we don't need do, and the word order stays the same as in a statement:

✅ Who called you? — (someone called you — who?)
✅ Who did you call? — (you called someone — who?)
✅ What happened?

Compare: Who called you? and Who did you call? are completely different questions. Some languages show this difference with the form of the question word itself (a bit like who and whom in formal English); in everyday English, it's shown by whether do is there or not.

⚠️ Who did call you? — you sometimes hear it for emphasis (“So who did call you?”), but in a normal question it's wrong.

Indirect questions — Can you tell me…?

A polite question often starts with Can you tell me…, Do you know…, I wonder…. In that case, the second part loses its question form and goes back to statement word order:

❌ Can you tell me where is the station?
✅ Can you tell me where the station is?
❌ Do you know what time does the shop open?
✅ Do you know what time the shop opens?

This mistake is very common even at B1–B2 level, because your brain is in “question mode”. Remember: you've already asked the question with Can you tell me, so the second part is an ordinary statement.

Short answers — Yes, I do

When a question starts with an auxiliary verb, the natural short answer repeats the same auxiliary:

✅ Do you like coffee? — Yes, I do. / No, I don't.
✅ Is he at home? — Yes, he is. / No, he isn't.
✅ Have you finished? — Yes, I have. / No, I haven't.

Just “Yes” or “No” isn't wrong, but Yes, I do / No, I don't sounds more complete and more polite, especially when the answer is no.

Test yourself

Fill in one word (the hint in brackets gives the tense or the verb):
1. Where ___ your parents live now? (Present Simple)
2. ___ you at work yesterday? (to be)
3. What time ___ the train usually leave? (Present Simple)
4. Who ___ this cake? It's delicious! (make, Past Simple)
5. Excuse me, do you know where the bank ___? (to be, present)
6. ___ your brother speak German at work every day? (Present Simple)

Answers: 1. do. 2. Were. 3. does. 4. made (the question is about the subject, so no do). 5. is. 6. Does.

A tip: for one week, every evening write down five questions you could ask someone tomorrow — a colleague, a friend, a teacher. Read them out loud too, so the pattern becomes automatic. If you get confused between tenses, the article Present Simple or Present Continuous will help, and for starting a conversation — small talk in English.

Frequently asked questions

Why can't I say Where you live?

A Present Simple wh-question (where, what, when…) needs the auxiliary do/does: Where do you live? The exceptions are to be (Where is he?) and questions about the subject (Who lives here?). In casual speech you will also hear questions asked with intonation only, like You live here?, but these are checking questions.

Does she works or Does she work?

Does she work is correct: does already shows the third person, so the main verb stays without -s. The same happens with did: Did you go, not Did you went.

Who called you and Who did you call — what's the difference?

Who called you? asks who made the call (who is the subject, so no do). Who did you call? asks which person you phoned (who is the object, so do is needed).

Is Can you tell me where is the station correct?

No. In an indirect question the word order stays the same as in a statement: Can you tell me where the station is?
