The Passive Voice in English — explained simply 🔁

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“This house was built in 1950” — in your own language you'd probably say this without thinking. In English, though, the same idea often comes out like this: This house built in 1950 — and that's a mistake that grates on an English speaker's ear. The Passive Voice is one of those topics where everyone knows the rule, yet using it is still hard.

Below we'll look at one formula, all the main tenses, the two main situations where the passive is used, and the mistakes that come from the logic of other languages. At the end — a short quiz.

What the Passive Voice is and when we need it

In the active voice the subject does the action: Ana wrote this letter. In the passive the subject receives the action: This letter was written by Ana. What was the object in the active sentence becomes the subject in the passive one.

Two main situations:

1. The doer is unknown or unimportant. My bike was stolen — I don't know who stole it; English is spoken here — nobody cares who speaks it. Someone stole my bike is possible here, but the passive sounds more natural.

2. The object matters more than the doer. The bridge was built in 1912 — we're talking about the bridge, not the builders. That's why the passive is so common in news, instructions and official texts.

✅ Someone stole my bike yesterday. — Correct, but the focus is on “someone”.
✅ My bike was stolen yesterday. — More natural: the doer is unknown, and the bike is what matters.

The formula: be + V3

The passive has one formula for every tense: the verb be in the tense you need + the third form of the main verb (V3, the past participle). The tense changes only be — V3 always stays the same. So if you already know the third forms from the Present Perfect, you've got half of the passive in your hands.

The main tenses:

✅ Present Simple: The office is cleaned every day.
✅ Past Simple: The office was cleaned yesterday.
✅ Future: The office will be cleaned tomorrow.
✅ Present Perfect: The office has been cleaned.
✅ Present Continuous: The office is being cleaned right now.
✅ Modal: The office must be cleaned before Monday.

Pay attention to is being cleaned and has been cleaned — these two forms are the ones learners mix up most often. being = “now, in progress”; been = “already, the result”. The Past Continuous works the same way: The office was being cleaned when I arrived. In conversation you'll also hear get instead of be: My bike got stolen — it's the same passive, just informal.

The “by” phrase — when we say who did it

If you still want to name the doer, in the passive it takes by: The novel was written by a Georgian author. But here's an important observation: if the by-phrase is essential, an active sentence is often better. The letter was written by Ana is correct, but Ana wrote the letter is more natural — if Ana is what matters, say it in the active.

The by-phrase is really needed when the doer is new, interesting information: This song was written by a 14-year-old — we're talking about the song, but the writer's age is surprising.

❌ The book was written from a famous writer.
✅ The book was written by a famous writer. — The doer takes by, not from.

Typical mistakes that come from other languages

1. Leaving out be. This is the most common mistake, and it often comes from your first language: in many languages the passive is a single word or a single verb ending, with no helping verb. In English, however, built without be has an active, past-tense meaning — an English speaker hears The house built… as an unfinished sentence and waits to find out what the house built.

❌ This house built in 1950.
✅ This house was built in 1950.
❌ The meeting cancelled.
✅ The meeting has been cancelled. / The meeting was cancelled.

2. V2 or the base form instead of V3. With regular verbs V2 and V3 are identical (cleaned), so the mistake doesn't show. With irregular verbs it does: was wrote, was took, is speak.

❌ The letter was wrote yesterday.
✅ The letter was written yesterday.
❌ English is speak all over the world.
✅ English is spoken all over the world.

3. Sentences with no “who”. Many languages have a handy way to say “I was told”, “I was asked”, “my bag was stolen” without naming anyone — an impersonal “they” or a special verb form. In English this is very often exactly the passive: I was told, I was asked. Learners, though, usually say They told me — that's also correct if “they” are specific people; if not, the passive is more accurate.

⚠️ They told me to wait outside. — Correct if we know who “they” are.
✅ I was told to wait outside. — Who told me is unknown or unimportant.

4. Passives of intransitive verbs. As a rule, only verbs that take an object have a passive. happen, arrive, die, appear don't take an object, so they have no passive either.

❌ An accident was happened on the road.
✅ An accident happened on the road. — happen has no passive.
❌ My grandfather was died in 1990.
✅ My grandfather died in 1990.

Your language's passive and the English passive — what matches and what doesn't

Most languages have some way of expressing the passive, and for typical cases it matches English well: when someone really does something to the thing you're talking about (writing a letter, building a house, cancelling a meeting), the English passive fits: the letter is being written, the house was built, the meeting was cancelled. Remember this rule of thumb. But be careful with verbs of change of state — “happen”, “change”, “grow”: even if your language uses a passive-looking or reflexive form for them, English uses the active: happen, change, grow.

There are two differences, though. First: in many languages the passive is one word, while English always needs two elements (was built) — and it's exactly this “second part” that tends to get lost. Second: some languages use a passive or reflexive form with a completely different meaning — to say that something is easy or pleasant to do: literally “this book is read easily” or “this book reads itself easily”, in the sense of “it's easy to read”, not that someone is reading it right now. In English the equivalent is not a passive but a simple active form: This book reads well — not is read well.

⚠️ This book is read very easily.
✅ This book reads very easily. / This book is easy to read. — “Is read” is grammatical, but it describes people reading it, not the book's quality. Here “reads” describes a quality of the book, not an action someone performs.

Quick summary

Passive = be in the right tense + V3. Use it when the doer is unknown or unimportant, or when the object matters more; add the doer with by if you need to. The most common mistake is leaving out be; the second is using V2 instead of V3. If you catch these two, a large share of your passive mistakes is already solved.

Note: be used to isn't always the passive — I'm used to it means “I'm accustomed to it”. I explain the difference in the article on used to.

Check yourself

Put the verb in the correct form — or, where there's a choice, pick the correct option. Each item has one answer.

1. This bridge ______ (build) in 1912 — the exact year is on the sign.
2. Look! The road ______ (repair) right now — we can't drive through.
3. "Sorry, all the tickets ______ — I've just checked the website." (a) have already been sold (b) have already been sell (c) has already been sold
4. "The accident ______ last night." (a) was happened (b) happened (c) has happened by nobody
5. "This song was written ______ a teenager." (a) from (b) of (c) by

Answers: 1. was built — a specific past date, Past Simple passive. 2. is being repaired (informal: is getting repaired is also possible) — “right now”, in progress, Present Continuous passive. 3. a — Present Perfect passive: been + V3 (sold, not sell); tickets is plural — have, not has. 4. b — happen has no passive. 5. c — the doer takes by.

One practical tip: when you write an English sentence where something is done to the subject (and someone really does it), run two checks — is be there? Is it the third form? You need the list of third forms anyway, because the same V3 also works in the Present Perfect; and if the difference between the Present Continuous and the Present Simple isn't solid yet, this article will make the is cleaned/is being cleaned pair easier. Frequently asked questions

How is the Passive Voice formed in English?

With one formula in every tense: the verb be in the tense you need + the third form of the main verb (V3). The tense changes only be: is cleaned, was cleaned, will be cleaned, has been cleaned, is being cleaned, must be cleaned.

When is the passive used in English?

In two main situations: when the doer is unknown or unimportant (My bike was stolen) and when the object matters more than the doer (The bridge was built in 1912). If the doer is what matters, the active form is more natural.

Why is “This house built in 1950” wrong?

It's missing be: the correct sentence is This house was built in 1950. In many languages the passive is a single word, but English needs two elements — and it's exactly this second part that gets lost. Without be, built is the active past tense.

Does every verb have a passive?

No. As a rule, only verbs that take an object have a passive. happen, arrive, die and appear don't take an object, so was happened and was died are mistakes — the correct forms are happened and died.
