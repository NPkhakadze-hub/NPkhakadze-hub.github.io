How to remember English words — a system that works 🧠

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“I learn words, but a week later I've forgotten everything” — that's probably the sentence I hear most often from students. And it isn't weakness or a bad memory: the brain deliberately throws away whatever we haven't needed. Our job is to convince the brain that we really do need the word.

Below are three principles that sit at the heart of every method, and a concrete system — one that needs just fifteen minutes a day.

Why we forget new words

A new word leaves a weak trace in the brain, and if we don't meet it again, the trace gradually fades. Forgetting is fastest right after learning and then slows down. That isn't bad news — it simply means that learning something once doesn't work, however long you sit with that list in your hands.

On the other hand, every review strengthens the trace and slows down forgetting. That's why five five-minute meetings with a word are more powerful than one half-hour session.

Three principles at the heart of every method

1. Review spread out over time. Not one long list in a single evening, but coming back to the same words several times, with longer and longer gaps.

2. Recalling, not rereading. Looking over a list gives you a feeling of familiarity, not knowledge. Recalling takes effort — and it's exactly that effort that strengthens memory.

3. Context, not a word on its own. A word doesn't live alone: it has its own preposition, its own neighbouring verb and its own situation.

Spaced repetition — what it looks like in practice

The simplest scheme, which needs nothing more than a notebook: you meet a new word today, then review it the next day, the day after, a week later and a month later. If you can't recall it at one of the reviews, the word starts the journey again.

In a notebook it works like this: one page per day, with the dates at the top of the page showing when to come back to it. An app does exactly this counting for you — nothing more.

Active recall — the two-minute test

Check whether you really know a word: cover up the meaning and try to build a sentence with the English word. If no sentence comes, you don't know the word yet — it's just familiar.

The opposite direction is essential too. Going from English to your own language is much easier, but when you speak you need the reverse: from the thought in your head to the English word.

Familiarity: “I recognise it when I see it.” Knowledge: “It comes to me when I speak.” These are two different levels, and when you learn, the second one is the goal.

A card that works

A bad card is one word and one translation. A good card has four things: the word, its pronunciation, one sentence where the word relates to your own life, and its meaning. You should write the sentence yourself — a ready-made example sticks much less well.

✅ reluctant /rɪˈlʌktənt/ — I was reluctant to speak at the meeting, but I did. — unwilling; not keen to do something.

That's why someone else's ready-made collection (a so-called deck) usually doesn't work: it doesn't know your situations. Writing your own card is, in itself, your first review.

One word or a phrase

English words travel in pairs and groups. decision on its own doesn't give you much — you need make a decision. These combinations (collocations) are what we learn together, not separate words.

❌ I want to do a decision about the job.
✅ I want to make a decision about the job. — a decision takes make.

This one habit solves two problems at once: you remember the word, and you know how to put it into a sentence.

Where to find the words you'll actually need

Lists of “the 1,000 most useful words” don't work because they aren't about anyone's actual life. A much better source is whatever you read or listen to yourself: a TV series, emails at work, an article about your profession, a song.

A practical filter: write a word down only if one of two conditions is met — either you've come across it several times recently, or you often use its equivalent in your own language when you talk. That way the list adapts to your topics by itself.

A relatively small number of frequent words covers a large part of everyday conversation, so for a beginner a pile of rare words is worth less than being able to speak freely, in correct phrases, with the frequent ones.

How to move a word into your speaking

A word learned from a card is still only at the recognition level. Moving it into your speaking is a separate step — and it works best in the first few days.

A simple habit: use the new word the same day or the next day in a real sentence — even if it's just to yourself, out loud. A few uses like this push the word from passive knowledge towards active.

The second habit is a “sentence diary”: three sentences a day about something that really happened, using new words. It works twice over — you review the words and practise grammar at the same time.

Three mistakes that slow your learning down

1. A long list in one go. A big list learned in one evening usually fades quickly; a few words, reviewed several times, stick much better.

2. Lots of similar words at once. When you learn five synonyms in one go, they get mixed up, and later you can't work out which one fits which situation. It's better to let each word settle in on its own first, and compare them afterwards.

3. Word-for-word translation. Carrying a phrase straight over from your own language often produces the wrong combination.

❌ I have a big experience in this field.
✅ I have a lot of experience in this field. — in this meaning, experience is uncountable: a lot of experience, not “a big experience”.

App or notebook

Anki is free on computers and Android, counts the intervals for you and brings a word back just when you're about to forget it. In return, it takes some setting up on the first day. Quizlet is simpler, but some of its features are paid. A notebook has the lowest barrier and gives you full control, but you have to keep track of the dates yourself.

Choose the way you'd choose a gym: the one that works is the one you'll actually go back to. Fifteen minutes a day with a notebook gives you far more than a perfectly set-up app that you open once a week.

Test yourself

Fill in make, do or take in the correct form.

1. She ___ a serious mistake in the report.
2. Could you ___ me a favour?
3. She has ___ great progress this year.
4. He ___ his best, but it wasn't enough.
5. Let's ___ a photo before we leave.
6. The noise from the street ___ it difficult to concentrate last night.

Answers: 1. made — make a mistake. 2. do — do someone a favour. 3. made — make progress. 4. did — do your best. 5. take — take a photo. 6. made — make it difficult.

Start small: don't add any new words this week — take the twenty you've already written down and give each one a sentence of your own. Then come back to them tomorrow and the day after. The article on make and do continues the same logic, and if you're starting from zero, you'll find learning English from scratch useful.

Frequently asked questions

How many new words should I learn a day?

The rhythm of review matters more than the number. It's better to take fewer words and come back to them several times than to learn a long list in one evening and never look at it again. If you can't keep up with your reviews, cut down the number of new words.

Anki or a notebook — which is better?

Anki's advantage is that it calculates the review intervals itself and brings a word back just when you're about to forget it; on the other hand, it takes some setting up on the first day. A notebook has no barrier, but you have to keep track of the dates yourself. The one that works is the one you'll actually come back to.

Are someone else's ready-made flashcard decks any good?

They usually work poorly, because the examples have nothing to do with your situations. Writing your own card is already your first review: when you write a sentence from your own life for a word, the word is already sitting in context.

I recognise a word but can't recall it when I speak — why?

These are two different levels of knowledge: recognising a word in a text is easier than producing the English word from a thought in your head. That's why you need to review in both directions — and always by building your own sentence.
