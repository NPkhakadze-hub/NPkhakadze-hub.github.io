Past Simple or Past Continuous — how to tell a story about the past ⏳

26 October 2026 · Nodar

“I was watching TV when the lights went out.” In your own language you probably wouldn't think twice about how to say this — but in English many learners say: I watched TV when the lights went out. Grammatically this is correct, but it means something else: it sounds as if you started watching TV after the lights went out.

To tell a story about the past, English has two main tenses: Past Simple and Past Continuous. The first moves the story forward; the second paints the background. Below we'll see when you need which, which verbs don't go into the Continuous, and where your native language can lead you astray. At the end — a short test.

Form: was/were + -ing

Past Continuous has two parts: was or were (the verb be in the past) + the main verb in its -ing form. was goes with I, he, she, it; were with you, we, they. In the negative you add not (wasn't, weren't), and in questions was/were moves in front of the subject.

✅ I was working all evening.
✅ He wasn't listening.
✅ Were you waiting long?
❌ They was playing outside.
✅ They were playing outside.

A process at a specific moment in the past

First and foremost, Past Continuous shows what was in progress at a specific moment in the past: the action had started earlier and was still going on at that moment. That's why it often comes with an exact time reference — at 8 pm yesterday, at midnight, this time last year.

✅ At 8 pm yesterday I was having dinner.
✅ This time last year we were living in Berlin.

Compare this with Past Simple: I had dinner at eight means you ate at eight — one complete fact. At eight I was having dinner, on the other hand, means that at eight o'clock dinner had already started and was still going on.

The background and the interrupting action

When we tell a story, the two tenses most often appear together. The long action, the background, is in Past Continuous; the short action that interrupts it or happens in the middle of it is in Past Simple.

✅ I was cooking when the phone rang.
✅ While I was walking home, it started to rain.
✅ She was driving to work when she saw the accident.
⚠️ I cooked when the phone rang. — grammatically correct, but it means you started cooking after the phone rang.

Sometimes the tense of one verb changes the meaning of the whole sentence:

✅ When Nino arrived, we had dinner. — first she arrived, then we had dinner.
✅ When Nino arrived, we were having dinner. — dinner had already started.

Two parallel processes and the background of a story

When two long actions were going on at the same time, both are in Past Continuous. They're often linked by while.

✅ While I was working, he was watching TV.
✅ The kids were playing outside while we were talking.

By the same logic, at the start of a story Past Continuous sets the scene: what the surroundings were like, what was happening all around. Then Past Simple brings in the event.

✅ It was a warm evening. The sun was setting, people were walking along the river and a street musician was playing the guitar. Suddenly a dog ran into the road.

Notice: It was a warm evening — here be describes a state, so it isn't in the Continuous. More on this below.

Past Simple — actions one after another

Past Simple expresses a completed action. When several actions happened one after another, they're all in Past Simple — that's exactly how a story moves forward.

✅ I got up, had a shower and left for work.
✅ He opened the door, looked around and went in.

Past Continuous doesn't fit here: I was getting up, I was having a shower… no longer shows the sequence. All you see is background, and the story doesn't move.

when or while

while (“during the time that”) goes with a long action, so it's usually followed by Past Continuous. when (“at the moment that”) more often goes with a short action, that is, with Past Simple.

✅ While I was cleaning the flat, I found my old diary.
✅ I was cleaning the flat when I found my old diary. — the same idea, but the focus is on finding it.
❌ I was cleaning the flat while I found my old diary.

But when can also go with a long action: When I was cleaning the flat, I found my old diary — this is correct too. when can also refer to a period of life: when I was a child — “in my childhood”. In short: when goes with both kinds of action, whereas while goes almost only with a process; while doesn't fit with a short, one-off action.

Verbs that don't go into the Continuous

Some verbs describe not an action but a state: knowing, feeling, wanting, owning. These verbs (stative verbs) usually don't go into the Continuous — neither in the present nor in the past. They include know, understand, believe, like, love, want, need, own, belong, seem.

❌ I was knowing the answer.
✅ I knew the answer.
❌ He was seeming tired.
✅ He seemed tired.
❌ This ring was belonging to my grandmother.
✅ This ring belonged to my grandmother.

Some verbs have two meanings — one a state, the other an action. In the action meaning, the Continuous is perfectly natural:

✅ We were having lunch. — (have = eat, an action)
❌ We were having a big house.
✅ We had a big house. — (have = own)
✅ I was thinking about you. — (a process)
✅ I thought he was right. — (an opinion)

I write about these verbs in more detail in the article on Present Simple and Present Continuous — the rule is the same there.

Your native language — a help and a trap

Many languages have two kinds of past form: one for an action that was in progress or unfinished (“I was doing, I was reading”), and one for a completed action (“I did, I read it through”). If yours does, it gives you good support: the first roughly matches Past Continuous, the second Past Simple. That's what happened in the Nino example: we had dinner — a completed meal; we were having dinner — a meal in progress. But the match isn't exact, and it trips people up in two places.

1. The “in progress” past form often expresses habits too. Walking to school every day as a child isn't a process — it's a repeated action. In English, a past habit takes Past Simple or used to. This is probably the most common mistake in this topic.

⚠️ When I was a child, I was walking to school every day. — doesn't sound natural: the Continuous here suggests a temporary or background situation, not a habit.
✅ When I was a child, I walked to school every day.
✅ When I was a child, I used to walk to school. — the same idea; used to stresses that it's no longer like that.

With a repeated action, the Continuous appears only in special contexts, for example a temporary period: That month I was working nights — a temporary arrangement, not a permanent habit. To describe a habit, as a rule, choose Past Simple or used to. The difference between used to and would is explained in the article on used to.

2. State verbs often take the “in progress” form in other languages. In many languages, “knew”, “loved”, “wanted” and “had” are naturally expressed with that ongoing past form, but in English they're Past Simple: I knew, I loved, I wanted, I had. Don't follow the form of your own language here — look at the meaning of the verb.

And one more detail: a completed past action isn't always Past Simple in English. When you lost your keys and still don't have them, British English will, as a rule, use I've lost my keys (in American English Past Simple is often used here too: I lost my keys) — I write about this in the article on Present Perfect and Past Simple.

Test yourself

Choose the correct option or put the verb in the right form. Each item has one correct answer.

1. At ten o'clock last night I ___, so I didn't hear the phone. (a) watched a film (b) was watching a film
2. I ___ my phone while I was running for the bus. (a) dropped (b) was dropping
3. I was waiting for the bus ___ I saw an old friend. (a) when (b) while
4. Sorry, I ___ (not / know) it was your birthday.
5. Yesterday I ___ (get up) at seven, ___ (have) breakfast and ___ (leave) for work.
6. When I looked out of the window, the sun ___ (shine) and the birds were singing.
7. The lights went out while we ___ dinner, so we finished it by candlelight. (a) had (b) were having
8. Last summer I ___ (spend) two weeks at my grandmother's.

Answers: 1. b — at ten the film was already in progress, so I didn't hear the phone. 2. a — dropping the phone is a short action that happened in the middle of the running. 3. a — while doesn't fit with a short, one-off action (saw). 4. didn't know — know is a state verb. 5. got up, had, left — actions one after another. 6. was shining — this is background: when I looked out of the window, the sun was shining. 7. b — dinner is the long action, and the lights went out right in the middle of it. 8. spent — one completed period (last summer), so Past Simple.

A practical tip: in the evening, tell the story of your day in English — out loud or in a notebook. First paint the background (It was raining. I was sitting in a café…), then list the events one after another (Then my friend called, and we went…). Before each verb, ask yourself: is this background or an event? Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between Past Simple and Past Continuous?

Past Simple expresses a completed action or a sequence of actions (I got up and left). Past Continuous shows what was in progress at a specific moment in the past, or paints the background of a story (I was cooking when the phone rang).

When do I use while and when do I use when?

while usually goes with a long action, that is, with Past Continuous. when more often goes with a short action (when the phone rang), though it can also go with a long one. while doesn't fit with a short, one-off action.

Why is “I was knowing” a mistake?

know is a state verb (stative verb). Verbs like this — know, like, want, belong, seem and others — usually don't go into the Continuous, so the correct form is I knew.

Do I use Past Continuous for things I did every day in the past?

Usually not. In many languages the same past form describes both a process and a habit, but in English a past habit takes Past Simple or used to: I walked to school every day, or I used to walk to school.
